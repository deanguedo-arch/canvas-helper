4.1 Roots, estimates and irrational numbers

Read root notation, locate non-perfect roots and compare real numbers in forms that do not look alike.

Start with what a root means

A square root asks for the nonnegative number whose square gives the radicand. For example, √49 is 7 because 7² = 49; −7 is also a number whose square is 49, but the symbol √49 names the principal, nonnegative root. An odd root keeps the sign: ∛(−8) = −2 because (−2)³ = −8.

You will use three related but different skills: find an exact root, estimate a non-perfect root, and compare or classify a number. An estimate alone does not classify a number, and an exact form can be ordered without changing it to a rounded decimal.

Bracket first, estimate second

Locate √52. The nearest square numbers surrounding 52 are 49 and 64.

7² = 49 < 52 < 64 = 8²

so 7 < √52 < 8

52 is 3 above 49 and 12 below 64, so √52 is closer to 7 than to 8. This comparison supports an estimate just above 7. The bracket is exact even if a later decimal is rounded.

Contrast: √49 = 7 exactly; √52 is not an integer. Writing √52 = 7 would replace a non-perfect root with an incorrect exact value.

Classify only after checking the number

A rational number can be written as a ratio of integers with nonzero denominator. A terminating or repeating decimal is rational. The square root of a whole number is rational exactly when the number is a perfect square after simplifying its factors; for instance √72 = 6√2 remains irrational because √2 does.

For ordering, choose a comparison method that preserves enough accuracy. To compare 3.6, √13 and 11/3, square-root bracketing gives 3.6² = 12.96 < 13 < 13.0321 = 3.61², so 3.6 < √13 < 3.61. Since 11/3 is greater than 3.61, the order is 3.6 < √13 < 11/3.

Guided decision: choose the bounds

Before estimating ∛90, choose the consecutive whole numbers whose cubes surround 90. Enter the lower and upper whole numbers. Then compare the distances from 90 to those cubes to decide which endpoint is closer.

Try first. If your bound is off, compare the nearby cubes rather than guessing a decimal.

Faded practice: classify and compare

A learner says √85 is rational because it has a decimal approximation and places √85 below 9. In your notebook, correct the classification and use consecutive squares to justify the order against 8.9. Enter the lower bound so you must choose the comparison yourself.

Then explain in one sentence why having a decimal approximation does not make a number rational. Keep that explanation with your written work.

Repair: root index is not a divisor

If you calculate ∛64 as 64 ÷ 3, pause at the meaning: which number multiplied by itself three times gives 64? Check a proposed root by raising it to the index. For a square root, also check that the principal root is nonnegative.

Use the radical form lab to compare square, cube and fourth roots, then return to your original work and try a different root.

4.2 Mixed and entire radicals

Move between equivalent radical forms by tracking perfect-power factors.

Why factors can move across a radical

For nonnegative factors, the square root of a product can be split because squaring the product of the roots returns the original product: (√u·√v)² = uv. A perfect square inside a square root can therefore become its nonnegative root outside. The same reasoning works for cube roots with perfect cubes, including negative values under odd roots.

A mixed radical has a coefficient outside; an entire radical keeps the coefficient inside. To move an outside coefficient inward, raise it to the radical index so the value is preserved: 3√5 = √(3²·5), while 2∛7 = ∛(2³·7). The index controls the power.

Extract completely, not just once

Simplify √72. First identify a square factor: 72 = 36·2. Then split and evaluate the exact square root.

√72 = √36·√2 = 6√2

(6√2)² = 36·2 = 72

The second line checks the result. A smaller square factor such as 4 also works, but √72 = 2√18 is incomplete because 18 still contains a square factor.

Match the conversion to the index

Write 3∛2 as one cube root. Since ∛27 = 3, put 3³ = 27 inside with the radicand.

3∛2 = ∛(27·2) = ∛54

Extracting the perfect cube 27 returns 3∛2. Squaring the 3 instead would produce a different value. This reverse check catches the common “use the square rule every time” error.

Guided decision: find a useful factor

Simplify √180. Choose a perfect-square factor before you calculate; several factors are possible. Enter the factor and the remaining factor. The check will tell you whether the split preserves 180 and whether the result is complete.

After the split is checked, finish the radical and verify by squaring your mixed form.

Faded practice: decide what enters

Convert 4∛3 to entire form. No conversion steps are supplied: decide what power the coefficient needs, multiply by the radicand, and enter the new radicand.

Repair: an equivalent form may still be unfinished

If you obtain 2√45 for √180, your factor split is valid, but the radical still contains a square factor. Look for the largest remaining perfect square, extract it, then combine the outside coefficients. Return to your first factor choice and show the corrected line beside it.

Open the 4.2 worked repair, then use a different radical for an independent check.

4.3 Product, quotient and zero exponent laws

Use repeated multiplication to explain why exponents add, subtract or become zero.

Start with repeated factors

An exponent counts repeated factors: a³ = a·a·a. Multiplying powers with the same base joins the groups of factors, so a³a² = (a·a·a)(a·a) = a⁵. The base stays the same and the exponents add.

This rule does not apply across addition: a³ + a² is a sum of unlike powers, not a⁵. It also needs matching bases; a³b² cannot become one power of a.

Why division subtracts

For a⁵/a², write the quotient as repeated factors. Two matching factors cancel, leaving three in the numerator.

(a·a·a·a·a)/(a·a) = a·a·a = a³, a ≠ 0

therefore aᵐ/aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ when a ≠ 0

We subtract because division removes matching factors, not because the exponent numbers themselves are divided. The restriction remains because a was in the original denominator.

Why a zero exponent equals one

For a nonzero base, a⁴/a⁴ equals 1 because every factor cancels. The quotient rule describes the same expression as a⁴⁻⁴ = a⁰. Both routes must give the same value, so a⁰ = 1 for a ≠ 0. Zero does not mean “multiply the base by zero.”

For coefficients, do ordinary arithmetic separately from exponent operations. In (18a⁷b⁴)/(6a²b³), divide 18 by 6, subtract the exponents of a and of b, and keep both original denominator restrictions.

Guided decision: identify the operation

Choose which expression can be simplified by joining repeated factors. Then enter the resulting exponent. The examples are similar on purpose: the operation and the base are the two decisions.

Faded practice: organize the quotient yourself

Simplify (20m⁶n⁴)/(5m²n). You choose the coefficient calculation and each exponent operation. Enter the coefficient, exponent of m, exponent of n, and the restrictions. Show your intermediate line on paper.

Repair a quotient-operation error

If your coefficient is correct but the exponent is not, the answer alone does not show which operation you used. First identify whether you added, subtracted or divided the exponents. Then cancel matching factors in a smaller example such as z⁷/z². Two denominator factors cancel two numerator factors; state the leftover power and the nonzero restriction.

Return to your saved line, correct only the affected decision, and compare it with a different uncued quotient. Negative exponents are taught in Lesson 4.5; this lesson's first quotient examples leave positive exponents.

4.4 Powers of powers, products and quotients

Apply an outside exponent to every factor it governs.

Track the scope of the outside exponent

In (ab)ⁿ, parentheses make the whole product the base. Writing the base as repeated groups shows why every factor is raised: (ab)³ = (ab)(ab)(ab) = a³b³. For a power of a power, (aᵐ)ⁿ contains n groups of m factors, so the exponents multiply.

For a quotient, the grouped fraction is the base too: (a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ, provided b ≠ 0. Parentheses and the fraction bar show exactly what the outside power reaches.

Apply the power to every part

Simplify (−2x³y²)⁴. Treat the signed coefficient and each variable power as factors in one grouped product.

  1. Distribute the fourth power.

    (−2)⁴(x³)⁴(y²)⁴

  2. Evaluate the coefficient and multiply inner exponents.

    16x¹²y⁸

The sign is positive because the negative coefficient is raised to an even power. With an odd power it would remain negative.

Contrast the sign and the base

Without parentheses, −2² means −(2²) = −4. With parentheses, (−2)² = 4 because the negative sign is part of the base. The exponent does not reach backward across a missing pair of parentheses.

Likewise, (x+y)² is x²+2xy+y², not x²+y². The power of a product law applies to multiplication, not to a sum.

Guided decision: mark the base

For (−3x²)/(2y) all raised to the third power, decide which listed parts receive the exponent and what happens to the sign. Select the sign of the result and the exponent on x before opening feedback.

The denominator must remain grouped, and y cannot be zero.

Faded practice: simplify with less organization

Simplify (2m²n³)²·m⁴/n. Decide where to begin, write a line showing the power distribution, then give the final expression and the restriction from the original denominator.

Repair: do not leave a factor untouched

If you wrote 2x³y³ for (2xy)³, the variable exponents are correct but the coefficient did not receive the cube. Box the full base, then apply the outside exponent to each factor. For a quotient, also show the denominator factors raised to that power and preserve their restrictions.

Keep the first line, add the corrected line beneath it, then try a different grouped expression without the labels above.

4.5 Negative exponents and reciprocals

Read a negative exponent as a reciprocal instruction, not as a negative value.

Derive the reciprocal before using a shortcut

When dividing powers of the same nonzero base, exponents subtract. In a²/a⁵ the result of that subtraction is −3, but the original quotient also lets us cancel the two numerator factors against two of the five denominator factors:

a²/a⁵ = 1/a³

so a⁻³ = 1/a³, a ≠ 0

A negative exponent tells where the powered factor belongs in a reciprocal. It does not make the value negative. The value can be positive or negative depending on the base and the other operations.

Move only the factor with the negative exponent

Rewrite (3x⁻²)/(2y⁻³) using positive exponents. The coefficient 3/2 has no negative exponent, so it does not invert.

  1. Move x⁻² from the numerator to the denominator.

    The x factor becomes x² below the fraction bar.

  2. Move y⁻³ from the denominator to the numerator.

    The y factor becomes y³ above the bar.

  3. Keep the coefficient and restrictions.

    3y³/(2x²), x ≠ 0, y ≠ 0

Separate a negative base from a negative exponent

(−2)⁻³ = 1/(−2)³ = −1/8. The negative exponent created the reciprocal; the negative base and odd power created the negative sign. By contrast, −2⁻³ has no parentheses around the base, so it means −(2⁻³) and has the same value here for a different reason. With an even power the distinction matters: (−2)⁻² = 1/4 while −2⁻² = −1/4.

Guided decision: choose what crosses

Rewrite 5p⁻³/q⁻². Decide which powered factor belongs in the numerator and which in the denominator. Enter only the positive exponents in the resulting numerator and denominator; the coefficient stays where it is.

Faded practice: evaluate an exact negative power

Evaluate (2/3)⁻³. Show a reciprocal line on paper before entering an exact value. A decimal is also an equivalent exact value if it terminates.

Repair: preserve restrictions from the start

If x⁴/x⁴ became 1 in your work, the simplification is correct only for x ≠ 0 because the original denominator could not be zero. Write the restriction beside the original expression, then carry it to the simplified result.

Use the 4.5 reciprocal repair and then check a different expression where the negative exponent begins below the fraction bar.

4.6 Fractional exponents and radicals

Use the denominator as a root index and the numerator as a power.

Read the fraction exponent as two instructions

In aᵐ⁄ⁿ, the denominator n names the root index and the numerator m names the power. For nonnegative a, either order can work when both intermediate values are real:

aᵐ⁄ⁿ = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ = ⁿ√(aᵐ)

Choose the order that uses a perfect power early and avoids unnecessarily large numbers. Over real numbers, an even root needs a nonnegative radicand; an odd root allows negative radicands.

Choose a useful evaluation order

Evaluate 32³⁄⁵. The fifth root is easy because 32 = 2⁵.

  1. Take the fifth root.

    ⁵√32 = 2.

  2. Apply the numerator power.

    2³ = 8, so 32³⁄⁵ = 8.

You could cube 32 first and then take a fifth root; that is equivalent but much less manageable. The selected order is a mathematical choice, not a new rule.

Check when the real expression exists

For x⁵⁄⁴, the fourth root requires x ≥ 0. An odd root such as x¹⁄³ accepts any real x. A denominator of zero in the exponent is never allowed. The parity of the root index controls the real-number domain.

Also distinguish √(x²) from x: for real x, √(x²) = |x| because the principal square root is nonnegative. Replacing it with x would be wrong when x is negative.

Guided decision: assign each part its job

For 81²⁄⁴, select the root index and enter the power to apply after the root. Then calculate the exact value.

Faded practice: decide the real domain

For each expression, decide whether it is real for every real x or needs a restriction: x⁵⁄⁴ and x²⁄³. Enter the domain condition for each. Explain which root index controls the decision.

Repair: do not swap index and power

If you read 8²⁄³ as the square root followed by cubing, go back to the fraction: the denominator supplies 3, so the root is a cube root; the numerator supplies 2, so then square. State the chosen order before calculating and check by reversing the operation.

Compare both forms in the radical form lab, then return and apply the same choice to a different base.

4.7 Applying the exponent laws

Name each decision, simplify in a reliable order and preserve every restriction.

Plan from the visible structure

Multi-step expressions are easier to control when you mark what each symbol governs. First identify parentheses, outside powers, fraction bars and additions. Apply an outside power to its complete grouped base; then combine only powers with like bases. Keep any condition that made the original expression defined.

When an expression describes growth or decay, the exponent counts repeated equal-time changes. The coefficient gives the starting amount and the base gives the multiplier per time interval. A value can be computed correctly while its interpretation or units are still wrong.

Annotate each transformation

Simplify [(2x²y⁻¹)³·x]/(4x²), with x ≠ 0 and y ≠ 0.

  1. Apply the cube to every factor in the parentheses.

    (2x²y⁻¹)³ = 8x⁶y⁻³

  2. Multiply coefficients and combine x powers.

    2x⁵y⁻³ after simplifying the coefficient and x exponents.

  3. Use positive-exponent form if requested.

    2x⁵/y³, x ≠ 0, y ≠ 0

The original denominator and negative exponent both exclude zero. The restrictions survive even though the final denominator looks simpler.

Use a model as a relationship, not a label

A culture begins with 200 bacteria and doubles every hour. Its amount after t hours is B(t)=200·2ᵗ. After 4 hours, B(4)=200·16=3200. The starting amount and the hourly multiplier have different jobs. A linear rule such as 200+2t would describe adding two each hour, which is a different situation.

For a decaying quantity that keeps half its amount each hour, the multiplier is 1/2. State the time unit and verify that one step in the model matches the described change.

Guided decision: choose the model multiplier

A sample contains 500 mg of a substance and retains 80% of its amount each day. Select the daily multiplier and enter the amount after two days. Explain on paper why you multiply by the same factor twice rather than subtracting 20 mg.

Faded practice: simplify and verify

Simplify [(3p²q)²·p³]/(9p), using positive exponents. Organize the work yourself. Enter the final expression and the restriction carried from the original denominator.

Repair: locate the first invalid boundary

If your result changes at one line, identify the first boundary: did the exponent reach every grouped factor, did you combine only like bases, and did you carry denominator restrictions? Correct that line rather than restarting the whole expression. Verify the corrected result using a legal value or by reversing a transformation.

Keep the original line and correction together; then demonstrate the decision in a different expression or model without lesson cues.

4.8 Roots and powers transfer

Choose a useful representation when the question does not announce which chapter rule to use.

Choose a representation from the question

An unfamiliar problem will not name the chapter rule. Decide what the unknown represents, what information is given, and which operation reverses the relationship. Area is measured in square units, so a side length uses a square root. Volume uses cubic units, so an edge length uses a cube root. A repeated growth factor suggests an exponent model.

Prepared learners can go directly to the uncued demonstration. If you need support, first practise choosing the relationship, then remove the cues and try different values.

Connect a volume to an edge length

A cube has volume 250 cm³. Since V=s³, its edge is s=∛250 cm. Bracket the value with 6³=216 and 7³=343, so 6<s<7. The unit changes from cubic centimetres to centimetres because the cube root reverses the third power in the volume relationship.

Unlike square roots of positive non-perfect squares, ∛250 is still an exact value even though it is not a whole number. A decimal estimate is useful for a measurement, but retain the exact radical when asked.

Check units and reasonableness

A square has area 72 m². Since A=s², the positive side length is √72=6√2 m, about 8.49 m. Squaring the exact value returns 72 m². Reporting 6√2 m² would leave the area unit attached to a length.

For a circular display with area 49π cm², use A=πr² to get r²=49 and the physical radius r=7 cm. Verify by substituting: π(7)²=49π cm². The equation may have two algebraic square roots, but the context selects the nonnegative length.

Guided decision: choose the inverse relationship

A rectangular display uses a square panel with area 98 cm². Choose whether the side comes from a square root or cube root, and enter the exact side length in simplest radical form. Give the unit appropriate to the requested dimension.

Faded transfer: select the method without a cue

A cube-shaped container holds 500 cm³. Estimate the edge length to one decimal place and show two consecutive whole-number powers that support your estimate. No operation is named for you.

Then write the relationship and units in your notebook and explain why your estimate is reasonable.

Repair: keep the requested dimension and units together

If you use an area directly as a side length, identify the relationship first: area equals side times side. If your answer still has square units, check whether you took the needed root. For a cube, use the third power and cube root instead.

Return to your original work, repair the relationship and unit, and then solve a different context without the lesson title naming the method.

Use mixed practice when you are ready to choose the method without a lesson label.

Roots and number systems

Root
A value that produces a given number when raised to the stated index.
Radical
An expression containing a radical sign.
Index
The root being taken; an unprinted square-root index is 2.
Radicand
The expression under the radical sign.
Mixed radical
A radical with a coefficient outside the sign.
Entire radical
An equivalent form with the coefficient moved inside.
Rational number
A number expressible as a ratio of integers with nonzero denominator.
Irrational number
A real number whose decimal neither terminates nor repeats.

Exponent structures

Base
The repeated factor in a power.
Exponent
The number that describes repeated multiplication of the base.
Product of powers
A product of like bases; add the exponents.
Quotient of powers
A quotient of like nonzero bases; subtract the exponents.
Power of a power
A power raised to another power; multiply the exponents.
Zero exponent
A nonzero base raised to zero has value 1.
Negative exponent
An instruction to use the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power.
Rational exponent
An exponent whose denominator names a root and numerator names a power.
Restriction
A value excluded because it makes the original expression undefined.
Equivalent expressions
Expressions with the same value for every allowed variable value.

4.1 · Bracket a root and decide what it tells you

Start from the mistake. If √85 was called rational because a calculator gives a decimal, ask what makes a number rational: can its exact value be written as a ratio of integers? A decimal approximation does not answer that question.

  1. Locate it.

    Use 9²=81 and 10²=100 to get 9<√85<10. Since 85 is not a perfect square, √85 is irrational.

  2. Try a focused question.

    For √50, enter consecutive whole-number bounds and state whether its exact value is rational or irrational. Explain why a decimal estimate does not change the classification.
