The Cycling of Matter in the Biosphere

Let's start by discussing what the biosphere actually is. It is thin layer around planet Earth where life exists. It is made up of the air, water and land that life as we know it survives. Within the biosphere, matter is continually cycled and energy flows through different systems in order to maintain homeostasis (a balance) for all living things to survive.

1. Natural Cycles: Food Chains, Food Webs & Energy Pyramids

All living things need energy to survive. This energy is passed through ecosystems from organism to orgranism. Food chains, food webs and energy pyramids show how that energy is passed along.

Food chains: show a single pathway of energy flow in an ecosystem - the arrows represent the direction energy moves in the food chain.

Food webs: interconnected food chains in an ecosystem, showing how organisms depend on each other. These are more accurate representations of ecosystems because organisms feed on more than one type

Energy pyramids: show that energy decreases as you move up each level in an ecosystem.  It is shaped as a pyramid to illustrate that we MANY producers and herbivores to support a small number of ominvores and carnivores:

Herbivores eat plants.

Carnivores eat herbivores.

Only ~10% of energy moves to the next level.

2. The Cycling of Matter

Matter such as oxygen (O2), carbon (C) and water (H2O) need to be recycled within the biosphere. We will focus on two main systems: The Water Cycle and The Carbon Cycle.

The Water Cycle: All living things need water to survive and Earth has a limited amount. Therefore, water needs be recylced to be used over and over again.

There are 4 key steps to the water cycle:

Evaporation:  liquid water is heated up and changes to gas

Transpiration:  water evaporates from plant leaves

Condensation:  water vapour cools and returns to a liquid

Precipitation:  water droplets join together and fall back to the Earth

The Carbon Cycle: Carbon is the basic building block of all living organisms on Earth. Everything that is living or has died contains carbon. Producers (plants) take in carbon dioxide (CO2) through photosynthesis and consumers (animals) release CO2 through cellular respiration. When humans use fossil fuels such as oil and natural gas, we release the Earth's stored carbon back into the atmosphere.

Key Takeaways

The biosphere is a closed system for matter—it recycles through food webs and nutrient cycles.Human activity can support or disrupt these natural cycles.Technologies bring benefits, but many have unintended consequences for ecosystems and long-term sustainability.Looking at traditional knowledge, biodegradable materials, and modern recycling can help us reduce our impact.

Human Influence and Technology

Humans require many resources from the earth in order to meet our basic needs and live our day-to-day lives.  With approximately 8.3 billion people on the planet needing food, water, shelter and clean air, we have disrupted the Earth's natural cycles. Let's explore some of the impacts humans have had on the earth and how we are trying to improve our balance with the planet.

1.Human Influence on Natural Cycles

Traditional Aboriginal societies often used sustainable practices in order to live in balance with the land and protect the resources it provided them. Here are a few methods they used:

Rotating hunting and gathering grounds.

Using all parts of animals (reducing waste).

Controlled burns to renew ecosystems.

Modern societies often disrupt the planet's natural cycles by:

Overfishing or overhunting.

Clearing land for farming and cities.

Using fossil fuels, which add carbon dioxide to the atmosphere.

Create more waste than the Earth can recycle.

Pollution of fresh and saltwater.

5. Impact of Modern Agriculture

As mentioned earlier in the unit, we have a very large population on Earth and each one of the billions of people that live here need food to survive. Therefore, we rely on agriculture.  Agriculture - the growing and harvesting of crops, as well as raising livestock - is key to a healthy population.  These plants and animals also provide natural materials like wool, cotten and hides (leather) that are turned into materials we use every day.

Farming practices have changed over the past few decades, relying on more efficient methods and new technology.

Negative Impacts of Agriculture:

Pesticides & fertilizers disrupt soil and water cycles.

Monoculture (one crop over large fields) reduces biodiversity.

Runoff of chemicals → algal blooms → oxygen loss in lakes and rivers.

Large scale farming produces large amounts of greenhouse gases

Positive Impacts of Agriculture with Changing Methods and Technology:

Produces larger amount of food for growing population

Protects the vital soil needed to grow crops and raise livestock

Regrowth of natural plants

Increased populations of pollinators such as bees and other insects

Reduction of current levels of greenhouse gas emissions

6. Unforeseen Environmental Consequences of Technology

Fishing technology: large nets and factory ships remove fish faster than they can reproduce → collapse of populations.Pesticides (e.g., DDT): killed pests but also accumulated in food chains, harming birds and other wildlife.Automobile emissions: add greenhouse gases (CO₂, NOx) → climate change and smog.Industrial activities: create pollutants ecosystems can’t recycle (plastics, heavy metals).

Key Takeaways

The biosphere is a closed system for matter—it recycles through food webs and nutrient cycles.Human activity can support or disrupt these natural cycles.Technologies bring benefits, but many have unintended consequences for ecosystems and long-term sustainability.Looking at traditional knowledge, biodegradable materials, and modern recycling can help us reduce our impact.

Ecosystems, Biomes and Balance in Nature

All living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts within an ecosystem are connected and depend on one another. As we have learned in the previous two lessons of this unit, ecosystems want to achieve homeostasis and have many processes in place to try and stay balanced.

1. Ecosystem Components

An ecosystem is any area in the environment where living and non-living things interact. Ecosystems can be vast like a coral reef or small area like a rotting tree stump. Let's look at some examples of living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) factors within an ecosystem.

How Abiotic Factors Shape Life: Even though the abiotic parts of an ecosystem are not alive, they determine which organisms can stay alive.

1.Temperature - The Thermostat of Life: Most living things can only survive within a certain temperature range. Temperatures that are too high or too low can destroy cells and proteins necessary for life.

Example - Evergreen trees have thick, waxy needles to survive in -30oC

2.Altitude - Life on the Mountain: Altitude is how high up you are (elevation). As you go higher up in the Rocky Mountains there is less oxygen and the air is cold.

Example - mountain goats have thick fur and special hooves to survive in the harsh mountain environment

3. Climate - The Big Picture: Climate is the average weather pattern of temperature and precipitation. Hot and dry climates cannot support big forests, while cooler areas with lots of rain/snow allow massive forests of spruce and pine trees.

Result - since the producers (plants) are at the beginning of every food chain, they determine which animals can live there to eat those plants.

3. Changes in Populations

There are many factors that influence the populations of plants and animals within an ecosystem and they are very sensitive to change. A healthy ecosystem keeps the populations of plants and animals in an optimal range. This means that there aren't too many or too few of certain organisms within the habitat.

Two major factors that INCREASE the size of a population are:

Birth Rate - The number of organisms that are born within a certain period of time.

Immigration - The number of organisms that join a population.

Two major factors that DECREASE the size of a population are:

Death Rate - The number of organisms that die within a certain period of time.

Emigration - The number of organisms that leave a population.
