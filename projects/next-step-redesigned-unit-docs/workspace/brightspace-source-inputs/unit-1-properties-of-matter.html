Chemical Storage and Disposal

We are going to start off the course by exploring safety around household and workplace chemicals. Even though household chemicals are available to buy in stores and we often keep them around the house, they still pose many dangers. Many workplaces also require the use of chemicals, so it is important to know the basics to handle them safely.

1. When using chemicals, you should always keep the following questions in mind:

Does the substance have special handling or storage requirements?

How will it react with other substances?

How should it be disposed of?

Is it safe for me to use? Do I need any protective wear?

Common Household Items

2. WHMIS

WHMIS stands for Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System. It provides information on how to store, safely handle and dispose of chemicals used in the workplace.

All hazardous materials have a WHMIS label, which summarizes safe handling procedures by providing a recognizable symbol.

This means that we do not have to memorize how to safely use and dispose of each chemical; we just have to understand what the symbols on the labels mean

NOTE: Some materials will also have an MSDS (Material Safety Data Sheets) to provide additional detailed information about each chemical. This includes the physical and chemical properties, safe handling, first aid, and disposal procedures.

3. HHPS

HPPS stands for Hazardous Household Products Symbols. You may recognize some of these symbols, as they are more commonly encountered at home as opposed to the workplace, and therefore require a separate system of symbols to represent the dangers they pose.

The next time you are using a chemical at home, check the label to see if there are any of these symbols below:

Even though household chemicals are stored at home, they still pose significant dangers. Many cleaning products should NEVER be mixed. Here are a few examples:

Bleach + Ammonia = Toxic Chlorine Gas - causes shortness of breath & chest pain

Bleach + Vinegar = Toxic Chlorine Gas - causes burning eyes & breathing problem

Hydrogen Peroxide + Vinegar = Peracetic Acid  - burns skin, eyes and lungs

Classifying and Describing Matter

1. Types of Matter

What is Matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space in the world around us. Mattter can be organized into two categories:

1. Pure Substances

2. Mixtures

Pure Substances

Pure substances have the following characteristics:

1. Made up of one type of particle - These can be elements such as iron (Fe) and gold (Au) or

compounds such salt (NaCl)  or water (H2O)

2. All parts of a pure substance are identical

3. Has a defined chemical formula

4. Can exist in all three states of matter - Solids, Liquids and Gases

2. Mixtures

A combination of two or more substances such that each one’s properties are not lost but may be hidden.

Homogeneous Mixtures - Solutions

Mixtures that look like they have one set of properties.

Blended so well that every sample of the mixture will contain an even mixture of all matter that make it up- therefore “homogenous” means every part of the mixture is the same.

These are what we call “SOLUTIONS”  - the molecules of each substance in the mixture slip in between one another.

Examples of Solutions

Heterogeneous Mixtures - MECHANICAL MIXTURES

Mixtures that contain two or more parts that are still visible.

Means to be made up of parts or mixed. Different components may be visible to the naked eye (e.g. salsa), known as mechanical mixtures, or with a microscope (e.g. juice), known as suspensions.

Example: The properties of minerals in rocks are not hidden. Some minerals might be white, shiny and smooth-black, dull and rough. You can tell that the rock is a mixture and not a pure substance by looking at it.

Examples of Heterogeneous Mixtures

Mixtures

Determining whether substances are pure or mixed

Sometimes it can be difficult to tell whether substances are truly pure or mixed. We can examine their appearance, texture, and even density. However, it is often necessary to investigate their boiling and freezing points…

E.g. How do you know if the water you drink is pure?

Mixtures are composed of multiple substances, so they will boil and freeze at different temperatures than the pure substances that make them up. This is how we can test to see if a substance is pure or not.

Classifying and Describing Matter Continued........

1. The Particle Theory of Matter:

Anything in the universe that has mass is made up of tiny particles called molecules and atoms. This can be anything from the air we breathe, clothes we wear to a distant star millions of light years away.

The Particle Theory of Matter is made up of the five following rules:

All matter is made up of very small particles

All particles in pure substances are the same

There are spaces between particles; the amount of space varies depending on the state of matter

Particles are always moving. As particles gain energy, they move faster

The particles in a substance are attracted to one another

2. States of Matter

Matter may exist in three states, depending on how close together or far apart its particles are…

Interacive: The Three States of Matter

Directions: "Launch" the interactive below and the press the play button. Use the orange play button to see how the molecules are arranged and behave in each container for all 3 states of matter

Matter does not exist in a fixed state. That is, solids can become liquids, liquids can become gases, and vice versa.

When particles gain energy (heat up), they expand (move apart), causing solids to turn to liquid (e.g. ice melts to form water

When particles gain energy (heat up), they expand even more (move apart), causing liquids to turn to gas (e.g. water is vapourized as it begins to boil)

When particles lose energy (cool down), they condense (come together), causing liquids to turn to solid (e.g. water freezes to form ice)

When particles lose energy (cool down), they condense (come together), causing gases to form liquid (e.g. water vapour condenses to form water)

Converting Between States of Matter

Through the addition or loss of energy, matter can change states.

Particle Theory also helps explain other properties of matter...

3. Physical vs. Chemical Properties

Physical Properties of Matter - These can be measured or observed without changing what the substance actually is - the composition of the matter that makes up the substance.

For example: taste, smell, density, freezing or melting point

Chemical Properties of Matter - This is when a substance changes its chemical composition - the combination of molecules and atoms that make up a substance. To observe these properties, we must change what the substance actually is.

For example:  flammability, combustion, type of reaction that occurs when combined with other substances

Solutions and Solubility, Acids & Bases

1. Solutions

Solutions are formed when two or more substances are combined to create a mixture. The mixture appears as one substance. Some examples include apple juice, ketchup, hot chocolate and the air we breathe.  Solutions can exist as a solid, liquid or gas.

2. Solutes and Solvents

There are several key terms to know about solutions. Lets use a solution made up of sugar and water as an example:

Solute: is the substance that gets dissolved in a solution.  The solute in the example sugar. It GETS dissolved.

Solvent: is the substance that does the dissolving of a solute in a solution. It's usually the larger amount of the two substances.  The solvent in the example is the water. It DISSOLVES the sugar.

Soluble: means a solute is able to be dissolved in a particular solvent. In the sugar and water example, we would say that “sugar is soluble in water”.

Insoluble: this term means that a solute is NOT able to dissolve in a solvent   In the sugar and water example, the metal spoon you use to stir the solution will NOT dissolve in the water. We say “the spoon is insoluble in water”

3. How Much Can Be Dissolved?

Solubility is the term used to describe HOW MUCH (mass) of a solute can dissolve in a solvent. It is measured in g/L.  For example, we can dissolve 2000g of sugar in one litre of room temperature water.  The solubility of water is 2000g/L.

Concentration is the amount (mass) of a solute that is dissolved in a solution.  We would say that the concentration of the water is 2000g/L from the example above.

Rate of Dissolving

We can increase how fast and how much of a solute dissolves in a solvent by adding energy to the solution. This can be achieved in two ways:

Heating up the solvent

Stirring the solution

Heating up the solvent will also increase the amount of solute (solubility) that can be dissolved. For example, if 1 litre of water is heated up to 800C, we can dissolve approximately 3690g of sugar. This is much higher concentration compared to the room temperature example above.

Concentrated products, such as frozen orange juice, contain a high solute to solvent ratio. This allows us to simply add water (solvent) to make large quantities of the product ready to use.

Concentrated products are beneficial in that they require less packaging than “ready-made” or dilute products:

Classifying Matter and Using It Safely

1. What Is Matter?

Matter = anything that has mass and takes up space.Examples: air, water, wood, metal, food, plastics.

We classify matter by its properties (how it looks, behaves, or reacts).

2. Safety with Household Substances

Many household and workplace substances can be dangerous.

WHMIS (Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System): used in workplaces.HHPS (Household Hazardous Product Symbols): found on household products.

Common Safety Symbols:

Skull & crossbones – poisonous.Flame – flammable.Exploding bomb – explosive.Corrosion – can burn skin/eyes.

Always store chemicals properly, follow labels, and dispose of them safely (e.g., batteries, cleaners, motor oil).

3. Pure Substances vs. Mixtures

Pure Substances

Made of one type of particle only.Properties are the same throughout.

Examples:

Zinc (metal)Pure water (H₂O)Acetic acid

Mixtures

Made of two or more substances combined.Can be uniform (homogeneous) or non-uniform (heterogeneous).

Examples:

Brass = copper + zinc (alloy).Stainless steel = iron + carbon + other metals.Vinegar = water + acetic acid.Salt water = water + dissolved salt.

4. Real-Life Applications

Salting icy roads: lowers the freezing point of water → melts ice faster.Antifreeze in car radiators: prevents water from freezing in winter and boiling in summer.Alloys (like stainless steel): stronger, rust-resistant, used in construction and tools.

Mixtures often have properties more useful than the pure substances they’re made from.

5. Physical vs. Chemical Properties

Physical Properties

Can be observed without changing the substance.Examples: color, density, melting point, boiling point, conductivity, hardness.Ice melting = physical change (still water).

Chemical Properties

Describe how a substance reacts with other substances.Examples: flammability, reactivity with acid, rusting, decomposition.Rusting iron or burning wood = chemical change (new substances formed).

6. Particle Model of Matter

The particle model explains matter in terms of tiny particles always in motion:

Solids: particles tightly packed, vibrate in place → fixed shape, not easily compressed.Liquids: particles close but can slide past each other → take the shape of container, not easily compressed.Gases: particles far apart, move freely → expand to fill container, easily compressed.

Temperature affects particle motion: higher temperature = faster movement = more energy.

Key Takeaways

Matter can be classified into pure substances and mixtures, each with unique properties.Safety is critical → use WHMIS and HHPS symbols for safe handling, storage, and disposal.Physical properties describe appearance/behavior; chemical properties describe how substances react.The particle model helps explain states of matter and changes in them.

Solutions, Solubility, and Acids & Bases

1. Key Terms

Solution – a homogeneous mixture (looks the same throughout).Solvent – the substance that does the dissolving (usually present in larger amount).Solute – the substance being dissolved (usually present in smaller amount).Solubility – how much solute can dissolve in a solvent at a certain temperature.

Example: In salt water, water = solvent, salt = solute, and together they make a solution.

2. Examples of Solutions

Homogeneous solutions (evenly mixed):

Vinegar (acetic acid in water).Sugar in tea or coffee.Air (oxygen + nitrogen gases).

Not homogeneous (don’t mix evenly):

Oil + water.Sand in water.Salad dressing (if it separates).

3. Temperature and Solubility

Solids in liquids: dissolve better at higher temperatures.

Example: More sugar dissolves in hot tea than cold tea.

Gases in liquids: dissolve better at lower temperatures.

Example: Soda goes flat faster when warm (gas escapes).

Particle Model Explanation:

Higher temperature → particles move faster → solute particles mix more easily into the solvent.But for gases, higher temperature makes particles move too fast → escape from the liquid.

Real-life uses:

Pickling brine: high concentration of salt solution preserves food.Syrups for canning: high sugar concentration prevents spoilage.

4. Packaging & the Environment

Concentrated products (like frozen orange juice or concentrated fabric softener) use less packaging because water is added later by the consumer.Diluted products (ready-to-drink juice) need larger bottles → more packaging waste.Recycling and waste reduction: choosing concentrates helps reduce packaging materials and environmental impact.

5. The pH Scale

pH measures how acidic or basic a solution is.

Scale runs from 0 → 14:

pH < 7 = acid (sour, corrosive) → lemon juice, vinegar.pH = 7 = neutral → pure water.pH > 7 = base (bitter, slippery) → baking soda, soap.

Strong acids (pH ~0-2) and strong bases (pH ~12-14) can be very dangerous.

6. Corrosive Effects of Acids, Bases, and Salts

Acid rain: corrodes metal, stone, and harms ecosystems.Ocean salt spray: rusts cars and bridges near coastlines.High humidity + salt: speeds up rusting of iron.Bases (like strong cleaners) can also break down materials and irritate skin.

7. Dangers of Mixing Chemicals

Never mix household cleaners without knowing what they contain.Bleach + ammonia cleaners → toxic chlorine gas (very dangerous).Acids + water: always add acid to water, never water to acid (prevents splattering).Industrial and household accidents often come from improper chemical mixing.

Key Takeaways

Solutions are made of solutes dissolved in solvents.Solubility depends on temperature and the type of solute/solvent.Concentrated products reduce packaging waste → help the environment.pH shows if something is an acid or base.Acids, bases, salts, humidity, and temperature can corrode materials.Safety first: never mix bleach and ammonia, and always handle acids carefully.

Elements, Compounds, and the Periodic Table

1. Elements and Compounds

Element: a pure substance made of only one type of atom.

Examples: hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), carbon (C), sodium (Na).

Compound: a substance made when two or more elements chemically combine.

Examples:

Water (H₂O) = hydrogen + oxygen.Salt (NaCl) = sodium + chlorine.Carbon dioxide (CO₂) = carbon + oxygen.

Compounds can be broken down into their elements, but elements cannot be broken down into anything simpler.

2. Properties of Metals, Nonmetals, and Metalloids

Metals (left side of periodic table)

Shiny (luster).Good conductors of heat & electricity.Malleable (can be hammered into sheets).Ductile (can be stretched into wires).Usually solid at room temperature.Example: iron, copper, aluminum.

Nonmetals (right side of periodic table)

Dull (not shiny).Poor conductors (insulators).Brittle (break easily if solid).Many are gases at room temperature.Example: oxygen, sulfur, carbon.

Metalloids (border between metals & nonmetals)

Have properties of both metals and nonmetals.Some are shiny but brittle.Can sometimes conduct electricity (semiconductors).Example: boron, silicon.

3. Using the Periodic Table

The periodic table organizes elements by:

Atomic number (number of protons).Groups (columns) – elements with similar properties (e.g., Group 1 = very reactive metals).Periods (rows) – elements with gradually changing properties.

From the table, you can find:Name, symbol, atomic number, atomic mass, state of matter, and general properties.

4. Word Equations for Common Compounds

Word equations show how elements combine to form compounds:

Hydrogen + Oxygen → Water

(2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O)

Iron + Oxygen → Rust (Iron oxide)

(4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃)

Carbon + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide

(C + O₂ → CO₂)

Sodium + Chlorine → Salt (Sodium chloride)

(2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl)

Word equations are the “sentence version” of chemical reactions.

Key Takeaways

Elements are pure substances; compounds are made of elements joined together.Metals, nonmetals, and metalloids have different physical and chemical properties.The periodic table helps locate elements and predict their behavior.Common reactions (like rusting or burning) show how elements combine into compounds.
