Chemical Storage and Disposal

We are going to start off the course by exploring safety around household and workplace chemicals. Even though household chemicals are available to buy in stores and we often keep them around the house, they still pose many dangers. Many workplaces also require the use of chemicals, so it is important to know the basics to handle them safely.

1. When using chemicals, you should always keep the following questions in mind:

Does the substance have special handling or storage requirements?

How will it react with other substances?

How should it be disposed of?

Is it safe for me to use? Do I need any protective wear?

2. WHMIS

WHMIS stands for Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System. It provides information on how to store, safely handle and dispose of chemicals used in the workplace.

All hazardous materials have a WHMIS label, which summarizes safe handling procedures by providing a recognizable symbol.

This means that we do not have to memorize how to safely use and dispose of each chemical; we just have to understand what the symbols on the labels mean

Classifying and Describing Matter

1. Types of Matter

What is Matter?

Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space in the world around us. Mattter can be organized into two categories:

1. Pure Substances

2. Mixtures

Pure Substances

Pure substances have the following characteristics:

1. Made up of one type of particle - These can be elements such as iron (Fe) and gold (Au) or

compounds such salt (NaCl)  or water (H2O)

2. All parts of a pure substance are identical

3. Has a defined chemical formula

4. Can exist in all three states of matter - Solids, Liquids and Gases

2. Mixtures

A combination of two or more substances such that each one’s properties are not lost but may be hidden.

Homogeneous Mixtures - Solutions

Mixtures that look like they have one set of properties.

Blended so well that every sample of the mixture will contain an even mixture of all matter that make it up- therefore “homogenous” means every part of the mixture is the same.

These are what we call “SOLUTIONS”  - the molecules of each substance in the mixture slip in between one another.

Examples of Solutions

Heterogeneous Mixtures - MECHANICAL MIXTURES

Mixtures that contain two or more parts that are still visible.

Means to be made up of parts or mixed. Different components may be visible to the naked eye (e.g. salsa), known as mechanical mixtures, or with a microscope (e.g. juice), known as suspensions.

Example: The properties of minerals in rocks are not hidden. Some minerals might be white, shiny and smooth-black, dull and rough. You can tell that the rock is a mixture and not a pure substance by looking at it.

Examples of Heterogeneous Mixtures

Mixtures

Determining whether substances are pure or mixed

Sometimes it can be difficult to tell whether substances are truly pure or mixed. We can examine their appearance, texture, and even density. However, it is often necessary to investigate their boiling and freezing points…

E.g. How do you know if the water you drink is pure?

Mixtures are composed of multiple substances, so they will boil and freeze at different temperatures than the pure substances that make them up. This is how we can test to see if a substance is pure or not.

Classifying and Describing Matter Continued........

1. The Particle Theory of Matter:

Anything in the universe that has mass is made up of tiny particles called molecules and atoms. This can be anything from the air we breathe, clothes we wear to a distant star millions of light years away.

The Particle Theory of Matter is made up of the five following rules:

All matter is made up of very small particles

All particles in pure substances are the same

There are spaces between particles; the amount of space varies depending on the state of matter

Particles are always moving. As particles gain energy, they move faster

The particles in a substance are attracted to one another

2. States of Matter

Matter may exist in three states, depending on how close together or far apart its particles are…

Matter does not exist in a fixed state. That is, solids can become liquids, liquids can become gases, and vice versa.

When particles gain energy (heat up), they expand (move apart), causing solids to turn to liquid (e.g. ice melts to form water

When particles gain energy (heat up), they expand even more (move apart), causing liquids to turn to gas (e.g. water is vapourized as it begins to boil)

When particles lose energy (cool down), they condense (come together), causing liquids to turn to solid (e.g. water freezes to form ice)

When particles lose energy (cool down), they condense (come together), causing gases to form liquid (e.g. water vapour condenses to form water)

Converting Between States of Matter

Through the addition or loss of energy, matter can change states.

3. Physical vs. Chemical Properties

Physical Properties of Matter - These can be measured or observed without changing what the substance actually is - the composition of the matter that makes up the substance.

For example: taste, smell, density, freezing or melting point

Chemical Properties of Matter - This is when a substance changes its chemical composition - the combination of molecules and atoms that make up a substance. To observe these properties, we must change what the substance actually is.

For example:  flammability, combustion, type of reaction that occurs when combined with other substances

Solutions and Solubility, Acids & Bases

1. Solutions

Solutions are formed when two or more substances are combined to create a mixture. The mixture appears as one substance. Some examples include apple juice, ketchup, hot chocolate and the air we breathe.  Solutions can exist as a solid, liquid or gas.

2. Solutes and Solvents

There are several key terms to know about solutions. Lets use a solution made up of sugar and water as an example:

Solute: is the substance that gets dissolved in a solution.  The solute in the example sugar. It GETS dissolved.

Solvent: is the substance that does the dissolving of a solute in a solution. It's usually the larger amount of the two substances.  The solvent in the example is the water. It DISSOLVES the sugar.

Soluble: means a solute is able to be dissolved in a particular solvent. In the sugar and water example, we would say that “sugar is soluble in water”.

Insoluble: this term means that a solute is NOT able to dissolve in a solvent   In the sugar and water example, the metal spoon you use to stir the solution will NOT dissolve in the water. We say “the spoon is insoluble in water”

3. How Much Can Be Dissolved?

Solubility is the term used to describe HOW MUCH (mass) of a solute can dissolve in a solvent. It is measured in g/L.  For example, we can dissolve 2000g of sugar in one litre of room temperature water.  The solubility of water is 2000g/L.

Concentration is the amount (mass) of a solute that is dissolved in a solution.  We would say that the concentration of the water is 2000g/L from the example above.

Rate of Dissolving

We can increase how fast and how much of a solute dissolves in a solvent by adding energy to the solution. This can be achieved in two ways:

Heating up the solvent

Stirring the solution

Heating up the solvent will also increase the amount of solute (solubility) that can be dissolved. For example, if 1 litre of water is heated up to 800C, we can dissolve approximately 3690g of sugar. This is much higher concentration compared to the room temperature example above.

Concentrated products, such as frozen orange juice, contain a high solute to solvent ratio. This allows us to simply add water (solvent) to make large quantities of the product ready to use.

Classifying Matter and Using It Safely

1. What Is Matter?

Matter = anything that has mass and takes up space.Examples: air, water, wood, metal, food, plastics.

We classify matter by its properties (how it looks, behaves, or reacts).

2. Safety with Household Substances

Many household and workplace substances can be dangerous.

WHMIS (Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System): used in workplaces.HHPS (Household Hazardous Product Symbols): found on household products.

Common Safety Symbols:

Skull & crossbones – poisonous.Flame – flammable.Exploding bomb – explosive.Corrosion – can burn skin/eyes.

Always store chemicals properly, follow labels, and dispose of them safely (e.g., batteries, cleaners, motor oil).

3. Pure Substances vs. Mixtures

Pure Substances

Made of one type of particle only.Properties are the same throughout.

Examples:

Zinc (metal)Pure water (H₂O)Acetic acid

Mixtures

Made of two or more substances combined.Can be uniform (homogeneous) or non-uniform (heterogeneous).

Examples:

Brass = copper + zinc (alloy).Stainless steel = iron + carbon + other metals.Vinegar = water + acetic acid.Salt water = water + dissolved salt.

4. Real-Life Applications

Salting icy roads: lowers the freezing point of water → melts ice faster.Antifreeze in car radiators: prevents water from freezing in winter and boiling in summer.Alloys (like stainless steel): stronger, rust-resistant, used in construction and tools.

Mixtures often have properties more useful than the pure substances they’re made from.

5. Physical vs. Chemical Properties

Physical Properties

Can be observed without changing the substance.Examples: color, density, melting point, boiling point, conductivity, hardness.Ice melting = physical change (still water).

Chemical Properties

Describe how a substance reacts with other substances.Examples: flammability, reactivity with acid, rusting, decomposition.Rusting iron or burning wood = chemical change (new substances formed).

6. Particle Model of Matter

The particle model explains matter in terms of tiny particles always in motion:

Solids: particles tightly packed, vibrate in place → fixed shape, not easily compressed.Liquids: particles close but can slide past each other → take the shape of container, not easily compressed.Gases: particles far apart, move freely → expand to fill container, easily compressed.

Temperature affects particle motion: higher temperature = faster movement = more energy.

Key Takeaways

Matter can be classified into pure substances and mixtures, each with unique properties.Safety is critical → use WHMIS and HHPS symbols for safe handling, storage, and disposal.Physical properties describe appearance/behavior; chemical properties describe how substances react.The particle model helps explain states of matter and changes in them.

Solutions, Solubility, and Acids & Bases

1. Key Terms

Solution – a homogeneous mixture (looks the same throughout).Solvent – the substance that does the dissolving (usually present in larger amount).Solute – the substance being dissolved (usually present in smaller amount).Solubility – how much solute can dissolve in a solvent at a certain temperature.

Example: In salt water, water = solvent, salt = solute, and together they make a solution.

2. Examples of Solutions

Homogeneous solutions (evenly mixed):

Vinegar (acetic acid in water).Sugar in tea or coffee.Air (oxygen + nitrogen gases).

Not homogeneous (don’t mix evenly):

Oil + water.Sand in water.Salad dressing (if it separates).

3. Temperature and Solubility

Solids in liquids: dissolve better at higher temperatures.

Example: More sugar dissolves in hot tea than cold tea.

Gases in liquids: dissolve better at lower temperatures.

Example: Soda goes flat faster when warm (gas escapes).

Particle Model Explanation:

Higher temperature → particles move faster → solute particles mix more easily into the solvent.But for gases, higher temperature makes particles move too fast → escape from the liquid.

Real-life uses:

Pickling brine: high concentration of salt solution preserves food.Syrups for canning: high sugar concentration prevents spoilage.

4. Packaging & the Environment

Concentrated products (like frozen orange juice or concentrated fabric softener) use less packaging because water is added later by the consumer.Diluted products (ready-to-drink juice) need larger bottles → more packaging waste.Recycling and waste reduction: choosing concentrates helps reduce packaging materials and environmental impact.

5. The pH Scale

pH measures how acidic or basic a solution is.

Scale runs from 0 → 14:

pH < 7 = acid (sour, corrosive) → lemon juice, vinegar.pH = 7 = neutral → pure water.pH > 7 = base (bitter, slippery) → baking soda, soap.

Strong acids (pH ~0-2) and strong bases (pH ~12-14) can be very dangerous.

6. Corrosive Effects of Acids, Bases, and Salts

Acid rain: corrodes metal, stone, and harms ecosystems.Ocean salt spray: rusts cars and bridges near coastlines.High humidity + salt: speeds up rusting of iron.Bases (like strong cleaners) can also break down materials and irritate skin.

7. Dangers of Mixing Chemicals

Never mix household cleaners without knowing what they contain.Bleach + ammonia cleaners → toxic chlorine gas (very dangerous).Acids + water: always add acid to water, never water to acid (prevents splattering).Industrial and household accidents often come from improper chemical mixing.

Key Takeaways

Solutions are made of solutes dissolved in solvents.Solubility depends on temperature and the type of solute/solvent.Concentrated products reduce packaging waste → help the environment.pH shows if something is an acid or base.Acids, bases, salts, humidity, and temperature can corrode materials.Safety first: never mix bleach and ammonia, and always handle acids carefully.

Elements, Compounds, and the Periodic Table

1. Elements and Compounds

Element: a pure substance made of only one type of atom.

Examples: hydrogen (H), oxygen (O), carbon (C), sodium (Na).

Compound: a substance made when two or more elements chemically combine.

Examples:

Water (H₂O) = hydrogen + oxygen.Salt (NaCl) = sodium + chlorine.Carbon dioxide (CO₂) = carbon + oxygen.

Compounds can be broken down into their elements, but elements cannot be broken down into anything simpler.

2. Properties of Metals, Nonmetals, and Metalloids

Metals (left side of periodic table)

Shiny (luster).Good conductors of heat & electricity.Malleable (can be hammered into sheets).Ductile (can be stretched into wires).Usually solid at room temperature.Example: iron, copper, aluminum.

Nonmetals (right side of periodic table)

Dull (not shiny).Poor conductors (insulators).Brittle (break easily if solid).Many are gases at room temperature.Example: oxygen, sulfur, carbon.

Metalloids (border between metals & nonmetals)

Have properties of both metals and nonmetals.Some are shiny but brittle.Can sometimes conduct electricity (semiconductors).Example: boron, silicon.

3. Using the Periodic Table

The periodic table organizes elements by:

Atomic number (number of protons).Groups (columns) – elements with similar properties (e.g., Group 1 = very reactive metals).Periods (rows) – elements with gradually changing properties.

From the table, you can find:Name, symbol, atomic number, atomic mass, state of matter, and general properties.

4. Word Equations for Common Compounds

Word equations show how elements combine to form compounds:

(2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O)

(4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃)

Key Takeaways

Elements are pure substances; compounds are made of elements joined together.Metals, nonmetals, and metalloids have different physical and chemical properties.The periodic table helps locate elements and predict their behavior.Common reactions (like rusting or burning) show how elements combine into compounds.

Thermal Energy, Heat, and Temperature

Have you ever wondered why a cup of hot coffee cools down on a kitchen counter, or why a metal spoon gets hot when left in a pot of boiling soup? It all comes down to how energy moves.

To understand energy transfer, we first need to know the difference between three very important words: temperature, thermal energy, and heat. People often use these words to mean the same thing, but in science, they are very different!

Particles in Motion

Remember what we learned in Unit 1......... Everything around us is made of tiny particles (atoms and molecules). These particles are always moving. The energy of moving objects is called kinetic energy.

1. What is Temperature?

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.

If particles move fast, the temperature is high (hot).

If particles move slowly, the temperature is low (cold).

We measure temperature using a thermometer, usually in degrees Celsius (°C).

2. What is Thermal Energy?

Thermal energy is the total kinetic energy of all the particles in a substance.

Because it is a total, the amount of the substance matters! A large pot of boiling water has more thermal energy than a small cup of boiling water, even though they are at the exact same temperature. There are simply more moving particles in the large pot.

3. What is Heat?

Heat is the movement or transfer of thermal energy from one object to another.

Energy always flows in one direction: from the warmer object to the cooler object. When you hold an ice cube, it feels cold because heat is leaving your hand and moving into the ice.

Lesson 7: The Transfer of Heat

Conduction, Convection and Radiation

Now that we have learned how particles behave in hot and cold substances, we need to explore the ways heat moves around in the surrounding environment……….

1. Conduction:

Conduction is the transfer of heat by contact between two substances.

When two substances are touching, heat energy is transferred from the molecules in the HOT substance to the particles in the COLD substance.

For example, when a metal pot of water is placed on a hot burner on a stove.

2. Convection:

Convection is the transfer of heat through liquids and gases (fluids).

When warmed, particles in fluids will rise above the cooler fluid.

As the warmer fluid cools the particles move closer together making it more dense.  This increase in density causes the fluid to sink.

As their particles move farther apart, other warmed particles will replace the old particles and rise to the surface. This creates a Convection Current.

3. Radiation:

The transfer of energy without the movement of matter.

This energy is electromagnetic radiation (EMR) or Radiant Energy.

Radiant heat energy can come from the sun, fire or any hot object. You are giving off radiant heat energy to the surrounding room right now!

Lesson 8: Simple Machines

1. Energy

Lessons 6 and 7 were all about heat as a form of energy (thermal energy) causing particles in substances to speed up or slow down. There are, however, many other types of energy all around us that we experience on a daily basis. See the table below for a summary:

Types of Energy

Explanation

2. What are Simple Machines?

A machine is a device that helps us do work by changing the amount of force we need to apply or the distance over which we apply it. In our daily lives, we use many simple machines without even thinking about them.

Watch this video for more explanations and examples of simple machines.

3. Force Multipliers vs. Distance Multipliers

Most machines fall into two main categories based on how they help us. They either multiply our strength (force) or increase how far something moves (distance).

A. Force Multipliers

Main Goal: A force multiplier is a machine that allows you to move a large load with a small amount of effort.

Your Effort: You apply a small force over a long distance to move a heavy object a short distance.

Result: A large force is produced

Household Example: A car jack. You pump the handle many times (large distance) with a small amount of effort to lift a very heavy car a few inches (large force).

Workplace Example: A ramp (inclined plane) used to roll heavy boxes into a delivery truck.

B. Distance Multipliers

Main Goal: A distance multiplier is a machine that moves a load through a long distance using a large amount of effort over a short distance. These machines are used when you want to move something further or faster.

Your Effort: You apply a large force over a short distance

Result: A long distance is covered.

Household Example: A broom. Upper hand moves a short distance with a large force, while the bottom of the broom moves a long distance.

Workplace Example: Fishing rods or axes. Your hands are close together when applying for to the handle of each one.

4. Safety and Efficiency

Using simple machines safely is crucial in the workplace and at home.

Maintenance: Keep machines clean and lubricated to reduce friction. Friction creates unwanted heat and makes the machine less efficient.

Proper Use: Always use the right tool for the job. For example, using a screwdriver as a lever (crowbar) can cause the tool to break and lead to injury.

Lesson 9: Energy Efficiency and Conservation

1. What is Energy Efficiency?

Efficiency means using less energy to do the same amount of work. When a machine or a house is efficient, it does not waste energy as "lost" heat. Energy conservation is the habit of using less energy overall to protect our environment.

2. Keeping Heat In

To save energy at home, we need to stop heat from escaping. Many variables affect how quickly a building loses heat. Modern homes use special technologies to stay warm in the winter and cool in the summer.

Method

How it Works

Real-World Example

Insulation

Traps air to slow down heat transfer.

Fiberglass or spray-foam in walls.

Energy-efficient Windows

Uses multiple panes of glass to block heat loss.

Triple-pane windows in new houses.

Weather Stripping

Seals gaps around doors to stop drafts.

Rubber seals on the bottom of a front door.

3. Nonrenewable vs. Renewable Resources

Most of the energy we use today comes from nonrenewable resources. These are energy sources that will eventually run out, like coal, oil, and natural gas.

When we improve the efficiency of our cars and homes, we do not need to burn as much fuel. This helps us reduce our reliance on these limited resources and protects the environment for the future.

Heating & Cooling Systems – Thermal Energy Transfer

1. Heat Always Moves from Hot → Cold

Heat (thermal energy) naturally flows from a warmer object to a cooler one until they reach the same temperature.

Cooling systems use this principle:

Air conditioners move heat out of warm rooms into the outside air.

Car radiators transfer heat from the hot engine to the cooler outside air.

Without this flow, systems would overheat or not cool down properly.

2. Three Ways Heat Transfers

a) Conduction

Heat transfer by direct contact.

Fast-moving particles bump into slower ones, passing along energy.

Example: A metal spoon gets hot when left in a pot of soup.

b) Convection

Heat transfer through moving fluids (liquids or gases).

Warm fluid rises, cooler fluid sinks → creates convection currents.

Example: Boiling water (bubbles rise), warm air rising to form wind.

c) Radiation

Heat transfer by waves of energy (infrared radiation).

Can happen through empty space (no particles needed).

Example: Feeling the Sun’s warmth on your face, even in space.

3. Comparing Structures – Traditional vs. Contemporary

Contemporary houses: insulation, double-pane windows, furnaces, air conditioners. Designed to be energy efficient.Traditional Aboriginal structures: used natural, practical designs for heat retention:

Tipis: cone shape, adjustable flaps for air flow, hide coverings kept warmth inside.

Igloos: compact snow structure, snow is a great insulator → heat from people + lamps stays inside.

Both designs show clever ways of keeping warm in different climates.

4. Particle Model of Matter

All matter is made of tiny particles in constant motion.

The higher the temperature, the faster the particles move.

Example: Brownian motion – tiny dust or pollen grains jiggle randomly in water or air because they’re being hit by moving particles.

Heat transfer happens because fast particles (hot) collide with slower particles (cold), sharing energy.

5. Convection & Conduction in Natural & Technological Systems

Sea breezes: during the day, land heats faster than water → warm air rises over land, cool air from the sea rushes in.

Land breezes: at night, land cools faster than water → warm air rises over the sea, cool air flows from land to water.

Cast-iron pans: conduction spreads heat evenly through the pan.

Heaters & furnaces: use convection currents to circulate warm air in a room.

6. How Water Moderates Climate

Large bodies of water (oceans, lakes) heat up and cool down more slowly than land.

This means areas near water have milder climates:

Vancouver (near the Pacific Ocean): cooler summers, warmer winters.

Calgary (farther inland): hotter summers, colder winters.

Water stores and releases heat slowly, creating a “buffer” against extreme temperatures.

Key Takeaways

Heat transfer = always hot → cold.3 methods: conduction (contact), convection (fluid movement), radiation (waves).Structures (modern + traditional) use clever heat retention strategies.The particle model explains how heat works.Water plays a huge role in moderating Earth’s climate.

Controlling the Transfer of Thermal Energy

Thermal energy (heat) is useful—but it can also be wasteful or dangerous. Humans have developed many methods and devices to control how heat moves.

1. Reducing Heat Transfer

Technologies that slow down or reduce heat transfer help us stay comfortable and save energy.

Clothing strategies:

Winter clothing (wool, down, layered jackets) traps air → insulation.Summer clothing (light-coloured cotton) reflects sunlight, allowing heat to escape.

Insulation in buildings:

Fiberglass, foam, and cavity walls reduce conduction and convection.Double-pane windows trap air between panes → slows heat loss.

Everyday examples:

Travel mugs keep drinks hot/cold by using insulating walls.Coolers keep food cold on hot days.

By reducing heat transfer, we conserve energy and reduce costs.

2. Protection Against Dangerous Heat Transfer

Some situations require blocking or controlling intense heat for safety.

Household appliances:

Ovens and stoves are insulated so heat stays inside.

Toasters and kettles are designed with plastic/ceramic handles to prevent burns.

Protective clothing:

Firefighter suits – made of special heat-resistant fabrics (like Nomex) that protect from flames and high temperatures.

Oven mitts – thick, insulated fabric to stop burns.

Engines:

Internal combustion engines use cooling systems (water + antifreeze radiators) to prevent overheating.

Heat shields protect nearby components.

These devices keep people and machines safe when dealing with high heat.

3. How Heat Absorption & Loss Vary by Material

The way a substance absorbs or loses heat depends on its type and amount.

Water vs. oils:

Water heats and cools slowly (high specific heat capacity).

Oils heat and cool faster.

This is why water moderates climate and why oil in a pan heats quickly.

Metal vs. wood:

Metal conducts heat quickly → feels cold or hot right away.

Wood is a poor conductor (insulator) → safer for handles and utensils.

Amount matters:

A large pot of water takes longer to heat and cool than a small cup.

By choosing materials wisely, engineers can design better insulators, cookware, or climate-control systems.

Key Takeaways

We use insulation, clothing, and building strategies to reduce heat transfer.Protective technologies (firefighter suits, insulated appliances, engines) keep people and systems safe.Different materials absorb and lose heat at different rates—engineers and designers use this knowledge to solve real-world problems.

Simple Machines – Force, Distance, and Energy

1. What Are Simple Machines?

A simple machine is any tool or device that transfers energy to make work easier.They don’t create energy—they just change how energy and force are used.Examples: screws, ramps, levers, hammers, hockey sticks, tennis rackets.

Think of them as “helpers” that allow you to apply a smaller force or move farther with less effort.

2. Force Multipliers vs. Distance Multipliers

Simple machines usually act in one of two ways:

Force Multipliers

Allow you to apply a smaller force to move a heavier load.

Example:

Crowbar – pries heavy objects by applying little effort over a larger distance.

Automobile jack – lifts a heavy car using a small force.

You trade distance for force: you move your end farther, but the machine multiplies your effort into a stronger force.

Distance Multipliers

Allow you to apply force over a greater distance to increase speed or range.Example:

Hockey stick – small movement of your hands creates a big swing at the end → puck travels fast.

Tennis racket – multiplies the distance of your swing, transferring energy to the ball.

You trade force for distance: you use more force, but it carries farther and faster.

3. Common Household Simple Machines

Can opener – combines levers and gears, making it easy to cut through metal lids (force multiplier).Scissors – two levers working together; multiply force to cut paper or fabric.Hedge clippers – long handles act as levers; small effort becomes big cutting force.Ramps (inclined planes) – reduce the force needed to move objects up by spreading the effort over a longer distance.Screws – inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder; converts rotational energy into forward force (e.g., wood screw).Hammers – transfer energy from your arm into the nail, pushing it into wood.

Key Takeaways

Simple machines transfer energy and make work easier.They can be force multipliers (increase strength, but you move farther) or distance multipliers (increase range/speed, but you use more force).Everyday tools—from can openers to sports equipment—use these principles to save time and effort.

Digestion, Circulation, and Healthy Living

Our bodies have many systems that work together to keep us healthy. The digestive and circulatory systems are key to making sure our cells get both nutrients and oxygen to function properly and keep us alive.

The Digestive System: Taking in Matter

The digestive system is how your body takes in food and breaks it into tiny molecules your cells can actually use.

Food is mechanically broken down (chewing, churning in the stomach) and chemically broken down (enzymes and stomach acid).

By the time food reaches the small intestine, nutrients (like glucose, amino acids, vitamins, minerals, and fatty acids) are absorbed into the bloodstream.

This absorption increases the blood sugar level (energy available to cells) and mineral levels (e.g., calcium, sodium).

The Circulatory System: Transporting Matter

Once the digestive system has brokend down and released nutrients into the blood, the circulatory system takes over and transports the materials to the rest of the body.

The heart pumps blood, moving nutrients, oxygen, and hormones around the body.

The lungs add oxygen to the blood and remove carbon dioxide.

Together, the heart + lungs + blood vessels make sure the nutrients from digestion reach every single cell, while wastes are carried away.

1.Temperature - The Thermostat of Life: Most living things can only survive within a certain temperature range. Temperatures that are too high or too low can destroy cells and proteins necessary for life.

Example - Evergreen trees have thick, waxy needles to survive in -30oC

2.Altitude - Life on the Mountain: Altitude is how high up you are (elevation). As you go higher up in the Rocky Mountains there is less oxygen and the air is cold.

Example - mountain goats have thick fur and special hooves to survive in the harsh mountain environment

3. Climate - The Big Picture: Climate is the average weather pattern of temperature and precipitation. Hot and dry climates cannot support big forests, while cooler areas with lots of rain/snow allow massive forests of spruce and pine trees.

Result - since the producers (plants) are at the beginning of every food chain, they determine which animals can live there to eat those plants.

3. Changes in Populations

There are many factors that influence the populations of plants and animals within an ecosystem and they are very sensitive to change. A healthy ecosystem keeps the populations of plants and animals in an optimal range. This means that there aren't too many or too few of certain organisms within the habitat.

Two major factors that INCREASE the size of a population are:

Birth Rate - The number of organisms that are born within a certain period of time.

Immigration - The number of organisms that join a population.

Two major factors that DECREASE the size of a population are:

Death Rate - The number of organisms that die within a certain period of time.

Emigration - The number of organisms that leave a population.

5. Changing Ecosystems and Environmental Laws

Humans are constantly making changes to our ecosystems, some are positive and some are negative. Canada and Alberta have put laws and restrictions in place to help protect and minimize damage to our ecosystems. The table below has 3 examples.

Makes it illegal to release "deleterious substances" (poisons) into water where fish live and regulates factory discharge.

Species at Risk Act (SARA)

Wildlife Act & Alberta Land Stewardship Act

Protects the "critical habitat" of endangered species (like the Woodland Caribou) and plans how much land can be developed.
