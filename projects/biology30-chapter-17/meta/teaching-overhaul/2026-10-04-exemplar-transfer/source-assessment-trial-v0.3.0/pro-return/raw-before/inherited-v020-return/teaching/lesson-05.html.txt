<div class="content-section" data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-01" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-01" id="ch17-l05-teaching-01"><h2>Find possible gametes before drawing boxes</h2><p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-01-p1" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-01-p1">For AaBb with <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-independent-assortment" type="button">independent assortment</button>, choose one <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-allele" type="button">allele</button> from the A/a pair and one from the B/b pair. The possible gametes are AB, Ab, aB and ab, each with <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-probability" type="button">probability</button> 1/4. An Aabb parent has only Ab and ab as distinct possibilities.</p><p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-01-p2" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-01-p2">Do not place Aa or Bb on a gamete edge. Those are two alleles of one locus, not one allele from each of two loci. A correctly sized square follows from the distinct gametes, not from a rule that every two-trait cross needs sixteen boxes.</p><figure class="science-figure" data-figure-id="ch17-two-trait"><div class="figure-toolbar"><strong>Independent assortment: follow both allele choices</strong><button class="text-link" data-enlarge-figure="ch17-two-trait" type="button">View larger</button></div><img alt="Follow each branch from AaBb through one allele at the first locus and one at the second. Each endpoint is one haploid gamete, with probability one quarter." decoding="async" loading="lazy" src="./assets/figures/ch17-two-trait.svg"/><figcaption>Follow each branch from AaBb through one allele at the first locus and one at the second. Each endpoint is one haploid gamete, with probability one quarter. Schematic — not to scale.</figcaption></figure></div><div class="content-section" data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-02" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-02" id="ch17-l05-teaching-02"><h2>Solve separate loci when independence is stated</h2><p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-02-p1" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-02-p1">For AaBb × AaBb, the A locus gives a 3/4 dominant-<button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-phenotype" type="button">phenotype</button> probability and 1/4 recessive probability. The B locus gives the same. If the loci assort independently, the probability of both dominant phenotypes is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16.</p><p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-02-p2" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-02-p2">The other phenotype combinations are 3/16, 3/16 and 1/16, producing the familiar 9:3:3:1 expectation. This ratio is specific to the stated heterozygote cross and allele relationships.</p><p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-02-p3" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-02-p3">For a different cross, recompute. In AaBb × aaBb, probability of A_ is 1/2 and probability of B_ is 3/4, so both dominant phenotypes have probability 3/8. A memorized 9/16 would be wrong.</p></div><div class="content-section" data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-03" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-03" id="ch17-l05-teaching-03"><h2>Make the assumption part of the answer</h2><p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-03-p1" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-03-p1">Genes close together on the same chromosome can be linked. Their gamete combinations may not occur equally, so the simple product calculation can fail. Later lessons will use offspring data to test this.</p><p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-03-p2" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-03-p2">For now, state when independent assortment is assumed. Check that the probabilities of all mutually exclusive phenotype categories add to one. When calculating an expected count, multiply the appropriate probability by the total number of offspring.</p><p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-03-p3" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-teaching-03-p3">Both the square and product method are tools for organizing the same biological possibilities. Choose the clearer method, but show enough steps that the allele contributions can be checked.</p></div><div class="worked-example" data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l05-worked" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l05-worked" id="ch17-l05-worked"><p class="section-label">Worked example</p><h3>Solve a two-locus cross without unnecessary boxes</h3><p>In an independent complete-dominance model, RrTt × rrTt produces offspring. Find the probability of red, short offspring: R_tt.</p><ol><li>At the colour locus, Rr × rr gives P(R_) = 1/2.</li><li>At the height locus, Tt × Tt gives P(tt) = 1/4.</li><li>Both conditions are required, so multiply: 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8.</li><li>For 80 offspring the expected count is 80 × 1/8 = 10.</li><li>The result relies on independence of the two loci.</li></ol></div>
