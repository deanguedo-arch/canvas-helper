<h2>Each parent contributes one allele</h2>
<p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-02-p1" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-02-p1">A chromosome is a DNA-bearing structure carrying many genes. In an ordinary <strong>diploid</strong> cell there are two chromosome sets. The corresponding chromosomes form a <strong>homologous pair</strong>: one came from each parent, and both carry the same kinds of genes at corresponding loci. An Rr individual has R at the seed-shape locus on one homologue and r at that locus on the other.</p>
<p>Before meiosis, DNA is copied. Each chromosome then consists of two <strong>sister chromatids</strong>, the copied versions joined together. Copying an R-bearing chromosome produces two R-bearing sister copies; it does not turn r into R or mix the two allele versions. We are following ordinary copying and separation, without a mutation changing the allele.</p>
<p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-02-p2" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-02-p2"><strong>Meiosis</strong> reduces the chromosome sets from two to one. The homologous chromosomes separate in the first division. The sister chromatids separate in the second division, with no further DNA copying between those divisions. A cell with one chromosome set is <strong>haploid</strong>. In a flowering plant, the reproductive life cycle produces haploid gametes after this reduction. We only need to follow the allele contribution here: an egg or sperm carries one allele at this locus, not the parent's whole pair.</p>
<p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-02-p3" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-02-p3">Thus an RR parent can contribute R; an rr parent can contribute r; and an Rr parent can contribute R or r. A <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-probability" type="button">probability</button> is the chance of an outcome under the stated conditions. Under ordinary segregation, either allele from the heterozygote has a probability of one half of being in a gamete. Dominance does not decide which chromosome separates into that gamete. The two allele versions remain distinct as they are passed on.</p>
