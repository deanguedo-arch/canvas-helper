<h2>A flower colour disappears, then returns</h2>
<p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-01-p1" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-01-p1">Mendel crossed pea plants from lines with purple flowers and lines with white flowers. The first offspring generation had purple flowers. The colours had not produced the pale-purple blend that a blending explanation would suggest. When that generation reproduced, white flowers appeared again. <strong>How could a phenotype disappear for a generation without its allele being lost?</strong></p>
<p>Keep the distinction from lesson01 in view. A <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-phenotype" type="button">phenotype</button> is an expressed characteristic, such as flower colour. A <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-genotype" type="button">genotype</button> is the allele combination at the locus being considered. An <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-allele" type="button">allele</button> is a version of a gene, and a <strong>locus</strong> is the gene's position on a chromosome. An allele can be present in the genotype even when its associated recessive phenotype is not expressed.</p>
<h3>Know what was crossed</h3>
<p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-01-p2" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-01-p2">A <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-true-breeding" type="button">true-breeding</button> line consistently produces the same form of the relevant trait when bred within the line under the stated conditions. That description comes from breeding history, not from looking at one plant. In the simple one-gene model used here, the contrasting true-breeding lines are homozygous: each individual has two copies of the same allele at that locus.</p>
<p>Controlled crossing makes the parentage known. A pea flower can supply pollen and can also contain eggs. <strong>Pollination</strong> transfers pollen to the receiving flower; a sperm cell delivered through the pollen tube can later join an egg. That joining is <strong>fertilisation</strong>. To compare chosen lines, Mendel controlled which plant supplied the pollen instead of allowing an unintended pollen donor or self-pollination to change the cross.</p>
<p>The chosen starting parents form the <strong>P generation</strong>. Their offspring form the <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-f1-generation" type="button">F1 generation</button>, the first filial generation. Offspring produced when the F1 plants reproduce in the specified design form the <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-f2-generation" type="button">F2 generation</button>. These labels tell you how the generations are related. They do not mean that every P individual is homozygous or every F1 individual is heterozygous in every possible experiment.</p>
<h3>Follow the same question using seed shape</h3>
<p>We will now move from flower colour to another pea characteristic: round or wrinkled seeds. Use <strong>R</strong> for the round-seed allele and <strong>r</strong> for the wrinkled-seed allele. Under complete dominance, <strong>RR and Rr seeds are round; rr seeds are wrinkled</strong>. Rr is a heterozygote because its two alleles differ. Dominant describes this heterozygote's phenotype; it does not mean common, better or more likely to be inherited.</p>
<p>In this model, the shape being scored belongs to each offspring seed with its own genotype. A seed can then be grown into a plant, which will contribute alleles to the next generation of seeds. Keep those generations separate: a parent's genotype helps determine its gametes, while the genotype of a particular offspring seed comes from the two gametes that formed it.</p>
