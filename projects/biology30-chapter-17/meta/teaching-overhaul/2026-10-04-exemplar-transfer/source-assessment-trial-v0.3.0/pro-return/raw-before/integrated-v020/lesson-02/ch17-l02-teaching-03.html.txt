<h2>A predicted ratio is not an offspring schedule</h2>
<p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-03-p1" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-03-p1">The 3:1 phenotype ratio predicts three quarters round and one quarter wrinkled over many offspring from this cross. It does not assign a compulsory order to the offspring or guarantee those exact proportions in a small group. Each fertilisation is another event; an earlier round seed does not make a later seed owe the cross a wrinkled result.</p>
<p>Mendel's reported seed-shape counts included 5,474 round and 1,850 wrinkled seeds, approximately 2.96 round for each wrinkled seed. That is close to 3:1, not exactly 3:1. The useful evidence is a repeated pattern that the inheritance model explains, not a perfect match from every small sample.</p>
<p>For this prediction, we have used known parental genotypes, ordinary equal segregation, random fertilisation and the stated complete-dominance relationship. We also assume that the phenotypes are expressed reliably and recorded without one kind of offspring being selectively lost from the count. Chance can change the observed proportions among possible outcomes. A consistent mismatch can also mean that a starting genotype, observation or model assumption needs checking.</p>
<p data-canvas-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-03-p2" data-canvas-helper-edit-key="ch17-l02-teaching-03-p2">Mendel inferred that inherited factors remained distinct from the breeding patterns he observed. The later chromosome account explains how those factors can be copied, separated and combined. The <button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-term-id="ch17-word-law-of-segregation" type="button">law of segregation</button> explains the allele contributions; complete dominance explains why the Rr heterozygote looks round in this model. Neither rule says that the r allele was destroyed.</p>

<h3>Does reversing the parents reverse the result?</h3>
