Can two characteristics travel together?

Mendel had followed one characteristic at a time through generations of pea plants. His next question was whether the inheritance of one characteristic influenced another. If a plant passed on an associated with tall growth, would a green-pod allele be more likely to travel with it? To investigate that relationship, he needed to follow both characteristics in the same plants.

Look first at the two contrasts below. Compare the height of the plant on the left with the smaller plant beside it. Then look at the pods in the upper-right inset: green on the left and yellow on the right. Height and pod colour are separate characteristics, even though one plant has both.

We can name four possible combinations of these appearances: tall with green pods, tall with yellow pods, short with green pods, and short with yellow pods. Each combination describes a , the observed characteristics. The picture alone does not tell us which combinations a particular cross will produce or how frequent they will be. For that, we need to know the parents and follow their allele contributions.

Choose the starting plants and define the letters

The experiment begins with a true-breeding line of tall plants with green pods and a true-breeding line of short plants with yellow pods. means that repeated breeding within the line produces the same forms of the studied characteristics under the stated conditions. That breeding history matters. Seeing one tall plant with green pods would not, by itself, tell us all of its alleles.

Use T and t for the height gene's alleles, and G and g for the pod-colour gene's alleles. An allele is a version of a gene. A locus is that gene's position on a chromosome. Different letters keep the two loci separate; uppercase and lowercase forms of the same letter identify versions at one locus.

Complete dominance means that the heterozygote, with two different alleles, has the same phenotype as the homozygote with two dominant alleles: Tt is tall like TT, and Gg has green pods like GG. Dominant describes that phenotype relationship. It does not mean that an allele is better, more common, or favoured when gametes form.

In this simple model, the true-breeding parents are TTGG and ttgg. Each is at both studied loci: its two height alleles match, and its two pod-colour alleles match. The four-letter records two allele pairs. It does not mean that a plant has four versions of one gene.

These starting plants are the P generation, the parental generation. Write their cross as TTGG × ttgg. The cross sign connects the two parents; it is not an instruction to multiply letters. A controlled cross uses pollen from the chosen donor so an unintended plant does not change the parentage. Pollen can deliver a sperm cell to an egg in the receiving flower. Fertilisation joins those two gametes, so both chosen parents contribute genetic information.

Follow the P contributions into F1

A gamete, such as an egg or sperm, carries one chromosome set and contributes one allele at each studied locus. The TTGG parent can contribute T at the height locus and G at the pod-colour locus. Its gamete label is therefore TG. The ttgg parent can contribute only t and g, giving tg. A two-locus gamete label has one letter for each locus, whereas a diploid plant's genotype records a pair at each.

Use the arrows in the next image to follow one height letter and one pod-colour letter from each parent. Then inspect the lists beneath them. The repeated TG labels on the left all name the same distinct gamete type. The repeated tg labels on the right also name just one type.

There are many physical gametes, but only one distinct allele combination from each parent at these two loci. TG has probability 1 from TTGG, and tg has probability 1 from ttgg. Joining them restores a pair at each locus: T with t gives Tt, and G with g gives Gg. Every available combination is therefore TtGg.

This is the pattern we need to explain. How can an F1 plant supply the combinations that produce these offspring, and why does the diagram predict unequal phenotype frequencies? Mendel used breeding patterns to infer that the allele contributions for these characteristics assorted independently. We will use chromosome movement to explain what that means, then return to the grid and account for its probabilities.

Two chromosome pairs can orient independently

Start inside an F1 plant with genotype TtGg. For this model, place the height locus on one chromosome pair and the pod-colour locus on a different pair. The two members of a homologous pair carry corresponding kinds of genes: one height homologue carries T and the other t; one pod-colour homologue carries G and the other g. These are ordinary autosomal loci, on chromosomes other than sex chromosomes.

DNA copying occurs before meiosis. Each copied chromosome has two sister chromatids joined together, so an X in the diagram is one copied chromosome, not the whole homologous pair. Normal copying increases physical copies of the tracked loci; it does not create additional allele versions. We still follow T and t at one locus and G and g at the other.

At metaphase I, homologous pairs line up before separating. The direction faced by one pair does not determine the direction faced by another pair. The image below lets us compare that difference. Start with the two top cells labelled Possibility 1 and Possibility 2. Keep the long red/blue pair in view, then notice that the short pair reverses its arrangement.

Follow Possibility 1 down to the middle cells. One cell receives the two blue homologues and the other receives the two red homologues. Under Possibility 2, each middle cell instead receives one blue and one red homologue. The colours distinguish the chromosome origins in this schematic. They do not mean dominant and recessive, and the image does not print T, t, G or g on the chromosomes.

The first separation is meiosis I: homologues move apart while the sisters of each copied chromosome remain joined. The middle row is labelled Metaphase II because the chromosomes are positioned for the second division. There is no new round of DNA copying between divisions. In meiosis II, the sisters separate, leaving one long and one short chromosome in each bottom cell. The repeated bottom cells within a combination group repeat a chromosome combination; they do not add another distinct type.

Connect that movement to the pea alleles. A chromosome carrying T can travel with the chromosome carrying G or with the chromosome carrying g. The t-bearing chromosome also has either pod-colour partner available across the alternative orientations. One meiosis follows one arrangement; it need not produce all four combination types. The four types describe the possibilities across many meioses.

In flowering plants, meiosis first produces haploid spores, with one chromosome set. Later stages maintain that haploid set and produce the structures that form eggs and sperm. The bottom labels summarize the allele-combination consequences, rather than drawing that entire plant life cycle. The contribution we need for a cross remains one allele at each locus in a gamete.

means that the allele contribution at one studied locus does not determine the contribution at the other under this model. It explains how the two combinations present in the P lines can give rise to other combinations. We have described assortment within a parent. The meeting of gametes at fertilisation is a separate event.

Homozygosity answers a different question from appearance. For example, TTGg is homozygous at the height locus but heterozygous at the pod-colour locus. TtGG reverses those genotype descriptions, yet both plants are tall with green pods. To decide whether a plant is homozygous at a locus, compare its two alleles there. To decide its phenotype, apply the expression rule. A dominant phenotype cannot do both jobs.

The four phenotype categories are mutually exclusive: one scored plant cannot be both tall/green and tall/yellow, for example. They also include every possible phenotype in this model. Their probabilities therefore pass a useful check: 9/16 + 3/16 + 3/16 + 1/16 = 1.

Choose the probability operation for the biological event

We can check the phenotype results using the single-locus crosses. Tt × Tt gives TT at ¼, Tt at ½ and tt at ¼, so tall has probability ¾ and short ¼. Gg × Gg similarly gives green pods at ¾ and yellow pods at ¼. Under the independent model, a tall, green-pod offspring must meet both independent requirements: ¾ × ¾ = 9/16. The product concerns two characteristics of the same offspring.

The other joint probabilities follow in the same way: tall/yellow is ¾ × ¼ = 3/16; short/green is ¼ × ¾ = 3/16; short/yellow is ¼ × ¼ = 1/16. Each product agrees with the corresponding group in the full grid. Independent assortment and random fertilisation justify the combinations; the phenotype rules determine which genotypes count.

Add probabilities when joining alternatives that cannot both describe the same outcome. All tall offspring belong to either the tall/green or tall/yellow category, so their combined probability is 9/16 + 3/16 = 12/16 = ¾. The denominator still includes all offspring. If two descriptions overlap, first separate them into categories that do not overlap, so the same offspring is counted once. The words “and” or “or” alone do not justify an operation; the relationship between the events does.

To compare these genotype predictions with observed phenotype counts, we also assume that each genotype expresses the assigned phenotype reliably. concerns whether an associated phenotype is expressed; complete penetrance means it is expressed in every individual carrying the relevant genotype under the stated conditions. We assume no genotype or phenotype group is selectively lost before scoring. Those conditions are part of why the predicted allele combinations can be read as phenotype proportions.

The 9:3:3:1 ratio is an expectation for this particular heterozygote cross under these assumptions. It does not schedule the next sixteen offspring, and an earlier tall plant does not make the next offspring owe the cross a short plant. Finite samples can differ by chance. Different parents will change the gametes and their weights, even if two characteristics are still being followed. The next cross shows why deriving the contributions is more useful than carrying a memorized ratio into every problem.

A tomato cross with four gamete types from one parent and two from the other

Now switch from peas to tomatoes. We are following fruit colour and plant height, so define the letters for this new problem. The loci assort independently, fertilisation is random, and the phenotype rules apply reliably to the offspring being scored.

When two descriptions overlap, count each offspring once

Keep the same tomato cross, but now ask about an offspring that is red-fruited or tall, including one that is both. This is an inclusive “or”: meeting either description is enough. The denominator is still all offspring from the cross.

Use offspring evidence before predicting a squash cross

The tomato problem supplied the parents' genotypes in words. A breeding problem can instead give only their appearances, leaving some alleles unknown. We need to resolve what the evidence allows before drawing a prediction grid. A precise calculation from a guessed parent would still answer the wrong cross.

Switch now to summer squash fruit colour and fruit shape. In this stated model, W is completely dominant to w for colour: WW and Ww give white fruit, while ww gives yellow fruit. D is completely dominant to d for shape: DD and Dd give disk-shaped fruit, while dd gives spherical fruit. These allele rules concern the fruit produced by the plants, with independent assortment and reliable expression as specified.

Ask how much the evidence actually settles

The squash offspring expressed the recessive form at both loci, so it required both recessive contributions. Other offspring observations may be less decisive. A dominant phenotype can still leave a second allele unknown, even when we know which allele was passed on. Inspect each missing allele separately rather than assuming that one offspring always settles an entire parental genotype.

Seeing a recessive offspring can establish that a parent carries the required recessive allele under the model. A finite run without that phenotype does not establish that the allele is absent: a heterozygous parent can, by chance, repeatedly contribute its dominant allele. Keep the difference between positive evidence and an unobserved possibility in mind for the next breeding record.

Compare the evidence and its limit

The white offspring is vv, so each parent supplied v. Parent 1 already showed the violet phenotype and therefore had V; supplying v forces it to be Vvll. Parent 2 was already known to be vv. For leaf shape, parent 1 supplies l. The smooth offspring must have received L from parent 2, giving the offspring vvLl. The actual gamete contributions to this offspring were vl from parent 1 and vL from parent 2.

Parent 2 could still be vvLL or vvLl. Both can supply vL, so this observed offspring does not reveal the second leaf-shape allele. The classmate's claim goes too far: in the squash example the offspring expressed the recessive form at both loci and required both recessive contributions; here smooth leaves show that L was supplied, not that parent 2 lacks l.

A further lobed-leaf offspring would be ll. Parent 1 supplies l, so the second l would have to come from parent 2, forcing that parent to be vvLl. If parent 2 is vvLL, it contributes L every time and all offspring are smooth. If it is vvLl, its L and l contributions make both smooth and lobed offspring possible. A finite run containing only smooth offspring is possible under either candidate; it does not logically force LL. You do not need to assign a probability to the two possible parents to state this limit.
