Why Mendel compared pea plants

Gregor Mendel helped establish the ideas of dominant and recessive inheritance by studying pea plants. He wanted to understand how characteristics passed from parents to offspring. Would crossing plants with contrasting traits produce a blend, or would the offspring show one form rather than the other? To follow his investigation, start with the plants he chose and then examine what happened in each generation.

Consider a pea plant with purple flowers and one with white flowers. What flower colour would you predict if they were crossed? A blending explanation would suggest an intermediate colour, such as pale purple. That is a prediction we could compare with actual offspring. Knowing which plants were the parents, and then observing what their offspring produced, gives us a way to investigate inheritance.

Mendel needed information about the plants' breeding history as well as their appearance.

Choose starting plants whose breeding history is known

A line consistently produces the same form of the characteristic when bred within the line under the stated conditions. Mendel allowed pea plants to self-pollinate, using their own pollen, over generations to establish such lines. One purple flower on one plant would not establish that history. For the simple one-gene, complete-dominance model we are using, the contrasting true-breeding lines are : each plant has two copies of the same allele at the relevant gene's position.

Pea flowers can supply pollen and also contain eggs. Pollination is the transfer of pollen to the receiving part of a flower. A pollen tube can then deliver a sperm cell to an egg. The joining of the sperm and egg is fertilisation. An egg or sperm is a gamete, a reproductive cell that contributes genetic information to an offspring. Pollination brings the pollen into position; fertilisation is the later joining of the two gametes.

In self-pollination, the pollen comes from the same plant. A controlled cross between chosen plants instead uses a selected pollen donor. Mendel controlled the pollen source so that an unintended donor or unwanted self-pollination would not change the parentage. That control matters: an offspring observation is only useful for interpreting a particular cross if we know which plants contributed to it.

The observation concerns which phenotype was expressed in each generation; we have not watched an allele disappear.

This gives us the problem to explain: how can the white-flower phenotype be absent from F1 appearance and occur again in F2, without an inherited allele blending away or having to be recreated? Mendel inferred that inherited factors remained distinct. The chromosome account developed later explains how those factors can persist, separate and meet again. We will build that account rather than treating the pattern as a rule to memorize.

Take the same question from flower colour to seed shape

We will now follow a different pea characteristic: round or wrinkled seeds. The purple and white flowers helped establish the experimental question. Seed shape will let us follow the same kind of generation pattern with explicit allele symbols and a worked cross.

An is a version of a gene. For this seed-shape model, let R represent the allele associated with round seeds and r the allele associated with wrinkled seeds. The uppercase and lowercase forms are versions of the same letter because we are tracking the same gene. Under the stated complete-dominance relationship, RR and Rr seeds are round; rr seeds are wrinkled. Complete dominance means that the heterozygote has the same phenotype as the homozygote carrying two dominant alleles.

An Rr individual is , or a heterozygote, because its two alleles differ. Dominant describes the phenotype of that heterozygote. It does not mean that R is more common, better, or more likely than r to enter a gamete. Nor does a round appearance by itself tell us whether a seed is RR or Rr.

In this model, the shape being scored belongs to each offspring seed with its own genotype. That seed can grow into a plant which contributes alleles to the next generation of seeds. The plant carrying a seed and the offspring within that seed belong to different generations. Keep this distinction in view as you read the next drawing.

Begin at the top: the two seed forms stand for the contrasting true-breeding starting lines. Follow the arrows to the all-round F1 group, then to the round and wrinkled F2 groups. One drawn seed represents a category or generation here, not the number of seeds grown. The arrows abbreviate reproduction; they do not show the egg and sperm that have to join.

The flower table used self-pollination of F1 plants. This seed drawing describes crosses between F1 plants. Those are different ways to arrange the reproduction. We will see why an Rr plant self-pollinating and a cross between two Rr plants give the same allele-combination prediction in this model. For now, notice the shared pattern: one form is expressed in F1, and both forms occur in F2. The labelled 3:1 pattern still needs a mechanism and a probability explanation.

How an allele reaches a gamete

To explain the seed pattern, start inside an Rr plant. A chromosome is a DNA-bearing structure that carries many genes. A locus is a gene's position on a chromosome. In an ordinary diploid cell there are two chromosome sets. Corresponding chromosomes form a homologous pair: one came from each parent, and both have the same kinds of genes at corresponding loci. At the seed-shape locus, this plant has R on one homologue and r on the other.

Copying increases copies, not allele versions

Meiosis is the cell-division process that reduces two chromosome sets to one. DNA is copied before that process begins. Each chromosome then has two sister chromatids, the copied DNA-bearing parts joined together. At the locus we are following, normal copying produces two R-bearing copies from the R-bearing chromosome and two r-bearing copies from the r-bearing chromosome. There are now four physical copies of that locus, but there are still only two allele versions, R and r. Copying has not changed r into R or mixed their sequences.

An X-shaped chromosome in a diagram usually represents one copied chromosome with two joined sister chromatids. It is not a pair of homologous chromosomes. The corresponding copied chromosome from the other parent is a second structure. For the single-locus account here, we assume ordinary copying and separation without a mutation changing the tracked allele.

Separate the pair, then separate the copies

Meiosis uses two divisions for that reduction. In meiosis I, the homologous chromosomes separate into different cells. Their sister chromatids remain joined at this stage. There is no further round of DNA copying between the divisions. In meiosis II, the sister chromatids separate. The resulting cells have one chromosome set and are haploid.

Use the chromosome drawing to distinguish those two separations. It contains two chromosome pairs and two possible arrangements, so we will deliberately follow just the long chromosome pair in the left-hand arrangement. The short pair and the comparison of arrangements answer a later question about two genes.

At the top left, locate the long blue X and the long red X. They represent the two homologues, while the two joined parts of each X represent its sister chromatids. Follow the long blue X into one middle cell and the long red X into the other. That is the outcome of the first division: the homologues are apart, but each chromosome is still copied. The middle row is labelled Metaphase II, when chromosomes are positioned for the second division and the sister chromatids are still joined. At the bottom, follow the long single rods. The sisters have separated in the second division. The short rods belong to the other chromosome pair, which we are setting aside.

The image has no R or r labels, so its colours alone cannot tell us which seed-shape allele a chromosome carries. Connect it to our model by following the corresponding locus: an Rr plant starts with R on one homologue and r on the other, and a haploid contribution receives one allele at that locus. The picture is a simplified chromosome account, not a complete pea reproductive life cycle.

In flowering plants, meiosis produces haploid spores, cells that continue the reproductive life cycle. Later cell divisions use mitosis to separate sister chromatids after DNA copying, maintaining the haploid chromosome number in the resulting cells. They produce the small haploid structures that form the sperm and egg cells. Gametes therefore arise through further stages after plant meiosis. The point we need is preserved through those later divisions: the chromosome set has been reduced, so an egg or sperm contributes one allele at the seed-shape locus, not the parent's whole Rr pair.

Which allele can be contributed?

An RR parent has only R available at this locus, so every normal gamete contribution carries R. An rr parent contributes r. An Rr parent can contribute R or r. Under ordinary equal segregation, each alternative has a probability of one half. A expresses the chance of an outcome under stated conditions.

Segregation is the separation of the two alleles' contributions during the production of haploid reproductive cells. Dominance does not direct that separation toward R. It tells us how Rr is expressed, while chromosome separation explains how R and r remain available as separate contributions. The r version does not have to be visible in the parent's phenotype to be inherited.

Worked example

Explain the seed-shape generations

We can now explain the pattern rather than only name it. The given starting lines are true breeding for round and wrinkled seeds. We are using the one-gene complete-dominance model: RR and Rr give round seeds; rr gives wrinkled seeds. Known breeding history allows us to write the starting cross as RR × rr. The cross sign × means these are the two parents being crossed; it does not tell us to multiply the letters.

Account for the F1 generation

Look first at the parent labels in this drawing. Each has two letters because a parent is diploid at the tracked locus. Then look at the single F1 category below them. We need the gamete step between those two levels to explain why that category has its particular genotype.

The RR parent can contribute only R, and the rr parent can contribute only r. At fertilisation, one egg and one sperm join. Their chromosome sets restore a diploid pair in the zygote, the first cell of the offspring. Every available combination in this cross therefore contains one R and one r: Rr. Apply the phenotype rule only after making that allele pair. Rr is round under complete dominance.

The F1 category is round because of its Rr phenotype rule, not because r was removed. The r allele is present in every F1 genotype in this cross. Following contributions has supplied information that appearance alone could not: a round seed could be RR or Rr in general, but the known RR × rr parentage identifies Rr here.

Account for the F2 generation

Grow the F1 seeds into plants. Each Rr plant can supply R or r in its eggs and R or r in its sperm. Self-pollination can bring its own pollen to a flower, but fertilisation still joins two gametes. A controlled cross between two Rr F1 plants also joins one R-or-r contribution to another R-or-r contribution. For either specified arrangement, the allele cross we need is Rr × Rr.

A organizes those combinations. It does not create the gamete types; the meiosis account established them first. We use a square now because we want to keep each contribution pair visible and avoid overlooking a possible combination.

In the square below, read the letters across the top as sperm contributions and the letters down the left as egg contributions. This is our stated reading convention for this image, which does not label the parental roles. Either axis could be assigned to either parent if the labels were stated consistently. The edge letters each represent a gamete's single allele; an interior entry combines the allele above it with the allele beside it and represents an offspring genotype.

Follow all four positions once, using the edges to justify each entry:

  • Upper left: an R egg contribution meets an R sperm contribution, giving RR.
  • Upper right: an R egg contribution meets an r sperm contribution, giving Rr.
  • Lower left: an r egg contribution meets an R sperm contribution. It is again Rr; writing rR to track the contributing parents would name the same heterozygous genotype.
  • Lower right: an r egg contribution meets an r sperm contribution, giving rr. This offspring expresses the wrinkled phenotype.

The lower-right entry explains the returning form. Each F1 parent already carried r and could pass it on. Two r contributions can meet at fertilisation, producing rr. No allele had to be recreated. This seed-shape model is autosomal: the tracked gene is on a chromosome other than a sex chromosome. Its phenotype rule is the same whether R arrived in the egg or the sperm, so the two middle combinations belong to the same genotype category.

Explain why the four pairs are equally likely

Equal segregation gives an R-or-r contribution a probability of ½ on each edge. We also assume random fertilisation: which of the two egg contributions occurs does not bias which sperm contribution joins it. To find the probability of a particular pair, both events must occur. For example, an r egg and an r sperm have joint probability ½ × ½ = ¼. The multiplication is justified by the independent contributions in this model, not by the fact that the diagram happens to contain four boxes.

The same reasoning gives ¼ to each of the four positions. Together they cover all the possible contribution pairs, and ¼ + ¼ + ¼ + ¼ = 1. We add these probabilities because an individual offspring occupies one of these alternative contribution categories. It cannot occupy two different positions at the same time.

Keep the two processes separate as well. Segregation explains why one parent can provide a single-allele contribution. Fertilisation explains how two contributions form the offspring's pair. Alignment and separation within one parent's meiosis cannot, by themselves, join that parent's gamete to a gamete from another parent.

Compare the prediction with the counted offspring

The 3:1 phenotype ratio predicts three quarters round and one quarter wrinkled over many offspring from the specified Rr × Rr cross. It does not assign the order in which offspring must appear or guarantee an exact ratio in a small group. Every fertilisation is another event under the same model. An earlier round seed does not make the next event owe the cross a wrinkled seed.

Mendel recorded how many offspring showed each seed form. One reported seed-shape result was 5,474 round and 1,850 wrinkled. To express that as round seeds for each one wrinkled seed, divide both counts by the wrinkled count: (5,474 ÷ 1,850) : (1,850 ÷ 1,850) ≈ 2.96 : 1. Dividing both sides by the same number preserves the comparison and makes the two ratios easy to compare.

The observed 2.96:1 is close to the predicted 3:1, but it is not exactly 3:1. The four boxes in a Punnett square organize probabilities; they are not a schedule for producing seeds. Larger samples tend to give proportions closer to the model's probabilities, but the model does not require every larger sample to be closer than every smaller one. The important distinction is between a probability prediction and the particular counts that occurred.

Our prediction used known starting genotypes, equal segregation, random fertilisation and complete dominance. We also assumed that the stated phenotypes were expressed reliably and counted without one kind of offspring being selectively lost. Those conditions connect the allele model to the observed phenotype counts. A short run can differ by chance. A repeated, substantial mismatch gives us a reason to check the starting genotypes, observations and assumptions, rather than declaring from one small sample that segregation has stopped.

Use each part of the explanation for its own job

Mendel inferred distinct inherited factors from the breeding patterns. The later chromosome account supplies a physical explanation for their transmission. The describes the separation of the two allele contributions so that a normal gamete receives one allele at the locus. Fertilisation restores a pair. Complete dominance explains why Rr and RR share the round phenotype. None of these steps requires the r allele to disappear when its phenotype is unexpressed.

Return briefly to the flower-colour table. It followed a different characteristic, but its question was the same. A purple-flowered F1 plant could carry the recessive flower-colour allele without expressing white flowers. If two recessive contributions met in an F2 offspring, the white phenotype could be expressed again. The observed flower sequence now has the same kind of allele-contribution explanation as the seed-shape sequence.

We can now answer the opening inheritance problem. An allele can persist through normal copying, separate into a gamete contribution, and meet another copy of the same recessive allele at fertilisation. The phenotype can therefore be absent from one generation's appearance and occur in the next without blending or recreation.
