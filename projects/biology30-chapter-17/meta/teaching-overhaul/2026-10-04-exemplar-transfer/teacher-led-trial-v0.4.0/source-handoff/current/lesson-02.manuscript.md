# Mendel’s experiments and segregation

## A flower colour disappears, then returns

Mendel crossed pea plants from lines with purple flowers and lines with white flowers. The first offspring generation had purple flowers. The colours had not produced the pale-purple blend that a blending explanation would suggest. When that generation reproduced, white flowers appeared again. **How could a phenotype disappear for a generation without its allele being lost?**

Keep the distinction from lesson01 in view. A **phenotype** is an expressed characteristic, such as flower colour. A **genotype** is the allele combination at the locus being considered. An **allele** is a version of a gene, and a **locus** is the gene's position on a chromosome. An allele can be present in the genotype even when its associated recessive phenotype is not expressed.

### Know what was crossed

A **true-breeding** line consistently produces the same form of the relevant trait when bred within the line under the stated conditions. That description comes from breeding history, not from looking at one plant. In the simple one-gene model used here, the contrasting true-breeding lines are homozygous: each individual has two copies of the same allele at that locus.

Controlled crossing makes the parentage known. A pea flower can supply pollen and can also contain eggs. **Pollination** transfers pollen to the receiving flower; a sperm cell delivered through the pollen tube can later join an egg. That joining is **fertilisation**. To compare chosen lines, Mendel controlled which plant supplied the pollen instead of allowing an unintended pollen donor or self-pollination to change the cross.

The chosen starting parents form the **P generation**. Their offspring form the **F1 generation**, the first filial generation. Offspring produced when the F1 plants reproduce in the specified design form the **F2 generation**. These labels tell you how the generations are related. They do not mean that every P individual is homozygous or every F1 individual is heterozygous in every possible experiment.

### Follow the same question using seed shape

We will now move from flower colour to another pea characteristic: round or wrinkled seeds. Use **R** for the round-seed allele and **r** for the wrinkled-seed allele. Under complete dominance, **RR and Rr seeds are round; rr seeds are wrinkled**. Rr is a heterozygote because its two alleles differ. Dominant describes this heterozygote's phenotype; it does not mean common, better or more likely to be inherited.

In this model, the shape being scored belongs to each offspring seed with its own genotype. A seed can then be grown into a plant, which will contribute alleles to the next generation of seeds. Keep those generations separate: a parent's genotype helps determine its gametes, while the genotype of a particular offspring seed comes from the two gametes that formed it.

## Each parent contributes one allele

A chromosome is a DNA-bearing structure carrying many genes. In an ordinary **diploid** cell there are two chromosome sets. The corresponding chromosomes form a **homologous pair**: one came from each parent, and both carry the same kinds of genes at corresponding loci. An Rr individual has R at the seed-shape locus on one homologue and r at that locus on the other.

Before meiosis, DNA is copied. Each chromosome then consists of two **sister chromatids**, the copied versions joined together. Copying an R-bearing chromosome produces two R-bearing sister copies; it does not turn r into R or mix the two allele versions. We are following ordinary copying and separation, without a mutation changing the allele.

**Meiosis** reduces the chromosome sets from two to one. The homologous chromosomes separate in the first division. The sister chromatids separate in the second division, with no further DNA copying between those divisions. A cell with one chromosome set is **haploid**. In a flowering plant, the reproductive life cycle produces haploid gametes after this reduction. We only need to follow the allele contribution here: an egg or sperm carries one allele at this locus, not the parent's whole pair.

Thus an RR parent can contribute R; an rr parent can contribute r; and an Rr parent can contribute R or r. A **probability** is the chance of an outcome under the stated conditions. Under ordinary segregation, either allele from the heterozygote has a probability of one half of being in a gamete. Dominance does not decide which chromosome separates into that gamete. The two allele versions remain distinct as they are passed on.

Worked example

## From the starting cross to the second generation

Start with the true-breeding seed-shape lines: **RR × rr**. The multiplication-style cross sign means these are the two parents being crossed; it is not an instruction to multiply the letters.

The RR parent contributes R in its gametes. The rr parent contributes r. At fertilisation, one egg and one sperm join, restoring a pair of alleles in the **zygote**, the first cell of the offspring. Every combination available in this cross is therefore Rr. Applying the phenotype rule after combining the alleles gives round F1 seeds. The r allele is still present in each seed's genotype.

Grow the F1 seeds into plants. When an Rr F1 plant self-pollinates, its eggs can carry R or r and its sperm can carry R or r. The same allele-combination reasoning applies to a controlled cross between two Rr F1 plants. The F2 cross is **Rr × Rr**.

Read the small grid from its edges inward. The left edge gives an egg's allele; the top edge gives a sperm's allele. Each interior entry combines those two contributions. The edges represent gamete possibilities, and the interior entries represent offspring genotypes. They are not the same kind of cell.

Possible F2 allele combinations from Rr × Rr

| Egg allele ↓ / sperm allele → | R, probability ½ | r, probability ½ |
|-------------------------------|------------------|------------------|
| R, probability ½              | RR — round       | Rr — round       |
| r, probability ½              | rR — round       | rr — wrinkled    |

Follow the lower-right entry first. The egg contributes r and the sperm contributes r, so the offspring is rr and expresses the wrinkled phenotype. Neither allele had to be recreated. Both were available in the F1 parents before they formed gametes.

Now compare the upper-right and lower-left entries. Rr and rR are the same heterozygous genotype; the written order only shows which gamete supplied which allele in this table. The gene in this model is **autosomal**, meaning it is on a chromosome other than a sex chromosome. Here the complete-dominance rule is the same whichever parent supplied R.

With equal R/r gamete probabilities and random fertilisation, the four contribution pairs are equally likely. They group into **1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr** for genotype. RR and Rr both express round seeds, so the phenotype grouping is **3 round : 1 wrinkled**. Grouping by appearance combines two genotype categories; it does not change the allele pairs inside them.

This distinction separates two processes. **Segregation** produces single-allele contributions as homologous chromosomes separate during gamete production. **Random fertilisation** combines one parent's gamete with the other's. Chromosomes aligning in one parent's meiosis do not themselves join that parent's gamete to a gamete from another parent.

## A predicted ratio is not an offspring schedule

The 3:1 phenotype ratio predicts three quarters round and one quarter wrinkled over many offspring from this cross. It does not assign a compulsory order to the offspring or guarantee those exact proportions in a small group. Each fertilisation is another event; an earlier round seed does not make a later seed owe the cross a wrinkled result.

Mendel's reported seed-shape counts included 5,474 round and 1,850 wrinkled seeds, approximately 2.96 round for each wrinkled seed. That is close to 3:1, not exactly 3:1. The useful evidence is a repeated pattern that the inheritance model explains, not a perfect match from every small sample.

For this prediction, we have used known parental genotypes, ordinary equal segregation, random fertilisation and the stated complete-dominance relationship. We also assume that the phenotypes are expressed reliably and recorded without one kind of offspring being selectively lost from the count. Chance can change the observed proportions among possible outcomes. A consistent mismatch can also mean that a starting genotype, observation or model assumption needs checking.

Mendel inferred that inherited factors remained distinct from the breeding patterns he observed. The later chromosome account explains how those factors can be copied, separated and combined. The **law of segregation** explains the allele contributions; complete dominance explains why the Rr heterozygote looks round in this model. Neither rule says that the r allele was destroyed.
