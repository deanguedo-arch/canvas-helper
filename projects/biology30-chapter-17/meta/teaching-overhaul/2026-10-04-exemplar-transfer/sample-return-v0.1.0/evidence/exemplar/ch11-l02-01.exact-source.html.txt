<h2>How can one cell reach a distant muscle?</h2>
<p>A nerve signal may need to travel from your spinal cord toward a muscle in your foot. A chain of round, identical cells would not explain the long fibres inside a nerve. Neurons have specialized shapes that let one cell receive many inputs and send information over a considerable distance.</p>
<p>Start with a typical <strong>multipolar neuron</strong>: it has many dendrites and one axon. <strong>Dendrites</strong> are branching receiving regions. The <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="cell body" data-bio-term-route="lesson-02" type="button">cell body</button></strong>, or soma, contains the nucleus and organelles and maintains the cell's metabolism. The <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="axon" data-bio-term-route="lesson-02" type="button">axon</button></strong> carries action potentials toward its terminal branches. An <strong>action potential</strong> is a brief electrical change across a neuron’s membrane. Regenerating that change along the axon carries a nerve signal. For now, connect this signal with the continuous axon; Lessons 4 and 5 will explain the ion movements that produce it. An <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="axon terminal" data-bio-term-route="lesson-02" type="button">axon terminal</button></strong> passes a signal to another cell at a junction called a synapse.</p>

<p>Look first at the large yellow cell body on the left. Trace one short branch outward: that is a dendrite. Now follow the long yellow extension to the right under the blue coverings. That continuous extension is the axon; the blue coverings are insulation around it, not separate neurons. The large horizontal arrow means the usual direction of information flow through this model.</p>
<p>The narrowed region where the axon begins is labelled the <strong>axon hillock</strong>. Signals arriving at different receiving regions can combine and influence whether an action potential begins near the start of the axon. The cell body does not consciously choose. Its membranes respond to the combined electrical effects of incoming signals.</p>
<p>Finish at the terminal branches. Their branching allows the neuron to communicate with more than one target. The next cell is separated from a chemical synaptic terminal by a tiny <strong>synaptic cleft</strong>. The electrical signal along an axon and the chemical signal across that cleft are different parts of the pathway.</p>
