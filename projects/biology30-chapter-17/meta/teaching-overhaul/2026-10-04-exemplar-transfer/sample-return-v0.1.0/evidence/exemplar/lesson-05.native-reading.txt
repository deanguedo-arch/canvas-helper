Two-trait crosses and independent assortment
How do two single-gene predictions combine?

Find possible gametes before drawing boxes
For AaBb with independent assortment, choose one allele from the A/a pair and one from the B/b pair. The possible gametes are AB, Ab, aB and ab, each with probability 1/4. An Aabb parent has only Ab and ab as distinct possibilities.
Do not place Aa or Bb on a gamete edge. Those are two alleles of one locus, not one allele from each of two loci. A correctly sized square follows from the distinct gametes, not from a rule that every two-trait cross needs sixteen boxes.
Independent assortment: follow both allele choicesView larger
Follow each branch from AaBb through one allele at the first locus and one at the second. Each endpoint is one haploid gamete, with probability one quarter. Schematic — not to scale.
Solve separate loci when independence is stated
For AaBb × AaBb, the A locus gives a 3/4 dominant-phenotype probability and 1/4 recessive probability. The B locus gives the same. If the loci assort independently, the probability of both dominant phenotypes is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16.
The other phenotype combinations are 3/16, 3/16 and 1/16, producing the familiar 9:3:3:1 expectation. This ratio is specific to the stated heterozygote cross and allele relationships.
For a different cross, recompute. In AaBb × aaBb, probability of A_ is 1/2 and probability of B_ is 3/4, so both dominant phenotypes have probability 3/8. A memorized 9/16 would be wrong.
Make the assumption part of the answer
Genes close together on the same chromosome can be linked. Their gamete combinations may not occur equally, so the simple product calculation can fail. Later lessons will use offspring data to test this.
For now, state when independent assortment is assumed. Check that the probabilities of all mutually exclusive phenotype categories add to one. When calculating an expected count, multiply the appropriate probability by the total number of offspring.
Both the square and product method are tools for organizing the same biological possibilities. Choose the clearer method, but show enough steps that the allele contributions can be checked.
Worked example
Solve a two-locus cross without unnecessary boxes
In an independent complete-dominance model, RrTt × rrTt produces offspring. Find the probability of red, short offspring: R_tt.
At the colour locus, Rr × rr gives P(R_) = 1/2.
At the height locus, Tt × Tt gives P(tt) = 1/4.
Both conditions are required, so multiply: 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8.
For 80 offspring the expected count is 80 × 1/8 = 10.
The result relies on independence of the two loci.
