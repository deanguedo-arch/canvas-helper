How can a trait disappear in one generation and reappear in the next?

Follow the alleles across generations
Mendel studied traits in pea plants and used controlled crosses between lines with contrasting forms. In a simple example, let R represent round seeds and r wrinkled seeds, with complete dominance of R. A true-breeding RR line crossed with rr produces Rr offspring in the F1 generation.
Every F1 individual can have the round phenotype while still carrying the r allele. The recessive allele has not disappeared or blended into a weaker version. It remains part of the genotype.
Segregation explains reappearance
An Rr individual forms gametes containing R or r. The alleles segregate as homologous chromosomes separate during meiosis. In the simple model, each allele has a one-half chance of entering a gamete.
When two Rr individuals produce the F2 generation, gametes can combine as RR, Rr, rR or rr. Rr and rR name the same heterozygous genotype. The expected genotype ratio is 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr. With complete dominance, the expected phenotype ratio is 3 round : 1 wrinkled.
The wrinkled phenotype reappears because an offspring can receive r from both parents. No new r mutation is required to explain that result.
Expected ratios are probabilities
A 3:1 expected ratio is not a promise that every group of four offspring contains exactly three round and one wrinkled. Each offspring is another event. Chance variation is especially noticeable in small samples.
Mendel's repeated patterns supported a particulate model of inheritance: inherited units remain distinguishable rather than permanently blending. Later chromosome and DNA evidence connected those units with cellular mechanisms.
When interpreting a cross, identify the parental genotypes, gamete possibilities and allele combinations before applying the phenotype rule. A remembered ratio should be the result of that reasoning, not a substitute for it.
Worked example
Recover a hidden allele
A true-breeding dark plant is crossed with a true-breeding pale plant. All F1 plants are dark; some F2 offspring are pale.
Under complete dominance, use DD for the true-breeding dark parent and dd for pale.
The F1 genotype is Dd. The d allele remains present despite the dark phenotype.
F1 parents can each contribute d in a gamete.
An F2 dd offspring expresses pale, explaining reappearance without blending or a new mutation.
