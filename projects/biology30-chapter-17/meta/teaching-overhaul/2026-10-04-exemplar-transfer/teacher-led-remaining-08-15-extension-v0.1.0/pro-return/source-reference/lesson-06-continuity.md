# Incomplete dominance and codominance

<!-- Authored teaching interval begins. Boundary/protection notes are in the separate handoff records. -->

## A flower cross with a different result

A red-flowered plant and a white-flowered plant produce pink-flowered offspring. That observation gives us a reason to examine the expression rule in an inheritance model. Under complete dominance, a heterozygote has the same scored appearance as one homozygote. What should we conclude when its appearance is different from both?

We will follow flower colour in a stated four-o'clock plant model. The starting lines breed consistently for red or white flowers under the conditions being studied. That breeding history, together with the model, identifies the starting plants as homozygous. A single photograph of a flower would not establish the breeding history.

Let **R<sub>1</sub>** name the red-associated **allele**, or version of the flower-colour gene, and **R<sub>2</sub>** the white-associated version. The small 1 and 2 are labels attached to R. Each complete symbol names one allele; the number does not count chromosomes or tell us to multiply. In plain text, the same symbols are written R1 and R2. A diploid plant has two copies at this locus, so R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>1</sub> and R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>2</sub> each describe a homozygote, while R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub> describes a heterozygote with different versions.

### Read the parents before the offspring

In the image, begin with the two flowers at the top. Match the red flower to R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>1</sub> and the white flower to R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>2</sub>. The cross sign connects the chosen parents. Follow their arrows to the pink flower below, then compare its two allele symbols with the symbols in the parents. The complete picture is visible; read those levels in order to keep parentage clear.

![A red R1R1 flower and a white R2R2 flower at the top are joined by a cross sign. Arrows from both lead to a pink R1R2 flower below. The diagram abbreviates reproduction between the parent and offspring levels.](../assets/teaching-batch2-v010/slide-50-image42.png)

Known red and white starting lines produce a pink heterozygous offspring category in this flower-colour model. The arrows omit the separate gamete and fertilisation stages.

The starting parents are the **P generation**. Their offspring are the **F1 generation**. A normal gamete contributes one allele at this locus: the red parent contributes R<sub>1</sub> with probability 1, and the white parent contributes R<sub>2</sub> with probability 1. Fertilisation joins an egg and a sperm, restoring the allele pair. Every available pairing in this cross is therefore R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>, with probability 1 × 1 = 1. The single pink flower stands for that offspring category, not a counted sample of one plant.

Now compare the expressed colours. The **phenotype**, the characteristic we observe or measure, is pink in the heterozygote, between the red and white homozygote appearances. That relationship is **incomplete dominance**. We name it from the comparison of genotypes and their phenotypes, rather than from the colour pink by itself.

In this flower model, the red-associated allele contributes to red-pigment production. A heterozygote has one red-associated copy out of its two allele copies and makes less red pigment than the red homozygote, giving an intermediate pink appearance. It still has two copies of the locus, R<sub>1</sub> and R<sub>2</sub>. The colour does not mean that the DNA versions have fused into a new pink allele. Nor does incomplete dominance require every intermediate phenotype to arise through exactly the same pigment mechanism.

### Ask what the pink plants can pass on

To test whether the original versions remain available, let the pink F1 plants reproduce. Self-pollination of an R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub> plant can bring together its own egg and sperm contributions; a cross between two R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub> plants gives the same parental allele pairs in this model. In either specified arrangement, the cross is **R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub> × R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>**, and the offspring are the F2 generation.

The chromosome mechanism has not changed. DNA is copied before meiosis. Homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis I, and sister chromatids separate in meiosis II, with no additional DNA copying between those divisions. In flowering plants, meiosis first produces haploid spores; later stages maintaining the haploid set produce the structures that form gametes. The result needed here is one allele at this locus in an egg or sperm. Copying and separation retain the R<sub>1</sub> and R<sub>2</sub> versions.

Under equal segregation, each pink parent contributes R<sub>1</sub> with probability ½ or R<sub>2</sub> with probability ½. Incomplete dominance changes the plant's expressed colour, not those gamete probabilities. We also assume random fertilisation: the allele in the egg does not favour one of the possible sperm alleles. That is why we can multiply the two contribution probabilities for a particular offspring pair.

Read the table with sperm contributions across the top and egg contributions down the left. Each outside label is a single allele in a gamete. Each interior entry joins the allele above it to the allele beside it, forming an offspring genotype. Both row weights and both column weights total 1.

At the upper left, R<sub>1</sub> meets R<sub>1</sub>, giving the red homozygote. At the upper right and lower left, different parental routes bring one R<sub>1</sub> and one R<sub>2</sub> together. Reversing their order does not create another genotype in this ordinary autosomal model. At the lower right, each parent contributes R<sub>2</sub>, producing a white homozygote. White is possible because the parents carried and transmitted that version even while their own flowers were pink.

The four distinct contribution routes have total probability ¼ + ¼ + ¼ + ¼ = 1. For genotype grouping, add the two routes to the heterozygote: ¼ + ¼ = ½. The genotype ratio is **1 R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>1</sub> : 2 R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub> : 1 R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>2</sub>**. All three genotypes have distinguishable colours here, so the **phenotypic ratio**, the relative proportions of appearance categories, is also **1 red : 2 pink : 1 white**. The ratio belongs to these parents and this expression rule.

### What would the ratio mean in a planting?

Suppose 96 offspring plants from this cross are grown and scored. An **expected count** is the model's average predicted count for groups of that size. Multiply each phenotype probability by 96: red gives 96 × ¼ = 24, pink gives 96 × ½ = 48, and white gives 96 × ¼ = 24. The counts total 24 + 48 + 24 = 96, and the underlying probabilities total 1. We have assumed reliable expression and no selective loss of a genotype before scoring.

An actual planting need not contain exactly those counts. Chance affects which gametes meet, and the next fertilisation does not compensate for earlier flowers to complete a ratio. However, sampling variation can change the counts of *possible* outcomes; it cannot supply an allele absent from all available parental contributions. If a record contains a genotype the stated parents cannot produce, check the parentage, observations and model instead of explaining every mismatch as a small sample.

## Does a third appearance always mean an intermediate?

A coat can look different from either solid parental colour because both kinds of hair are present. That gives us a different observation to explain. Before calling the coat intermediate, we need to ask what its colour is made of.

Look at the horse's body in the photograph, then compare it with the darker mane, lower legs and much of the head. The body has the mixed grey-blue appearance called **blue roan**. In this coat type, black hairs and white hairs are intermixed. The photograph shows the overall coat appearance; it is not a microscope view of each hair or a breeding record identifying this particular horse's genotype.

![A blue-roan horse with a mixed grey-blue body, dark mane and lower legs, and a white facial marking. Original copyright and photographer text remain visible. The coat illustration supports a comparison of an intermixed body coat with darker regions.](../assets/teaching-batch2-v010/slide-55-image53.jpg)

Blue roan combines black and white hairs in the coat. Its overall grey-blue appearance does not mean that every hair has become an intermediate blue colour.

In **codominance**, both allele-associated products or characteristics can be detected in a heterozygote. For the coat comparison, both hair colours are present. In the incomplete-dominance flower model, the scored heterozygote colour is intermediate. A distant view that looks like an average is not enough to distinguish those explanations; examine what is being expressed.

We will now use a stated **red-roan cattle model** for the allele calculation. Its colours are red and white, whereas the horse photograph illustrates black and white hairs. Reuse R<sub>1</sub> and R<sub>2</sub> as labels within this new coat model: R<sub>1</sub> is associated with red hair and R<sub>2</sub> with white hair. These symbols do not identify the same gene as the flower symbols. The meaning comes from the model being described.

Read each row from the allele pair to the hair colours. Compare the middle row with the two homozygotes. The middle animal has both colours of hair, rather than each hair being uniformly pink.

A roan cow and roan bull in this model are both R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>. Each contributes R<sub>1</sub> or R<sub>2</sub> at ½. Their offspring therefore have the same four weighted allele pairings already derived for two heterozygotes: R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>1</sub> at ¼, the two routes to R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub> totalling ½, and R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>2</sub> at ¼. The genotype ratio is 1:2:1. Apply the *coat* table now: the phenotype ratio is 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white. The calculation survives the change of expression rule; the meaning of the heterozygote category changes.

Codominance does not require the two contributions to occur in different cells. Both can be expressed in the same cell. For example, a person with the AB blood-group phenotype can have both A and B molecular markers, called **antigens**, on the same red blood cells. We will examine that model next. The general comparison is whether both contributions are detectable, rather than whether a photograph happens to contain patches.

### Keep the genotype grouping and expression rule separate

A **genotype** records the allele combination. A **heterozygote phenotype** is the characteristic expressed when the two versions differ. A 1:2:1 genotype ratio alone does not tell us which phenotype rule applies.

<table><caption>How the same heterozygote-cross genotype grouping can be interpreted</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col">Expression relationship</th><th scope="col">Heterozygote compared with homozygotes</th><th scope="col">Phenotype grouping for this two-heterozygote cross</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th scope="row">Complete dominance</th><td>Matches one homozygote's scored phenotype</td><td>Combine its ½ with that homozygote's ¼: ¾ versus ¼, or 3:1</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Incomplete dominance</th><td>Intermediate phenotype</td><td>Three distinguishable groups: ¼, ½, ¼, or 1:2:1</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Codominance in the stated coat model</th><td>Both associated characteristics detectable</td><td>Three distinguishable groups: ¼, ½, ¼, or 1:2:1</td></tr></tbody></table>

Read the middle column before the ratio column. The two 1:2:1 phenotype ratios have different biological meanings. The shared numbers cannot tell us whether a heterozygote is intermediate or expresses both forms. The phenotype evidence supplies that distinction. None of these relationships ranks an allele as better, more common or more likely to enter a gamete.

## A plant can use different expression rules for different characteristics

An inheritance description applies to the characteristic and locus being studied, rather than to every gene in an organism. A plant can have complete dominance for height and incomplete dominance for flower colour. The contributions still need to be followed at each locus before the phenotype rules are applied.

Use a hypothetical plant model in which T is completely dominant to t: TT and Tt are tall, and tt is short. Retain the flower-colour rules R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>1</sub> red, R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub> pink and R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>2</sub> white. The two loci assort independently, fertilisation is random, and these phenotypes are expressed reliably. Compare a **short pink plant, ttR<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>**, with a **tall pink plant known to be TTR<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>**. Predict their offspring.

Each parent is homozygous at height and heterozygous at colour. The first contributes t with certainty and R<sub>1</sub> or R<sub>2</sub> at ½, giving tR<sub>1</sub> and tR<sub>2</sub>, each 1 × ½ = ½. The second contributes T with certainty and R<sub>1</sub> or R<sub>2</sub> at ½, giving TR<sub>1</sub> and TR<sub>2</sub>, each ½. Each gamete label contains one allele at each locus. Neither parent's complete diploid genotype belongs on a gamete edge.

Put the short parent's gametes down the left and the tall parent's across the top. For the upper-left entry, t joins T to form Tt and R<sub>1</sub> joins R<sub>1</sub> to form R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>1</sub>. It is a tall red offspring. Repeat the pairing within each locus in the other three cells.

<table><caption>ttR<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub> × TTR<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>: each cell has probability ½ × ½ = ¼</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col">Short parent gamete ↓ / tall parent gamete →</th><th scope="col">TR<sub>1</sub>, ½</th><th scope="col">TR<sub>2</sub>, ½</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th scope="row">tR<sub>1</sub>, ½</th><td>TtR<sub>1</sub>R<sub>1</sub>: tall, red</td><td>TtR<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>: tall, pink</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">tR<sub>2</sub>, ½</th><td>TtR<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>: tall, pink</td><td>TtR<sub>2</sub>R<sub>2</sub>: tall, white</td></tr></tbody></table>

Every cell is Tt, so every offspring is tall under complete dominance. Colour has three genotype categories at ¼, ½ and ¼, giving tall/red, tall/pink and tall/white in a 1:2:1 ratio. The probabilities total 1. A pink flower does not make the plant intermediate in height: the two characteristics have different expression rules. Conversely, being heterozygous at both loci does not require the same kind of phenotype at both.

A different parent could change either gamete list. For any new cross, first identify which alleles that parent actually carries, then list its distinct contributions and weights. A homozygote provides one allele type at its locus; a heterozygote provides two. Combine matching loci at fertilisation and only then decide which offspring categories meet the question. This is how the same method can handle a single flower colour or two characteristics with different dominance relationships.

Worked example

### When two genes contribute to one visible colour

Two genes can also affect the *same* visible characteristic. Making a pigment and placing that pigment in a petal are different jobs. If the second job does not occur, the petal's appearance may not reveal which pigment the first gene would otherwise produce. We need that kind of expression rule to interpret the optional coat-colour problem that follows.

Consider an explicitly hypothetical plant model with two independently assorting loci. P/p controls pigment identity: **P_** specifies purple pigment, while **pp** specifies amber pigment. P_ means PP or Pp; the underscore stands for the second allele at the P locus. D/d controls whether the pigment is deposited in the petal. **D_**, meaning DD or Dd, allows deposition. With **dd**, neither pigment is deposited and the petal is pale. These are the stated rules for this model, not a claim about every real flower.

Read the deposition pair first in the table. If it is dd, the visible colour is already settled. If it is D_, inspect the P/p pair to decide which pigment appears. This order makes the dependency in the biological explanation visible.

<table><caption>Two separate jobs contribute to the same petal phenotype</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col">Allele conditions</th><th scope="col">What the model allows</th><th scope="col">Visible phenotype</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th scope="row">P_D_</th><td>Purple pigment is specified and deposited</td><td>Purple</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">ppD_</th><td>Amber pigment is specified and deposited</td><td>Amber</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">P_dd or ppdd</th><td>Deposition does not occur, regardless of the pigment-identity pair</td><td>Pale</td></tr></tbody></table>

This is **gene interaction**: the phenotype associated with one locus depends on another locus. In this example, dd masks the visible effect of the P/p pair, a form of interaction called **epistasis**. The P alleles are still present and can be transmitted. Their colour effect is not visible under dd. This differs from incomplete dominance or codominance, which compare allele expression within one locus. It also differs from adding many small contributions to a continuously varying trait.

#### Establish the parents and contributions

Cross **PpDd × ppDd**. The first parent is purple because it has P_ and D_. The second is amber because it is pp and D_. We are given the genotypes, so we do not have to infer the second D-locus allele from appearance. Assume ordinary equal segregation, independent assortment, random fertilisation and reliable expression, with no genotype category selectively lost before scoring.

PpDd contributes P or p at ½ each and D or d at ½ each. Its four distinct gametes are **PD, Pd, pD and pd**, each with probability ½ × ½ = ¼. The ppDd parent contributes p with certainty and D or d at ½ each. It therefore has two distinct gametes, **pD and pd**, each with probability 1 × ½ = ½. The first list totals four quarters and the second totals two halves, so each accounts for all of that parent's contributions.

Place the four PpDd gametes down the left and the two ppDd gametes across the top. Eight cells list each distinct pairing once. Random fertilisation gives each cell weight **¼ × ½ = ⅛**. Read an offspring's P/p pair and D/d pair separately, then apply the deposition rule before its colour rule.

<table><caption>PpDd × ppDd: eight equally weighted contribution pairs</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col">PpDd gamete ↓ / ppDd gamete →</th><th scope="col">pD, ½</th><th scope="col">pd, ½</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th scope="row">PD, ¼</th><td>PpDD: purple</td><td>PpDd: purple</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Pd, ¼</th><td>PpDd: purple</td><td>Ppdd: pale</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">pD, ¼</th><td>ppDD: amber</td><td>ppDd: amber</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">pd, ¼</th><td>ppDd: amber</td><td>ppdd: pale</td></tr></tbody></table>

For PD with pD, P joins p and D joins D, producing PpDD. Deposition is possible, so its purple pigment is visible. Compare Pd with pd: P still joins p, but d joins d, producing Ppdd. That offspring is pale because dd blocks deposition. The change in the second allele pair explains why the shared Pp pair does not give both offspring the same colour.

The eight cells contain six distinct genotypes: PpDD at ⅛, PpDd at ⅛ + ⅛ = ¼, Ppdd at ⅛, ppDD at ⅛, ppDd at ¼ and ppdd at ⅛. In that order the genotype ratio is **1:2:1:1:2:1**. It totals eight parts. Different cells can be separate routes to the same genotype, so naming that genotype once must not discard its second route.

#### Group the offspring by the requested phenotype

Purple includes the PpDD cell and both PpDd cells, giving 3/8. Amber includes ppDD and both ppDd cells, also 3/8. Pale includes Ppdd *and* ppdd. These are different genotypes with the same deposition failure, so their probabilities add: ⅛ + ⅛ = ¼. Counting only the double-recessive ppdd offspring would miss the pale Ppdd group.

For the probability notation below, P( ) means “probability of” the event inside the brackets; it is different from the allele symbol P. The first-locus cross Pp × pp gives Pp or pp at ½ each. The second-locus cross Dd × Dd gives DD at ¼, Dd at ½ and dd at ¼, so D_ has probability ¾. Use those separate probabilities to check the cell grouping.

<table><caption>Check the phenotype probabilities in two ways</caption><thead><tr><th scope="col">Phenotype</th><th scope="col">From the eight cells</th><th scope="col">From the separate locus probabilities</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><th scope="row">Purple</th><td>3 × ⅛ = 3/8</td><td>P(P_) × P(D_) = ½ × ¾ = 3/8</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Amber</th><td>3 × ⅛ = 3/8</td><td>P(pp) × P(D_) = ½ × ¾ = 3/8</td></tr><tr><th scope="row">Pale</th><td>⅛ + ⅛ = ¼</td><td>P(dd) = ¼, with either P/p outcome</td></tr></tbody></table>

Independent assortment and random fertilisation let us multiply the locus probabilities. The biological interaction changes how those genotypes are *expressed*; it does not, by itself, stop the loci assorting independently.

The three phenotype groups do not overlap and include all offspring. Their sum is **3/8 + 3/8 + 2/8 = 1**, giving a **3 purple : 3 amber : 2 pale** phenotype ratio. Among 80 scored offspring, the expected counts are 80 × 3/8 = 30 purple, 30 amber and 80 × ¼ = 20 pale. The total is 80. These are expectations under the model, not a guarantee of a particular planting's counts.

#### Read the rule supplied with the coat-colour application

The optional Labrador application gives a different pair of genes, B/b and E/e. Read its genotype table as two locus pairs. B_ means BB or Bb, and E_ means EE or Ee. According to its stated rule, **B_E_ is black**, **bbE_ is brown**, and **any B/b pair with ee is yellow**. The two blanks before ee refer to the B-locus pair, not missing E alleles. Both B_ee and bbee belong to the yellow category.

Its supplied B/b rule distinguishes black from brown only when E_ permits that expression. With ee, the coat is yellow under the problem's model, so a yellow coat does not reveal the B/b genotype. This is two-gene interaction, not a pink intermediate or a coat expressing black and brown together. Use the genotypes in the actual question to derive new gamete lists and probabilities; the worked plant cross above had different parents. Then group every permitted genotype using the Labrador table and check that all phenotype probabilities total 1.

#### What changed in the inheritance explanation?

The pink offspring in the opening cross did not require a new transmission mechanism. The parents contributed distinct alleles, fertilisation restored a pair, and the heterozygote expressed an intermediate colour. A roan model instead expresses both associated hair colours. When two genes contribute to one characteristic, a further rule can make the visible result depend on both loci. The gametes and the expression rule each have a different job in the explanation.

So far, the flower and coat models used two versions at each studied locus. A population can contain more versions even though an ordinary diploid individual still has two copies at an autosomal locus. The ABO blood groups give us a way to examine that next question and to see codominance and complete dominance operating within one allele system.

## Meanings, examples and distinctions

<!-- ch17-concept-incomplete-dominance; /course-data/practiceConcepts/20 -->

<details>
<summary>incomplete dominance</summary>

<p><strong>Term:</strong> incomplete dominance</p>

<p><strong>Meaning:</strong> An allele relationship in which the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> Red and white flower alleles produce pink flowers in the specified heterozygote model.</p>

<p><strong>Optional cue:</strong> Identify the structure, quantity or relationship described.</p>

<p><strong>Explanation:</strong> An allele relationship in which the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype. An intermediate phenotype does not mean the alleles permanently blend.</p>

<p><strong>Claim to examine:</strong> Incomplete dominance permanently blends the alleles into a new allele.</p>

<p><strong>Correction:</strong> An allele relationship in which the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype. An intermediate phenotype does not mean the alleles permanently blend.</p>

</details>

<!-- ch17-concept-codominance; /course-data/practiceConcepts/21 -->

<details>
<summary>codominance</summary>

<p><strong>Term:</strong> codominance</p>

<p><strong>Meaning:</strong> An allele relationship in which both allele-associated products or traits are expressed in a heterozygote.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> A roan coat contains both red and white hairs in the model.</p>

<p><strong>Optional cue:</strong> Identify the structure, quantity or relationship described.</p>

<p><strong>Explanation:</strong> An allele relationship in which both allele-associated products or traits are expressed in a heterozygote. Codominance is not limited to separate patches of cells; both products can be present in the same cells.</p>

<p><strong>Claim to examine:</strong> Codominance always means an intermediate blended phenotype.</p>

<p><strong>Correction:</strong> An allele relationship in which both allele-associated products or traits are expressed in a heterozygote. Codominance is not limited to separate patches of cells; both products can be present in the same cells.</p>

</details>

<!-- ch17-concept-heterozygote-phenotype; /course-data/practiceConcepts/22 -->

<details>
<summary>heterozygote phenotype</summary>

<p><strong>Term:</strong> heterozygote phenotype</p>

<p><strong>Meaning:</strong> The characteristic expressed when two different alleles occur together.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> R1R2 may be pink in one model but red-and-white in another.</p>

<p><strong>Optional cue:</strong> Identify the structure, quantity or relationship described.</p>

<p><strong>Explanation:</strong> The characteristic expressed when two different alleles occur together. The same notation does not establish the inheritance relationship without its definition.</p>

<p><strong>Claim to examine:</strong> Every heterozygote has the dominant homozygote phenotype.</p>

<p><strong>Correction:</strong> The characteristic expressed when two different alleles occur together. The same notation does not establish the inheritance relationship without its definition.</p>

</details>

<!-- ch17-concept-phenotypic-ratio; /course-data/practiceConcepts/23 -->

<details>
<summary>phenotypic ratio</summary>

<p><strong>Term:</strong> phenotypic ratio</p>

<p><strong>Meaning:</strong> The expected relative proportions of expressed trait categories.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> An incomplete-dominance heterozygote cross gives three distinguishable phenotype categories.</p>

<p><strong>Optional cue:</strong> Identify the structure, quantity or relationship described.</p>

<p><strong>Explanation:</strong> The expected relative proportions of expressed trait categories. Phenotype ratios can change even when genotype segregation is unchanged.</p>

<p><strong>Claim to examine:</strong> Every heterozygote cross has a 3:1 phenotype ratio.</p>

<p><strong>Correction:</strong> The expected relative proportions of expressed trait categories. Phenotype ratios can change even when genotype segregation is unchanged.</p>

</details>
