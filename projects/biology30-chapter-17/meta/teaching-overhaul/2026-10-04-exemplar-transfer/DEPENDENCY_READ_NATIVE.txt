How can the same gene be associated with different traits?
Learning goal
Distinguish a gene from an allele, genotype from phenotype, and homozygous from heterozygous combinations.
Before you begin
Homologous chromosomes carry corresponding genes but may carry different alleles. Gametes contribute one allele at each autosomal locus in the simple diploid model.

Vocabulary help
Key terms
Words for this lesson
Use these definitions when you need help with a term.
gene
A DNA sequence that contributes to a functional product or inherited characteristic.
allele
An alternative version of a gene.
genotype
The allele combination an organism carries at the locus or loci being considered.
phenotype
An observable or measurable characteristic resulting from genotype and its biological context.
Start with a defined genetic model
A
gene
is a DNA sequence associated with a functional product or characteristic. Alternative versions are alleles. In a diploid autosomal model, an individual inherits one
allele
from each parent and therefore has two alleles at the locus being studied.
The
genotype
is the allele combination, such as BB, Bb or bb. The
phenotype
is the observed or measured characteristic, such as dark or pale pigment. A phenotype can also be an enzyme activity or another physiological property, not only a visible feature.
Dominance describes the heterozygote
Suppose a stated model says B gives dark pigment and is completely dominant to b. BB and Bb individuals have the dark phenotype, while bb individuals have the pale phenotype. The same phenotype can therefore correspond to more than one genotype.
Homozygous means the two alleles are the same, as in BB or bb. Heterozygous means they differ, as in Bb. A dominant allele is expressed in the heterozygote under the stated complete-dominance model. Dominant does not mean most common, healthiest or strongest.
Use the inheritance model supplied. Many traits involve multiple genes, environmental influences or allele relationships other than complete dominance. Do not assume that human eye colour can be accurately predicted by a single B/b model.
Notation keeps the reasoning visible
Define letters before solving a problem. Use the same letter for alleles of one gene and a different letter for a different gene. A capital and lowercase letter are convenient for a complete-dominance model; other models use different notation.
A useful first question is whether the phenotype determines a unique genotype. A recessive phenotype in a simple fully expressed model identifies bb. A dominant phenotype alone leaves BB or Bb possible. Keeping that uncertainty visible prevents an incorrect cross from the start.
Worked example
Infer what the phenotype does and does not tell you
In a fictional beetle model, dark shell B is completely dominant to pale shell b. One beetle is dark and another is pale.
The pale beetle must be bb under this model.
The dark beetle may be BB or Bb.
Write B_ for the unresolved dark genotype rather than silently choosing BB.
Additional parent or offspring information may resolve the missing allele.

Vocabulary help
Key terms
Words for this lesson
Use these definitions when you need help with a term.
Punnett square
A table combining possible parental gametes to organize offspring genotypes.
monohybrid cross
A cross between individuals heterozygous for one studied gene.
probability
The expected proportion of an outcome under specified conditions.
expected number
The predicted count obtained by multiplying a probability by the sample size.
Write gametes, not whole parental genotypes, on the edges
A
Punnett square
organizes combinations of gametes. For Aa × aa, the first parent produces A or a gametes; the second produces only a. Place those gametes on the edges and combine one from each parent in each cell.
The resulting possibilities are Aa and aa in equal proportions. With complete dominance, half are expected to have the dominant
phenotype
and half the recessive phenotype. The square shows why the 1:1 ratio applies; a 3:1 ratio would be the wrong shortcut for this cross.
A single-trait cross: Aa × Aa
View larger
Each row and column represents a gamete. Four equally likely combinations give a 1:2:1 genotype ratio under the stated model. A is completely dominant to a. Schematic — not to scale.
Count the outcome that was actually asked for
For Aa × Aa, the four equally likely combinations are AA, Aa, aA and aa. Grouping the two heterozygous combinations gives probabilities of 1/4 AA, 1/2 Aa and 1/4 aa. The dominant phenotype has
probability
3/4 under complete dominance.
Genotype
probability and phenotype probability therefore can differ. “Heterozygous” refers to two of the four combinations, while “dominant phenotype” includes three. Mark the requested group before choosing your numerator.
When converting to an
expected number
, multiply by the total. If 80 offspring each have a 1/4 chance of aa, the expected number is 80 × 0.25 = 20. The result is an expectation, not a promise.
Independent offspring do not compensate for earlier outcomes
In the simple model, one offspring's genotype does not force the next offspring to be different. If two Aa parents have several dominant-phenotype offspring, the modeled probability for the next offspring is still 1/4 recessive.
For specified independent events, multiply probabilities. Two recessive offspring in a row from Aa × Aa have probability 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16. Do not confuse this ordered event with “at least one recessive among two,” which asks for a different set of outcomes.
State assumptions such as complete dominance and ordinary segregation. They determine which genotype combinations count toward a phenotype.
Worked example
Predict a phenotype and an expected count
In a stated complete-dominance model, Tt is crossed with tt. Predict the fraction and expected number of recessive offspring among 60.
Tt produces T and t gametes; tt produces t gametes.
The equally likely combinations are Tt and tt.
The recessive phenotype is tt, probability 1/2.
Expected number = 60 × 1/2 = 30.
Check: the other expected 30 have the dominant phenotype; observed counts can vary.

Vocabulary help
Key terms
Words for this lesson
Use these definitions when you need help with a term.
test cross
A cross with a homozygous recessive individual used to investigate an unknown genotype.
homozygous
Having two identical alleles at a locus.
heterozygous
Having two different alleles at a locus.
sampling variation
Differences between observed sample outcomes and their expected proportions due to chance.
Choose a tester with a known contribution
An individual with a dominant
phenotype
may be AA or Aa. Crossing it with aa creates a useful test because the recessive tester can contribute only a. The phenotype of the offspring then provides evidence about the unknown parent's contribution.
If the unknown parent is AA, every offspring is expected to be Aa and show the dominant phenotype. If the unknown parent is Aa, the expected offspring are half Aa and half aa. A recessive offspring therefore demonstrates that the unknown parent contributed a, under the stated simple model.
Positive evidence and absence of evidence differ
One aa offspring from B_ × bb means the dominant-phenotype parent must carry the recessive
allele
, assuming the model, parentage and observations are correct. It is
heterozygous
.
By contrast, several dominant offspring do not logically prove the parent is
homozygous
. An Aa parent can, by chance, produce a run of A-bearing gametes. For four independent offspring in an Aa × aa cross, the
probability
that all show the dominant phenotype is (1/2)⁴ = 1/16.
More offspring with no recessive phenotype generally strengthen the homozygous interpretation, but finite samples still require cautious wording. Say what the evidence supports and what remains possible.
Use family information before calculating
Additional parent or offspring information can resolve an unknown
genotype
. A dominant-phenotype individual with an aa parent must have received a from that parent, so it must be Aa. You do not need another
test cross
to establish that conclusion within the model.
Start with the most informative constraints. Write the alleles that must be present, preserve unknowns where necessary, and only then calculate the next cross. Guessing an unresolved genotype can make every later arithmetic step misleading.
Worked example
A recessive offspring resolves the unknown
A black fictional mouse, B_, is crossed with a brown bb mouse. One offspring is brown.
The brown offspring is bb.
The known tester supplied one b.
The unknown black parent supplied the other b, so it carries b.
Its black phenotype also requires B in the complete-dominance model.
The unknown parent is Bb.
