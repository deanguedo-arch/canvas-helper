<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l06-gap"><h2>The electrical signal reaches a gap</h2><p>An action potential can travel along one continuous axon membrane. At a <strong>chemical synapse</strong>, however, the sending and receiving cells have separate membranes. The action potential does not simply leap across the fluid-filled gap. The sending neuron converts its arriving electrical event into release of a chemical messenger.</p><p>The sending side is <strong>presynaptic</strong>; the receiving side is <strong>postsynaptic</strong>. The junction is the synapse, and the gap itself is the <strong>synaptic cleft</strong>. The messenger is a <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="neurotransmitter" data-bio-term-route="lesson-06" type="button">neurotransmitter</button></strong>. A synapse from a motor neuron to a muscle fibre is a <strong>neuromuscular junction</strong>.</p><p>Think of three connected events: an electrical signal arrives, a chemical messenger crosses the cleft, and the receiving cell's activity changes. The cells do not share one action potential. A new one can be generated in the receiving cell if the conditions for it are met.</p></section>

<p>Begin at the axon entering the top of panel A. The large arrow follows the arriving action potential toward the terminal. The terminal boundary separates the presynaptic cytoplasm from the cleft. Small messenger dots cross the gap; the vesicles that held them do not travel into the next cell.</p><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>The action potential depolarizes the terminal.</strong> This voltage change opens voltage-gated calcium channels there. <section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l06-acetylcholine"><h2>The messenger and the enzyme have different jobs</h2><p><strong>Acetylcholine (ACh)</strong> is a neurotransmitter. At the skeletal neuromuscular junction, it activates receptors that allow a depolarizing membrane response and normally trigger muscle excitation. <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="cholinesterase" data-bio-term-route="lesson-06" type="button">Cholinesterase</button></strong>—specifically acetylcholinesterase at this junction—is an enzyme that breaks down ACh. It helps end the message.</p><p>These are not two opposing neurotransmitters. One carries a signal; the other catalyzes its breakdown. For ACh, breakdown products can be taken up and reused. For transmitters such as norepinephrine and dopamine, reuptake of the transmitter itself is an important removal mechanism. Do not assume that every transmitter is cleared in exactly the same way.</p><h3>Worked example: an impulse arrives but no message is released</h3><p>Suppose the action potential reaches a terminal normally, but its voltage-gated calcium channels cannot open. Predict what happens next.</p><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Keep the successful event.</strong> Conduction along the axon occurred; there is no evidence of a failure there.</li><li><strong>Locate the blocked event.</strong> Without the normal Ca²⁺ entry, the usual trigger for vesicle fusion is missing.</li><li><strong>Follow the consequence across the gap.</strong> Much less, or no evoked, transmitter is released, so the postsynaptic receptors do not receive the usual chemical signal.</li><li><strong>Limit the conclusion.</strong> The receiving cell might still respond to a different input. This result concerns communication through the affected terminal.</li></ol></section>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l06-excitation"><h2>Inputs can move a neuron toward or away from firing</h2><p>An <strong>excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)</strong> makes an action potential more likely. In a common example, channels allow a net inward movement of positive charge, producing a small depolarization toward threshold. An <strong>inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)</strong> makes firing less likely. In the simple examples used here, K⁺ leaves or Cl⁻ enters, making the inside more negative.</p><p>The effect depends on the receiving membrane and its receptors. “Excitatory” and “inhibitory” describe effects at a particular synapse. They are not permanent labels that explain every action of a transmitter throughout the body. Inhibition can also work without a large visible hyperpolarization, by reducing the effect of excitatory current.</p>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l06-summation"><h2>Summation asks what the inputs do together</h2><p><strong>Spatial summation</strong> combines inputs from different synapses close enough in time for their effects to overlap. <strong>Temporal summation</strong> combines repeated input from a synapse before the earlier effects have faded. In each case, the net effect at the trigger region determines whether threshold is reached.</p>

<p>On the left, several input routes converge: that spatial arrangement represents different presynaptic sources. On the right, closely repeated inputs represent a timing effect. The converging arrows do not mean that action potentials merge into a larger spike. Postsynaptic effects combine; the resulting axonal action potential remains all-or-none.</p><h3>Worked model: include inhibition in the calculation</h3><p>Use a simplified additive model at the trigger region. Resting voltage is −70 mV, threshold is −55 mV, and simultaneous contributions are +8 mV, +9 mV, and −5 mV.</p><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Add all contributions.</strong> +8 + 9 − 5 = +12 mV. Leaving out the inhibitory term would overestimate the net depolarization.</li><li><strong>Apply the net change to the starting value.</strong> −70 + 12 = −58 mV.</li><li><strong>Compare with threshold.</strong> −58 is still below −55, so no action potential is initiated in this model.</li><li><strong>Test the changed condition.</strong> Removing the −5 mV input would give a predicted depolarization to −53 mV, which crosses threshold and triggers an action potential. −53 mV is the model's threshold-crossing prediction, not the voltage at which the neuron would remain afterward.</li></ol><p>Real synapses interact in more complex ways than simple addition. The calculation is a model for reasoning about combined effects, timing, and threshold; it is not a universal equation for a real neuron.</p>
