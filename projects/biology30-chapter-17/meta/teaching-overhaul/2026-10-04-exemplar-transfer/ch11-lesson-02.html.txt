<h2>How can one cell reach a distant muscle?</h2>
<p>A nerve signal may need to travel from your spinal cord toward a muscle in your foot. A chain of round, identical cells would not explain the long fibres inside a nerve. Neurons have specialized shapes that let one cell receive many inputs and send information over a considerable distance.</p>
<p>Start with a typical <strong>multipolar neuron</strong>: it has many dendrites and one axon. <strong>Dendrites</strong> are branching receiving regions. The <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="cell body" data-bio-term-route="lesson-02" type="button">cell body</button></strong>, or soma, contains the nucleus and organelles and maintains the cell's metabolism. The <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="axon" data-bio-term-route="lesson-02" type="button">axon</button></strong> carries action potentials toward its terminal branches. An <strong>action potential</strong> is a brief electrical change across a neuron’s membrane. Regenerating that change along the axon carries a nerve signal. For now, connect this signal with the continuous axon; Lessons 4 and 5 will explain the ion movements that produce it. An <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="axon terminal" data-bio-term-route="lesson-02" type="button">axon terminal</button></strong> passes a signal to another cell at a junction called a synapse.</p>

<p>Look first at the large yellow cell body on the left. Trace one short branch outward: that is a dendrite. Now follow the long yellow extension to the right under the blue coverings. That continuous extension is the axon; the blue coverings are insulation around it, not separate neurons. The large horizontal arrow means the usual direction of information flow through this model.</p>
<p>The narrowed region where the axon begins is labelled the <strong>axon hillock</strong>. Signals arriving at different receiving regions can combine and influence whether an action potential begins near the start of the axon. The cell body does not consciously choose. Its membranes respond to the combined electrical effects of incoming signals.</p>
<p>Finish at the terminal branches. Their branching allows the neuron to communicate with more than one target. The next cell is separated from a chemical synaptic terminal by a tiny <strong>synaptic cleft</strong>. The electrical signal along an axon and the chemical signal across that cleft are different parts of the pathway.</p>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l02-worked">
<h2>Use structure to explain function</h2>
<p><strong>Question:</strong> Why does a neuron benefit from having many short dendritic branches and one long axon? A useful answer must explain what the shape makes possible.</p>
<ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Begin with reception.</strong> Many dendritic branches provide a large receiving surface. This allows many synaptic contacts rather than just one input.</li><li><strong>Connect reception to integration.</strong> Inputs influence the cell body and the region where the axon begins. Combining those effects allows the outgoing signal to depend on more than one incoming message.</li><li><strong>Connect length to distance.</strong> A long axon allows an action potential to reach a distant target while staying on one continuous cell membrane.</li><li><strong>Account for the ending.</strong> Terminal branches provide sites where the neuron can release chemical messengers to its target cells.</li></ol>
<p>The conclusion is a structure–function explanation: branching supports receiving and distributing information; the long extension supports conduction over distance. Simply listing the four part names would leave that reasoning out.</p>

<h2>Insulation changes how the impulse travels</h2>
<p>The <strong>myelin sheath</strong> is a layered, fatty covering around many axons. It reduces electrical leakage through the covered membrane and helps a voltage change spread farther and faster along the axon. Gaps in the sheath are <strong>nodes of Ranvier</strong>. At these exposed regions, the membrane regenerates the action potential.</p>
<p>In the PNS, a <strong>Schwann cell</strong> wraps a segment of one axon to form myelin. In the CNS, <strong>oligodendrocytes</strong> form myelin. The glial-cell inset in the neuron image includes an oligodendrocyte; it is not a claim that Schwann cells make CNS myelin.</p>

</a>
<figcaption>Compare where the impulse is regenerated. The arrows are a shorthand: the axon remains continuous beneath the sheath. Schwann cells shown here form peripheral myelin; CNS myelin is made by oligodendrocytes.</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>Compare the two panels from left to right. In the unmyelinated axon, neighbouring membrane regions regenerate the signal along the route. In the myelinated axon, the glowing gaps mark nodes where regeneration occurs. The curved arrows summarize movement of the signal between nodes. They do not show an ion jumping through the air or crossing a break in the axon.</p>
<p>This pattern is <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="saltatory conduction" data-bio-term-route="lesson-02" type="button">saltatory conduction</button></strong>. The word “jumping” describes where action potentials are regenerated. The underlying axon remains continuous, and local electrical current still travels through the regions under myelin. With other relevant conditions similar, myelin increases conduction speed. It does not make each action potential taller.</p>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l02-damage"><h2>Predicting the effect of damage</h2><p>Suppose an axon is intact but part of its myelin is damaged. First identify what has been lost: insulation. More current can leak across the affected membrane. The next region may reach threshold—the level of electrical change needed to regenerate an action potential—later, or may fail to reach it. The result can be delayed or blocked signalling. The possible body effect depends on the pathway: a sensory pathway carries information toward the CNS; a motor pathway contributes to movement.</p><p><strong>Multiple sclerosis (MS)</strong> involves immune-mediated damage in the CNS, including damage to myelin and sometimes axons. Depending on the affected pathways, symptoms may include altered sensation, visual problems, weakness, fatigue, or difficulties with coordination. The course differs among people. Myelin damage explains a mechanism for disrupted communication; it does not let us diagnose a person from a single symptom.</p><p>Do not use myelin alone to predict whether an injured neuron can regenerate. Recovery depends on the location and nature of the injury and its cellular environment. We will also distinguish grey and white matter later; grey matter is more than simply “neurons without myelin.”</p></section>
