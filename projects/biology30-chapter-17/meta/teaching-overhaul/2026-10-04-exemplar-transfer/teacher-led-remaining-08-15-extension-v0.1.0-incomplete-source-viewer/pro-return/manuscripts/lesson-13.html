Recombination frequency and chromosome maps

How can offspring counts estimate relative gene positions?

Learning goal

Calculate recombination frequency, construct simple maps and distinguish map distance from a universal physical base-pair distance.

Key terms

Words for this lesson

Use these definitions when you need help with a term.

recombination frequencyThe fraction of scored offspring or gametes that are recombinant for the loci considered.

map unitA unit of genetic map distance corresponding approximately to one percent recombination over short intervals.

linkage mapA map of relative gene order and distances estimated from recombination data.

double crossoverTwo exchange events within a chromosome region.

Turn a recombinant count into a comparable frequency

The fictional hair-and-nose cross gave 6 white/large, 2 white/small, 2 blonde/large and 6 blonde/small offspring. Its HN/hn phase identifies the two mixed appearances as recombinant. That record tells us more than “some exchange occurred”: it lets us state what fraction of the scored offspring inherited a recombinant combination.

A count alone cannot compare samples of different sizes. Four recombinant offspring out of sixteen is a different proportion from four out of one thousand. We need to divide by all the offspring that had an opportunity to belong to the recombinant group.

Recombination frequency is the fraction of scored gametes or offspring that are recombinant for the loci being followed. In a test cross, each offspring identifies one gamete contribution from the heterozygote under the stated model. Add both reciprocal recombinant classes in the numerator, and use all scored offspring in the denominator. Multiply the resulting fraction by 100% to express it as a percentage.

Recombination frequency = (number in recombinant class 1 + number in recombinant class 2) ÷ total number of scored offspring × 100%. A reciprocal pair consists of the two complementary new arrangements, such as Hn and hN from HN/hn. Both count as recombinant, even if their observed numbers differ.

Work through the fictional cross without losing a class

First classify using HN/hn, not by choosing a convenient numerator. White/small offspring are Hhnn and reveal Hn; blonde/large offspring are hhNn and reveal hN. Their total is 2 + 2 = 4 recombinant offspring. The denominator is 6 + 2 + 2 + 6 = 16, including the twelve parental offspring.

The recombinant fraction is 4/16 = 0.25. Multiplying by 100% gives 25%. This says that one quarter of the scored offspring inherited a recombinant combination for the hair and nose loci. It does not say that 25% of all genes changed, that 25% of every chromosome is exchanged, or that exactly 25% of meiotic cells had a crossover.

Using just 2 in the numerator would omit the other recombinant class and halve the result. Dividing 4 by the twelve parental offspring would answer “recombinants per parental offspring,” not “fraction of all offspring that are recombinant.” Keep the biological question attached to the fraction.

Twenty-five per cent is the observed value for this small fictional sample. Repeating a cross can give a different observed percentage. A sample estimate is useful evidence about the underlying recombination probability, with more uncertainty when few offspring are counted.

Why a frequency can estimate genetic distance

For two loci on the same chromosome, an exchange between them can produce a recombinant contribution. Under comparable biological conditions, a longer interval generally allows more opportunities for such exchange than a shorter interval. Geneticists use that relationship to build a linkage map: a model of relative gene order and separation inferred from inheritance data.

The chromosome drawing below makes the interval idea visible before we assign a unit. Find the three aligned coloured loci on the two homologues. Compare the small gap between H/h and d/D with the larger gap between B/b and H/h.

Compare intervals on one homologue, then match corresponding loci on the other. The colours identify loci; the drawing gives no numerical scale.

The symbols d and D still describe the same locus despite their different case and order across the pair. The shorter H-to-d interval suggests less opportunity for a separating crossover than the longer B-to-H interval. The picture does not let us read off a percentage or a number of DNA bases; offspring data are needed for a genetic estimate.

One map unit, also called one centimorgan (cM), corresponds approximately to 1% recombination over a short interval. In the simple classroom approximation, the fictional 25% estimate is reported as about 25 cM. That direct percentage-to-cM conversion assumes that undetected multiple exchanges are negligible; its accuracy worsens as intervals become longer. Keep it as an approximate genetic distance, especially for a value as large as 25%, rather than claiming an exact physical separation.

Read positions and intervals as different quantities

A short-interval chromosome mapView larger

The schematic positions are 0, 8 and 20 cM. The middle locus is 8 cM from the left locus and 12 cM from the right locus; the total map interval is 20 cM. Constructed short-interval teaching example — not physical base-pair distances.

The circled A, B and C mark the loci at 0, 8 and 20 cM. Circled D identifies the blue interval from 0 to 8, whose length is 8 − 0 = 8 cM. Circled E identifies the orange interval from 8 to 20, whose length is 20 − 8 = 12 cM. The full outside interval is 8 + 12 = 20 cM, which also equals 20 − 0.

Zero is a chosen reference position, not a statement that no DNA exists before that locus. Reversing the drawing would preserve the pairwise separations. These are genetic-map distances; the line is not a photograph of a chromosome or a calibrated base-pair ruler.

Try the fraction before interpreting its unit

Use your own notes or paper. A hypothetical test cross with established phase RS/rs gives 118 RS, 122 rs, 14 Rs and 6 rS contributions. Identify the parental and recombinant classes, calculate the observed recombination frequency, and give a short-interval map estimate. Explain why the unequal counts 14 and 6 both belong in the numerator.

Optional hint: name the counted groups

The slash supplies the original arrangements. Total the two other classes first, then add all four classes for the denominator. Convert the decimal fraction to a percentage before attaching cM as an approximate interpretation.

Compare the calculation and interpretation

RS and rs are parental; Rs and rS are recombinant. Recombinants total 14 + 6 = 20 and all offspring total 118 + 122 + 14 + 6 = 260. The fraction is 20/260 ≈ 0.0769, so the frequency is about 7.69%. Under the short-interval approximation, the distance is about 7.7 cM.

Both smaller classes differ from the original arrangements. Their different sample sizes do not make the larger one parental or permit dropping the smaller one. Check that your response uses 260, not 240, as its denominator and distinguishes the decimal 0.0769 from the percentage 7.69%.

Ask whether the distances determine one gene order

A distance between two loci says how far apart to place them on a genetic map. It does not automatically tell us which side of a third locus they occupy. A useful map must satisfy every supplied pairwise distance at the same time.

Add a second marker to the fictional example

Keep the fictional hair locus H/h and nose locus N/n from the previous cross. Introduce an eye-colour locus: EE and Ee give brown eyes, while ee gives blue eyes in this stipulated model. An EN/en heterozygote is crossed with en/en. The offspring are 7 EeNn, 1 Eenn, 1 eeNn and 7 eenn. The N/n large/small nose rule is unchanged.

The tester contributes en. EeNn and eenn therefore reveal the original EN and en arrangements; Eenn and eeNn reveal En and eN. The recombinant total is 1 + 1 = 2 out of 7 + 1 + 1 + 7 = 16, giving 2/16 × 100% = 12.5%. In the simple mapping model, use an approximate E–N distance of 12.5 cM along with the earlier H–N estimate of 25 cM. Both estimates come from small fictional samples, so do not treat their neat values as precise experimental measurements.

Draw both possibilities before choosing one

Place H at 0 and N at 25 on a tentative line. E must be 12.5 cM from N, but that allows a position of 25 − 12.5 = 12.5 or 25 + 12.5 = 37.5. One position puts E between H and N. The other puts E beyond N. We need the H–E comparison to distinguish them in this additive model.

Two pairwise distances sharing a locus can leave more than one order possible. An additional comparison between the two remaining loci can distinguish the alternatives. With real longer intervals, raw two-locus frequencies need not add exactly because some exchanges are hidden; infer an additive map only when its assumptions are appropriate. Do not invent a third measurement or force inconsistent data into a neat line.

When three consistent distances do settle the order

Consider a separate simple map in which P–Q = 3 cM, Q–R = 7 cM and P–R = 10 cM. The longest distance is P–R. The two shorter distances sum to it: 3 + 7 = 10. Their shared locus Q lies between P and R, giving P — 3 — Q — 7 — R.

Check all three distances, not just the sum. P is 3 from Q, Q is 7 from R, and travelling from P through Q to R covers 10. If P were between Q and R instead, Q–R would be 3 + 10 = 13, contradicting the supplied 7. If R were in the middle, P–Q would be 10 + 7 = 17, contradicting 3. The correct middle position is the one that satisfies all the comparisons.

A reversed map, R — 7 — Q — 3 — P, is equivalent. These data do not identify which end of the real chromosome should be printed on the left. Relative gene order and interval lengths are the justified result.

Practise identifying missing evidence in your own notes or on paper. Only J–K = 9 cM and J–L = 4 cM are known. Draw two possible relative orders, label their adjacent intervals, and state what K–L separation each would predict. Explain which additional comparison is needed. Treat these as additive map distances.

Compare the alternative maps

Put J at 0 and K at 9. L can be at 4, between them, giving J — 4 — L — 5 — K and K–L = 5 cM. Or L can be at −4 on the other side of J, giving L — 4 — J — 9 — K and K–L = 13 cM. A K–L comparison distinguishes these possibilities. A negative coordinate here is merely a location on the other side of the chosen zero; no interval length is negative.

Check both known distances in each drawing. Giving only the 13-cM possibility assumes J is in the middle without evidence. Giving only the 5-cM possibility assumes both other loci lie on the same side of J. Reversed versions of these two maps are equally acceptable.

Recognise what a two-locus record cannot see

The method counts combinations at the markers we score. It does not observe every exchange along a chromosome. That difference limits how directly we can turn a two-locus percentage into a long-distance map.

Follow two exchanges through three loci

Begin with homologues in phase ABC/abc, with loci in the order A, B, C. After copying, there are two ABC chromatids and two abc chromatids. Follow just one ABC chromatid and one nonsister abc chromatid through a double crossover, meaning two exchanges in this region. In this example the same two chromatids participate in both exchanges; the other two remain unchanged.

Read the table from top to bottom. The vertical bars mark the interval where the next exchange occurs; they are not chromosome breaks that remain in the final products. At each exchange the corresponding segment to the right is swapped between the two participating chromatids.

A double crossover can restore the outside-marker combination
StageFirst participating chromatidSecond participating chromatidWhat changes?
Starting arrangementsA | B Ca | b cFirst exchange will be between A and B
After first exchangeA b | ca B | CProducts are Abc and aBC; second exchange will be between B and C
After second exchangeA b Ca B cProducts are AbC and aBc

After the first exchange, the tracked outside combinations are Ac and aC, so A/C scoring would recognise them as recombinant. After the second, the outside combinations are AC and ac again, matching the original outside-marker combinations. The middle allele still shows the difference: AbC differs from ABC, and aBc differs from abc.

Thus an offspring receiving AbC could be classified as parental if we inspected only A and C, even though its chromosome had participated in two exchanges. Scoring B as well can reveal information the two outside markers miss. This is a concrete example of what double crossovers can do; not every possible two-crossover arrangement involves the same chromatids or restores the same marker combination.

Undetected multiple crossovers make a direct two-locus percentage underestimate longer genetic distances. As the association between distant markers becomes effectively random, the expected recombinant fraction approaches the independent value of 50% in the standard model. It is not an unbounded percentage ruler for distance.

Derive the 50% expectation and distinguish a sample

For independent assortment, AB, Ab, aB and ab each have probability ¼. If the original phase is AB/ab, the two recombinant combinations are Ab and aB, giving ¼ + ¼ = ½ = 50%. If the phase is Ab/aB, the two other combinations are recombinant and the same total results. Increasing separation cannot make the alleles more independent than independent in this standard model.

This is a limit for the expected biological two-locus recombination fraction, not an absolute restriction on every finite observed sample. If phase is fixed by known parental chromosomes, a sample could contain 11 recombinant offspring out of 20: 11/20 = 55%. Random sampling can put an observed proportion on either side of an underlying 50% expectation. The observation is not automatically impossible, and it does not define a 55-cM distance.

For an unexpectedly high sample value, check the phase assignment, arithmetic, scoring and model assumptions, and consider sample size. A large, reproducible excess above 50% would call for further investigation of those assumptions; it is not a general rule identifying genes on different chromosomes. Loci on different chromosomes and sufficiently separated loci on one chromosome can both give approximately 50% recombinants.

A linkage map can nevertheless span more than 50 cM. For example, six successive estimated 12-cM intervals span 6 × 12 = 72 cM. Adding information from shorter intervals preserves exchanges that an outside-marker comparison can miss. The 72-cM map span does not predict 72% recombinant offspring for its two endpoints; additive map distance and the observed two-locus fraction are different quantities.

A centimorgan is not a fixed number of bases

A map unit measures genetic separation through recombination behaviour. A base pair measures DNA sequence length. Recombination rates vary across chromosome regions, organisms and biological conditions, so there is no universal conversion from one cM to one fixed number of base pairs.

Imagine two stretches of the same physical DNA length in a hypothetical organism. If exchange is more frequent in one region, its markers can have a larger genetic distance than markers spanning the same base-pair length in the other. Conversely, two intervals with equal cM values need not contain equal numbers of bases. A linkage map estimates inheritance relationships; a physical sequence map answers a different measurement question.

Use the limits to evaluate a report

Write an explanation in your own notes or on paper. A report combines several short intervals into a 64-cM map. An observer says, “That is impossible because recombination stops at 50%; the map must also contain exactly 64 million base pairs.” Identify the two different mistakes and state what additional information would be needed for a physical length.

Compare the two corrections

The 50% standard-model limit concerns the expected recombinant fraction for a pair of outside markers. A map assembled from shorter intervals can exceed 50 cM, so a 64-cM span is not ruled out. Multiple exchanges can restore parental outside-marker combinations and make a direct endpoint comparison smaller than the mapped span.

The base-pair claim also fails. The relation between exchange frequency and physical length varies, so 64 cM cannot universally be converted to 64 million base pairs. A physical sequence map or an appropriate measured region-specific relationship would be needed. A complete response separates sample frequency, additive genetic distance and physical DNA length, rather than treating all three as interchangeable numbers.

Worked example

Build a three-locus map from an offspring investigation

Use a supplied teaching model of flies. At the eye-colour locus, E is completely dominant to e: EE and Ee give red eyes and ee gives purple eyes. At the wing-size locus, L is completely dominant to l: LL and Ll give large wings and ll gives vestigial wings. The model uses a heterozygote with phase EL/el crossed with the double-recessive tester el/el, with reliable expression, unbiased scoring and no class selectively lost.

Before reading the record, derive the comparison. Independent assortment would give EL, El, eL and el at ¼ each. The tester always contributes el. The four offspring classes would therefore each have probability ¼, or an expected 25 of each among 100. The table shows the actual supplied record in this teaching example.

EL and el match the given homologues; El and eL differ from them. The parental classes total 80 and the recombinant classes total 10 + 10 = 20. All four classes total 40 + 40 + 10 + 10 = 100. The observed recombination frequency is therefore 20/100 × 100% = 20%, supporting linkage under the model. Its simple map estimate is about 20 cM; the estimate is subject to sampling and possible undetected multiple exchanges.

Now add the supplied comparisons with a third locus for body colour. In this teaching model B specifies black rather than grey body colour. The eye/body map estimate is 14 cM, and the body/wing map estimate is 6 cM. We now have all three pairwise comparisons, not just two.

  • Find the longest comparison: eye to wing is 20 cM.

  • Test a middle locus: eye to body plus body to wing is 14 + 6 = 20 cM. Body colour therefore fits between the eye and wing loci in the simple additive map.

  • Label adjacent intervals: eye — 14 cM — body — 6 cM — wing. Writing 20 on the eye/body interval would confuse the full span with its first part.

  • Check every given: eye/body is 14, body/wing is 6, and eye/wing is 14 + 6 = 20. The reversed order expresses the same relationships.

The conclusion is an internally consistent approximate linkage map for the stated teaching data. It does not establish exact chromosome coordinates, a DNA sequence length, or a universal dominance rule for every fly strain. A real investigation would assess the counts, uncertainties and mapping assumptions more closely.

Interpret a fresh record with the phase reversed

Work independently in your own notes or on paper. A hypothetical organism has linked loci M/m and N/n. The heterozygote is Mn/mN, and the tester is mn/mn. Scored offspring reveal 138 Mn, 132 mN, 18 MN and 12 mn contributions. Identify the recombinant classes, calculate their frequency and a short-interval distance estimate, and reconstruct the diploid genotype of an offspring that received MN. Finally, explain whether replacing this record with a sample near 50% recombinants would prove that the loci occupy different chromosomes.

Compare the complete reasoning

Mn and mN match the original homologues and are parental. MN and mn are recombinant despite grouping capital letters or lowercase letters together. Recombinants total 18 + 12 = 30; all offspring total 138 + 132 + 18 + 12 = 300. Thus 30/300 = 0.10 = 10%, suggesting about 10 cM under the short-interval approximation.

An MN gamete meeting the tester's mn gives MmNn. MN is the contribution, not the full offspring genotype. A result near 50% would be consistent with effectively independent marker combinations, but could arise from different chromosomes or widely separated loci on the same chromosome. It would not prove which location applies.

Check that your response uses phase before counts, includes both recombinant classes, divides by all 300 offspring, converts the decimal correctly, reconstructs both diploid allele pairs, and keeps the location conclusion appropriately limited.

Prepare to read a different linked-gene notation

The optional supplied-data activity on printed page 602 uses another notation in Part B. There, a plus sign (+) represents the wild-type reference allele at the position where it appears. A label such as pr names the purple-eye allele and vg the vestigial-wing allele. A two-letter abbreviation can name one allele; pr is not two different gene contributions. “Wild type” identifies the reference form in this notation, not an assertion that every wild individual is genetically identical or that the allele must always be dominant.

For the stated recessive-marker model, pr vg / + + places pr with vg on one homologue and the corresponding wild-type alleles together on the other. Its parental gametes are pr vg and + +. The new arrangements pr + and + vg are recombinant. Each + must be read at its own locus: the + after pr is the wild-type wing allele, while the + before vg is the wild-type eye allele.

Read a tester such as pr vg / pr vg in the same way as an ordinary double-recessive tester: it contributes the same marker combination each time. A slash or stacked line preserves phase, which a compact two-locus genotype can hide. That is an advantage; having to recognise which locus each + belongs to is a notation cost. Use this reasoning to evaluate the notation rather than deciding from unfamiliar appearance alone. Part B's leg-length marker d follows the same positional convention.

Explain a no-recombinant result without overclaiming

Zero observed recombinants means none were detected in that sample for those markers. Very low recombination between close loci can produce a sample with none, especially when the sample is small. That does not prove a permanent inability to recombine. A changed biological condition that suppresses exchange can also change the outcome.

For the optional fly analysis, use this additional biological information: male Drosophila melanogaster normally do not undergo meiotic crossing over. If a marker-cross description does not state which sex is heterozygous, exchanging the parental sex roles can therefore matter. A heterozygous male can transmit the original linked arrangements without generating the crossover combinations seen from a heterozygous female.

Compare possible explanations with the whole record. If an earlier large sample from the same marker pair contained many recombinants, a later similarly large sample with none would be extremely surprising as ordinary sampling variation alone. “The loci must have become very close” does not explain a changed result from the same loci. Investigate the parent roles and other changed conditions, as well as recording or scoring errors, before drawing a new map. This keeps a plausible generic explanation separate from an explanation that fits both experiments.

Move from an experimental cross to human inheritance evidence

The chromosome mechanism also makes linkage analysis possible in humans, but investigators cannot arrange human test crosses as breeding experiments. They study consenting families' inherited marker combinations and existing biological samples with appropriate privacy and ethical safeguards. Families are smaller, generations are longer and not every family supplies an informative phase or genotype. The principle of tracing recombinant combinations remains useful, while the practical evidence and responsibilities differ.

The opening question is now answered: test-cross counts identify recombinant contributions; their fraction estimates genetic separation over suitably short intervals; several consistent distances can establish a relative gene order. Every step depends on what was actually measured. In the next lesson, that distinction between a genetic finding and the conclusions or decisions it supports becomes important when considering genetic testing.

Part B: Using Linked Gene Notation In order to describe linked genes, fruit ﬂ y geneticists developed a different notation for genes. The normal allele—the allele that is nearly always found in natural populations—is called the wild type allele. The wild type allele is represented with a plus sign (+). The mutant allele for a gene is represented with lower-case italic letters. Linked genes are represented with a horizontal line or a slash.

For example, the gene for purple eyes (pr) and the gene for vestigial wings (vg) are on chromosome number 2. The homozygous recessive genotype is represented as prvg _ prvg or pr vg / pr vg to indicate that these genes are on the same chromosome. The homozygous dominant genotype is represented as + + _
