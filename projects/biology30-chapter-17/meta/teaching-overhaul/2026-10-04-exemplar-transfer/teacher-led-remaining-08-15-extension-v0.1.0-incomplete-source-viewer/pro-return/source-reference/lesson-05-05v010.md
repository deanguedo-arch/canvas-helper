# Two-trait crosses and independent assortment

## Can two characteristics travel together?

Mendel had followed one characteristic at a time through generations of pea plants. His next question was whether the inheritance of one characteristic influenced another. If a plant passed on an **allele** associated with tall growth, would a green-pod allele be more likely to travel with it? To investigate that relationship, he needed to follow both characteristics in the same plants.

Look first at the two contrasts below. Compare the height of the plant on the left with the smaller plant beside it. Then look at the pods in the upper-right inset: green on the left and yellow on the right. Height and pod colour are separate characteristics, even though one plant has both.

![A tall pea plant on the left and a short plant beside it, with a separate upper-right inset showing a green pod on the left and a yellow pod on the right.](../assets/teaching-l05-v010/slide-35-image32.png)

Compare plant height separately from pod colour. These contrasting forms give us two characteristics to follow in the offspring.

We can name four possible combinations of these appearances: tall with green pods, tall with yellow pods, short with green pods, and short with yellow pods. Each combination describes a **phenotype**, the observed characteristics. The picture alone does not tell us which combinations a particular cross will produce or how frequent they will be. For that, we need to know the parents and follow their allele contributions.

### Choose the starting plants and define the letters

The experiment begins with a true-breeding line of tall plants with green pods and a true-breeding line of short plants with yellow pods. **True breeding** means that repeated breeding within the line produces the same forms of the studied characteristics under the stated conditions. That breeding history matters. Seeing one tall plant with green pods would not, by itself, tell us all of its alleles.

The drawing below shows the chosen parents. Read the left plant's height and its enlarged pod together, then do the same for the right plant. The outlined boxes and connecting lines enlarge the pods so their colours can be compared; they do not show offspring.

![The chosen P plants: a tall plant with green pods on the left crossed with a short plant with yellow pods on the right. Boxed pod details enlarge the colour contrast.](../assets/teaching-l05-v010/slide-36-image29.png)

The starting cross uses tall, green-pod and short, yellow-pod breeding lines. Their known breeding history supplies information beyond the appearances in the picture.

Use **T** and **t** for the height gene's alleles, and **G** and **g** for the pod-colour gene's alleles. An allele is a version of a gene. A **locus** is that gene's position on a chromosome. Different letters keep the two loci separate; uppercase and lowercase forms of the same letter identify versions at one locus.

Read each locus separately in the table. Complete dominance means that the heterozygote, with two different alleles, has the same phenotype as the homozygote with two dominant alleles: Tt is tall like TT, and Gg has green pods like GG. Dominant describes that phenotype relationship. It does not mean that an allele is better, more common, or favoured when gametes form.

In this simple model, the true-breeding parents are **TTGG** and **ttgg**. Each is **homozygous** at both studied loci: its two height alleles match, and its two pod-colour alleles match. The four-letter **genotype** records two allele pairs. It does not mean that a plant has four versions of one gene.

These starting plants are the **P generation**, the parental generation. Write their cross as **TTGG × ttgg**. The cross sign connects the two parents; it is not an instruction to multiply letters. A controlled cross uses pollen from the chosen donor so an unintended plant does not change the parentage. Pollen can deliver a sperm cell to an egg in the receiving flower. Fertilisation joins those two gametes, so both chosen parents contribute genetic information.

### Follow the P contributions into F1

A gamete, such as an egg or sperm, carries one chromosome set and contributes **one allele at each studied locus**. The TTGG parent can contribute T at the height locus and G at the pod-colour locus. Its gamete label is therefore **TG**. The ttgg parent can contribute only t and g, giving **tg**. A two-locus gamete label has one letter for each locus, whereas a diploid plant's genotype records a pair at each.

Use the arrows in the next image to follow one height letter and one pod-colour letter from each parent. Then inspect the lists beneath them. The repeated TG labels on the left all name the same distinct gamete type. The repeated tg labels on the right also name just one type.

![Combination arrows around TTGG give TG, TG, TG, TG. Arrows around ttgg give tg, tg, tg, tg. Each repeated list contains only one distinct gamete type.](../assets/teaching-l05-v010/slide-37-image46.png)

Different choices between identical allele copies repeat the same gamete label: TTGG contributes TG and ttgg contributes tg.

There are many physical gametes, but only one distinct allele combination from each parent at these two loci. TG has probability 1 from TTGG, and tg has probability 1 from ttgg. Joining them restores a pair at each locus: T with t gives Tt, and G with g gives Gg. Every available combination is therefore **TtGg**.

The resulting plants are the **F1 generation**, the offspring of the P cross. Look at the expanded display below. Read a TG label across the top and a tg label down the side, then follow them to their shared cell. Repeat that reading at another position and notice why the genotype does not change.

![An expanded P-cross grid repeats TG in all four columns and tg in all four rows. Every cell is TtGg. Its annotation identifies all F1 offspring as heterozygous at both loci, tall with green pods.](../assets/teaching-l05-v010/slide-37-image37.png)

The repeated cells show the same F1 result. One TG-by-tg cell would include the only distinct contribution pair in this starting cross.

The sixteen drawn cells do not represent sixteen different genotypes or a requirement to grow sixteen offspring. Repeating identical row and column labels has expanded the display without adding a new possibility. With each parental contribution certain, the one distinct pairing has probability **1 × 1 = 1**.

All F1 plants are **heterozygous** at both loci: Tt at height and Gg at pod colour. Apply the two phenotype rules only after forming those pairs. Tt makes them tall, and Gg gives them green pods. A plant heterozygous at two studied loci is a **dihybrid**; crossing two such plants is a **dihybrid cross**.

### Follow F1 reproduction before explaining F2

The next generation comes from the F1 plants, not another cross of the original P plants. Mendel allowed F1 plants to self-pollinate, using their own pollen. Fertilisation still joins an egg and sperm. Since an F1 plant is TtGg, the allele cross is **TtGg × TtGg**. Crossing two TtGg F1 plants gives the same pair of parental genotypes under this model. The offspring belong to the **F2 generation**.

Now examine the F2 diagram. Its whole grid and ratio are visible. Begin with the legend on the right: oval outlines mark tall plants, rectangular outlines mark short plants, and green or yellow outlines identify pod colour. Use those two features together to find the four phenotype groups before trying to explain the number of cells in each group.

![The complete TtGg by TtGg F2 grid has TG, Tg, tG and tg on both edges. F2 includes four phenotype combinations. The diagram gives their 9:3:3:1 model expectation; it is not a photograph or a counted set of sixteen plants.

The tall/green and short/yellow appearances of the original parents are present, but F2 also includes **tall/yellow** and **short/green**. Those combinations were absent from the selected P parents. The height and pod-colour forms have been brought together in new ways.

This is the pattern we need to explain. How can an F1 plant supply the combinations that produce these offspring, and why does the diagram predict unequal phenotype frequencies? Mendel used breeding patterns to infer that the allele contributions for these characteristics assorted independently. We will use chromosome movement to explain what that means, then return to the grid and account for its probabilities.

## Two chromosome pairs can orient independently

Start inside an F1 plant with genotype TtGg. For this model, place the height locus on one chromosome pair and the pod-colour locus on a different pair. The two members of a homologous pair carry corresponding kinds of genes: one height homologue carries T and the other t; one pod-colour homologue carries G and the other g. These are ordinary autosomal loci, on chromosomes other than sex chromosomes.

DNA copying occurs before meiosis. Each copied chromosome has two sister chromatids joined together, so an X in the diagram is one copied chromosome, not the whole homologous pair. Normal copying increases physical copies of the tracked loci; it does not create additional allele versions. We still follow T and t at one locus and G and g at the other.

At **metaphase I**, homologous pairs line up before separating. The direction faced by one pair does not determine the direction faced by another pair. The image below lets us compare that difference. Start with the two top cells labelled Possibility 1 and Possibility 2. Keep the long red/blue pair in view, then notice that the short pair reverses its arrangement.

![Two alternative orientations of long and short homologous chromosome pairs. The long pair retains its arrangement while the short pair reverses. Homologues separate into the middle cells, labelled Metaphase II; sisters then separate, producing four labelled chromosome-combination types across the two alternatives.](../assets/teaching-l05-v010/slide-41-image44.png)

Compare the two top orientations, then trace the long and short chromosomes into the lower cells. The two possibilities represent alternatives across meioses, not consecutive stages of one meiosis.

Follow Possibility 1 down to the middle cells. One cell receives the two blue homologues and the other receives the two red homologues. Under Possibility 2, each middle cell instead receives one blue and one red homologue. The colours distinguish the chromosome origins in this schematic. They do not mean dominant and recessive, and the image does not print T, t, G or g on the chromosomes.

The first separation is **meiosis I**: homologues move apart while the sisters of each copied chromosome remain joined. The middle row is labelled Metaphase II because the chromosomes are positioned for the second division. There is no new round of DNA copying between divisions. In **meiosis II**, the sisters separate, leaving one long and one short chromosome in each bottom cell. The repeated bottom cells within a combination group repeat a chromosome combination; they do not add another distinct type.

Connect that movement to the pea alleles. A chromosome carrying T can travel with the chromosome carrying G or with the chromosome carrying g. The t-bearing chromosome also has either pod-colour partner available across the alternative orientations. One meiosis follows one arrangement; it need not produce all four combination types. The four types describe the possibilities across many meioses.

In flowering plants, meiosis first produces haploid spores, with one chromosome set. Later stages maintain that haploid set and produce the structures that form eggs and sperm. The bottom labels summarize the allele-combination consequences, rather than drawing that entire plant life cycle. The contribution we need for a cross remains one allele at each locus in a gamete.

**Independent assortment** means that the allele contribution at one studied locus does not determine the contribution at the other under this model. It explains how the two combinations present in the P lines can give rise to other combinations. We have described assortment within a parent. The meeting of gametes at fertilisation is a separate event.

### Derive the gamete types and their weights

Ordinary equal segregation in a TtGg parent gives T or t with probability ½, and G or g with probability ½. A **probability** is the chance of a specified outcome under the stated conditions. Independent assortment means that receiving T does not change the ½ chance of G or the ½ chance of g.

To make a TG gamete, both the T contribution and the G contribution must occur in that *one* gamete. The **product rule** multiplies probabilities for such specified independent requirements. Here **P(TG) = ½ × ½ = ¼**, where P(TG) means “the probability of a TG gamete.” This multiplication concerns two loci within one parent's contribution, before any fertilisation.

Read the next table one row at a time. Keep one height allele, pair it with one pod-colour allele, and attach the probability justified by those two choices. A **gamete combination** is that set of one allele from each studied locus.

### Combine the F1 gametes at fertilisation

We can now return to **TtGg × TtGg** and explain the F2 grid. Both sides of this cross have the same four gamete types and weights, whether they represent self-pollination of one TtGg plant or a cross between two TtGg plants. We assume **random fertilisation**: which egg contribution occurs does not bias which sperm contribution joins it. This assumption lets us multiply the two gamete probabilities. It is distinct from independent assortment, which established the combinations within each contribution.

Put parent 1's gametes down the left edge and parent 2's across the top, as in the readable table below. Each edge label is a whole gamete with one allele per locus. Each interior cell combines a row gamete with a column gamete to give a possible diploid offspring genotype. The named parent roles could be transposed if the lists and weights moved with them.

For the **TG row and tg column**, join T with t at the height locus, giving Tt. Join G with g at the pod-colour locus, giving Gg. Write the offspring as **TtGg**, keeping each locus's pair together. Both parental contributions must occur, so this cell has probability **¼ × ¼ = 1/16**. The same weighting applies to every cell here.

There are sixteen contribution pairs, but only nine distinct genotypes. Locate **TtGg** in four positions: TG with tg, Tg with tG, tG with Tg, and tg with TG. These are different routes to the same allele pairs. Their probabilities add to **4 × 1/16 = 4/16**. Counting the genotype name only once would overlook three possible fertilisations. Counting all sixteen cell weights gives **16 × 1/16 = 1**.

### Group genotypes by what the plant expresses

To turn that genotype table into phenotypes, apply the complete-dominance rules separately. **T_** means TT or Tt, so it identifies the tall group without specifying the second height allele. **G_** means GG or Gg for green pods. Each underscore stands for the remaining allele at its *own* locus: T or t in T_, G or g in G_. It cannot stand for a letter from the other gene.

The nine genotype counts, in the order listed, are **1:2:2:4:1:2:1:2:1**. Their sum is sixteen. The phenotype grouping combines them into **9 tall/green : 3 tall/yellow : 3 short/green : 1 short/yellow**. That explains the ovals, rectangles and coloured outlines in the earlier F2 diagram. Four phenotype groups have not turned nine genotypes into four genotypes.

Homozygosity answers a different question from appearance. For example, TTGg is homozygous at the height locus but heterozygous at the pod-colour locus. TtGG reverses those genotype descriptions, yet both plants are tall with green pods. To decide whether a plant is homozygous at a locus, compare its two alleles there. To decide its phenotype, apply the expression rule. A dominant phenotype cannot do both jobs.

The four phenotype categories are **mutually exclusive**: one scored plant cannot be both tall/green and tall/yellow, for example. They also include every possible phenotype in this model. Their probabilities therefore pass a useful check: **9/16 + 3/16 + 3/16 + 1/16 = 1**.

### Choose the probability operation for the biological event

We can check the phenotype results using the single-locus crosses. Tt × Tt gives TT at ¼, Tt at ½ and tt at ¼, so tall has probability ¾ and short ¼. Gg × Gg similarly gives green pods at ¾ and yellow pods at ¼. Under the independent model, a tall, green-pod offspring must meet both independent requirements: **¾ × ¾ = 9/16**. The product concerns two characteristics of the *same offspring*.

The other joint probabilities follow in the same way: tall/yellow is **¾ × ¼ = 3/16**; short/green is **¼ × ¾ = 3/16**; short/yellow is **¼ × ¼ = 1/16**. Each product agrees with the corresponding group in the full grid. Independent assortment and random fertilisation justify the combinations; the phenotype rules determine which genotypes count.

Add probabilities when joining alternatives that cannot both describe the same outcome. All tall offspring belong to either the tall/green or tall/yellow category, so their combined probability is **9/16 + 3/16 = 12/16 = ¾**. The denominator still includes all offspring. If two descriptions overlap, first separate them into categories that do not overlap, so the same offspring is counted once. The words “and” or “or” alone do not justify an operation; the relationship between the events does.

To compare these genotype predictions with observed phenotype counts, we also assume that each genotype expresses the assigned phenotype reliably. **Penetrance** concerns whether an associated phenotype is expressed; complete penetrance means it is expressed in every individual carrying the relevant genotype under the stated conditions. We assume no genotype or phenotype group is selectively lost before scoring. Those conditions are part of why the predicted allele combinations can be read as phenotype proportions.

The 9:3:3:1 ratio is an expectation for this particular heterozygote cross under these assumptions. It does not schedule the next sixteen offspring, and an earlier tall plant does not make the next offspring owe the cross a short plant. Finite samples can differ by chance. Different parents will change the gametes and their weights, even if two characteristics are still being followed. The next cross shows why deriving the contributions is more useful than carrying a memorized ratio into every problem.

Worked example

## A tomato cross with four gamete types from one parent and two from the other

Now switch from peas to tomatoes. We are following **fruit colour and plant height**, so define the letters for this new problem. R represents the allele associated with red fruit and r the allele associated with yellow fruit. T and t represent the height alleles. In the stated complete-dominance model, RR and Rr plants produce red fruit, rr plants produce yellow fruit, TT and Tt plants are tall, and tt plants are short. The loci assort independently, fertilisation is random, and the phenotype rules apply reliably to the offspring being scored.

A grower crosses a plant that is **heterozygous for both traits** with one that is **heterozygous for height and homozygous recessive for fruit colour**. What are the parental genotypes and phenotypes? What proportions of offspring will be tall with red fruit, tall with yellow fruit, short with red fruit, and short with yellow fruit?

### Translate the words before drawing the grid

Heterozygous means that the two alleles at a locus differ. For parent 1, that gives Rr at fruit colour and Tt at height: **RrTt**. Parent 2 has two recessive colour alleles, rr, and different height alleles, Tt: **rrTt**. Record each phenotype by applying the colour and height rules to those pairs.

### Let the distinct gametes determine the dimensions

Parent 1 contributes R or r at probability ½ each, and T or t at probability ½ each. Independent assortment gives **RT, Rt, rT and rt**, each with probability **½ × ½ = ¼**. Parent 2 contributes r with certainty at the colour locus. Its height contribution is T or t at ½ each, so its distinct gametes are **rT and rt**, each with probability **1 × ½ = ½**.

### Combine one row and one column in every cell

Put the RrTt parent's gametes down the left and the rrTt parent's across the top. Every row has weight ¼ and every column weight ½. Random fertilisation gives each pairing probability **¼ × ½ = ⅛**. Equal cell weights follow from those probabilities, rather than from the grid's appearance.

Read the RT row first. With column rT, R joins r and T joins T, producing **RrTT**, red and tall. With column rt, R joins r and T joins t, producing **RrTt**, also red and tall. The genotype changed even though the phenotype did not. Continue by the same locus-by-locus operation in the remaining rows.
