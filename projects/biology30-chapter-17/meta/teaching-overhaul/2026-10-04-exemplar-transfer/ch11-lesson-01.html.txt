<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l01-purpose">
<h2>A cold hand gives us a starting point</h2>
<p>You step outside without gloves. Your skin cools. A moment later, you pull your hands into your sleeves. Your body has detected a change, processed information about it, and produced a response. This chapter explains how those three jobs happen, from the whole nervous system down to the membrane of a single cell.</p>
<p>Start with this question: <strong>How can a change at one place in your body produce a useful response somewhere else?</strong> We will first follow the information, then examine the cells carrying it, and finally return to the brain and the systems they form.</p>

<figcaption>Follow the arrows from sensory input to integration to motor output. The arrows show information flow; the dashed boundary locates processing in the CNS. The boxes describe jobs, not three individual cells.</figcaption>
</figure>
<p>Look at the middle box first. <strong>Integration</strong> means processing incoming information and organizing a response. The system cannot choose an appropriate response from an outgoing signal alone: information must first arrive. That is why <strong>sensory input</strong> comes before integration and <strong>motor output</strong> follows it.</p>
</section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l01-roles">
<h2>Separate the change, the detector, and the response</h2>
<p>A <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="stimulus" data-bio-term-route="lesson-01" type="button">stimulus</button></strong> is a change that can be detected. A <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="receptor" data-bio-term-route="lesson-01" type="button">receptor</button></strong> detects it and starts sensory signalling. An <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="effector" data-bio-term-route="lesson-01" type="button">effector</button></strong>, usually a muscle or gland, carries out the response. These names refer to different jobs. Cold is a stimulus; a temperature-sensitive receptor is a detector; contracting muscle is part of a response.</p>
<p>In the cold-hand example, receptors provide information about cooling. Processing in the CNS contributes to your decision to cover your hands. Motor signals reach skeletal muscles, which move your arms. The muscles do not need to understand the weather: they respond to the signals reaching them.</p>
<h3>Worked example: why pulling on a sleeve is not just “a message from the brain”</h3>
<ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Identify what changed.</strong> Skin temperature fell. We start with the stimulus because the response needs a cause.</li><li><strong>Identify how the change becomes information.</strong> Temperature-sensitive receptors change their signalling. Without detection, the CNS would not receive this particular information.</li><li><strong>Locate processing.</strong> The CNS combines the sensory information with other information, including your awareness of being cold and your available choices.</li><li><strong>Follow output to an effector.</strong> Motor neurons carry signals to skeletal muscles. Their contraction moves the sleeves over your hands.</li><li><strong>Explain the result.</strong> Covering the hands reduces further heat loss. A complete answer connects the detected change to what the response accomplishes.</li></ol>
<p>A reflex can use a much shorter processing route. Some other responses regulate organs without a conscious decision. Keep the three jobs in the model; do not assume that every response requires conscious thought.</p>
</section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l01-homeostasis">
<h2>Why communication matters for homeostasis</h2>
<p>Your cells work within a limited range of conditions. <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="homeostasis" data-bio-term-route="lesson-01" type="button">Homeostasis</button></strong> is the maintenance of relatively stable internal conditions while the environment and your activities change. “Relatively stable” allows small fluctuations. It does not mean that temperature, heart rate, and every other variable stay at one exact value.</p>
<p>Consider cooling of the body, rather than just a decision about clothing. Temperature information reaches control centres that can increase heat conservation and heat production. Reduced blood flow near the skin reduces heat loss; shivering produces heat through muscle activity. These responses oppose the original fall in body temperature. As the disturbance decreases, the need for a strong corrective response also decreases. This is the reasoning behind <strong>negative feedback</strong>.</p>
<p>Nervous and endocrine communication help coordinate these adjustments. Neurons carry rapid electrical changes along their membranes and usually communicate chemically at junctions. Endocrine glands release <strong>hormones</strong> into blood; a hormone affects cells with suitable receptors. Nervous responses are generally rapid and targeted, while many hormonal effects develop more slowly and last longer. The two systems work together, and those timing descriptions are general patterns.</p>
<p>Not every nervous response is a negative-feedback loop. Choosing to wave to someone is nervous communication, but it does not necessarily correct a disturbed internal variable. When you call something homeostatic, identify the condition being regulated and explain how the response helps regulate it.</p>
<p>For warming, connect the response to heat loss: sweat glands release sweat onto the skin, and evaporation removes heat from the body. The gland is the effector, releasing sweat is its activity, and cooling is the effect that can oppose a temperature rise. Use those distinctions in the next example.</p></section>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l01-organization">
<h2>Place those jobs in the nervous system</h2>
<p>The <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="central nervous system" data-bio-term-route="lesson-01" type="button">central nervous system (CNS)</button></strong> consists of the brain and spinal cord. The <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="peripheral nervous system" data-bio-term-route="lesson-01" type="button">peripheral nervous system (PNS)</button></strong> connects them to the rest of the body. Sensory pathways carry information toward the CNS; motor pathways carry signals away to effectors. “Motor” means output, including output to glands and internal organs.</p>
<div aria-label="Scrollable comparison table" class="pro-table-wrap" role="region" tabindex="0"><table><thead><tr><th>Division</th><th>Structures or connections</th><th>Job and example</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>CNS</td><td>Brain and spinal cord</td><td>Processes information and organizes responses; includes spinal reflex circuits.</td></tr><tr><td>PNS</td><td>Nerves outside the brain and spinal cord</td><td>Connects receptors and effectors with the CNS.</td></tr><tr><td>Somatic system</td><td>Somatic sensory pathways and motor pathways to skeletal muscle</td><td>Provides body information and controls skeletal muscles, including voluntary movements and reflexes.</td></tr><tr><td>Autonomic system</td><td>Motor pathways to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands</td><td>Regulates activities such as heart rate and digestion without a conscious command.</td></tr><tr><td>Sympathetic division</td><td>A division of autonomic output</td><td>Helps prepare the body for action; heart rate can increase while digestion slows.</td></tr><tr><td>Parasympathetic division</td><td>A division of autonomic output</td><td>Supports functions such as digestion and slowing the heart during recovery.</td></tr></tbody></table>
</div><p>You will use these divisions in detail later. For now, notice the nesting: sympathetic and parasympathetic are parts of autonomic control; they are not alternative names for the CNS and PNS.</p>
<h3>A neuron is a cell; a nerve is a bundle</h3>
<p>A <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="neuron" data-bio-term-route="lesson-01" type="button">neuron</button></strong> receives, conducts, and passes information. Many long nerve fibres are bundled with supporting tissue to make a <strong>nerve</strong>, much as many separate strands can share one cable. A single nerve can contain fibres carrying information in different directions. <strong>Glial cells</strong> support the working environment of neurons, provide nourishment and protection, and, in some cases, form insulation. They are active support cells, not spare neurons.</p>
