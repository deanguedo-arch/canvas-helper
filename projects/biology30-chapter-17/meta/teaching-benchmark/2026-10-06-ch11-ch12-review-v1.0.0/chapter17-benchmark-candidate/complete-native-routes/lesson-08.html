Why did white eyes return in the sons?

Thomas Hunt Morgan crossed red-eyed female fruit flies from a true-breeding line with a white-eyed male. Their first-generation offspring had red eyes. When red-eyed offspring from that generation reproduced, white eyes appeared again, but in the male offspring. The white-eye version had not vanished in the first generation. The new question is why its visible return depended on which sex chromosome an offspring received.

The allele-contribution method still applies: identify the parents, follow their gametes, combine contributions at fertilisation, and interpret the offspring. Now we must keep the chromosome attached to the allele. That extra information can explain a pattern that a square containing only R and r would hide.

What do X and Y mean?

Sex chromosomes are chromosomes involved in the chromosome system associated with sex development. In the typical XX/XY inheritance model used here, an egg-producing female has two X chromosomes and a sperm-producing male has one X and one Y. We will call the XX offspring daughters and the XY offspring sons in human examples. These are the categories of this specified classroom model, not a complete account of every variation in human sex development.

The letters X and Y name chromosome types. An X chromosome is not named for whether it happens to look X-shaped in a drawing. Any copied chromosome drawn as an X has two joined sister chromatids; the name X chromosome and the shape of a copied chromosome are different ideas. The other chromosomes are autosomes. A typical human somatic cell contains 22 pairs of autosomes as well as the sex chromosomes.

A locus is a gene's position on a chromosome. A contributes information used to make a functional product, and an is a version of that gene. A gene located on X is . Sex-linked is the broader description: it can include a gene on X or on Y. The fruit-fly eye-colour locus we are following is on X, with no corresponding allele at that locus on Y. This qualification matters because X and Y are not identical chromosomes carrying matching versions of every gene.

One copy is different from two identical copies

An XX individual has two copies of this X-linked locus, one on each X. It can have two matching alleles or two different alleles. An XY individual has just one copy at this locus, on its X. It is : there is one copy to consider, rather than a pair. Hemizygous is not another spelling of homozygous. A homozygote has two matching copies; a hemizygote has only one in this chromosome context.

For the fly model, let XR mean an X chromosome carrying the red-eye allele, and Xr mean an X carrying the white-eye allele. The small raised R or r is a superscript, an allele label attached to the chromosome symbol. It is not an exponent to calculate. XRXr names two chromosomes and their two allele versions. XrY names one allele-bearing X and a Y. Do not write Yr: that would place this allele at a locus the Y does not have. Plain-text questions may write the same genotypes as XRXr and XrY.

In the stated complete-dominance model, XRXR and XRXr females have red eyes; XrXr females have white eyes. XRY males have red eyes and XrY males have white eyes. The white-eye allele is recessive when compared with R in an XX heterozygote. Its expression in XrY does not make it dominant: there is no second allele at this locus for that comparison.

Derive the first generation before the second

The true-breeding red-eyed female is XRXR; the white-eyed male is XrY. During ordinary meiosis, chromosome copies are separated into haploid gametes. Each egg receives one maternal X, so this female supplies XR with certainty. The male's X and Y separate into different gamete contributions; across sperm, Xr and Y each have probability ½ under the model. Meiosis separates contributions within a parent. Fertilisation joins an egg and a sperm from the two parents.

Read the maternal column first: every offspring receives R. Then compare the paternal contributions. Daughters receive the father's r-bearing X, while sons receive his Y. Both categories are red-eyed, but their genotypes differ. The daughters retain r and can transmit it. No white-eyed offspring is predicted in this first generation under these stated parents.

Now cross an F1 daughter with an F1 son: XRXr × XRY. The mother can supply XR or Xr at ½ each. The father supplies XR or Y at ½ each. A daughter always receives his XR, but a son receiving Y expresses whichever eye-colour allele arrived on the mother's X. The returning white-eye phenotype is therefore possible in sons. The next part will account for every contribution pair and its probability using a human X-linked example with the same inheritance structure.

This is evidence connecting a particular inherited characteristic to a particular chromosome. The chromosome theory of inheritance links genes and their transmission to chromosomes. The more specific gene-chromosome account places different genes at particular loci along chromosomes. Naming the chromosome helps explain transmission; learning the order and spacing of several loci requires the additional evidence used in chromosome mapping.

Keep the chromosome, the phenotype and the denominator separate

One common form of red–green colour-vision deficiency can be modelled as . This example concerns that specified form, not every cause of altered colour vision. We are using a classroom inheritance model, not a colour-vision test or a way to determine a real person's genotype from a screen image.

Let XB carry the allele associated with the usual red–green colour discrimination in the model, and Xb carry the recessive colour-vision-deficiency allele. In this lesson, affected means expressing that particular phenotype. It does not make a judgement about a person. The B/b labels now refer to this human example, not the R/r eye-colour alleles of fruit flies.

Some practice questions use A/a instead of B/b for the same kind of recessive model: XA is the unaffected-associated version and Xa is the recessive phenotype-associated version. In plain text, XAXa means XAXa, and XaY means XaY. Define the allele meanings for the particular question; changing the label does not change the chromosome-contribution method.

Read the expression rules before using a square

Start on the left of the image, where two X symbols appear in each genotype. Compare XBXb with XbXb. Then look on the right, where each genotype has only one X-linked allele. The missing second copy, rather than a change in the definition of dominance, explains why one b allele can be expressed in an XY individual.

A in this simple recessive model has the recessive allele without the recessive phenotype: XBXb. Such an individual is , with different versions on the two X chromosomes. XbY is affected, not an unaffected carrier in this model, because its one copy is b. XbXb is also affected. We initially assume the genotype-to-phenotype rules apply reliably. Actual expression can be more complicated, which is why we state the model rather than applying this table as a diagnosis.

From parental chromosomes to four weighted combinations

Consider a carrier mother and an unaffected father: XBXb × XBY. During gamete formation, the two maternal X chromosomes separate, so the mother supplies XB or Xb, each with probability ½. The father supplies XB or Y, each at ½. No egg or sperm in this ordinary model carries the parent's entire two-chromosome combination.

We assume ordinary equal segregation, equal X-bearing and Y-bearing sperm contributions, and random fertilisation. Random fertilisation means that which maternal X occurs does not favour one paternal contribution. Therefore a specified egg-and-sperm pairing has ½ × ½ = ¼. To interpret those probabilities as observed phenotype proportions, we also assume reliable expression and no selective loss or selective sampling of a category before counting.

The table puts eggs down the left and sperm across the top. Every edge label is a gamete contribution. Every interior cell is an offspring after fertilisation. Read the row and column labels together before interpreting its phenotype.

In the lower-left cell, b comes from the mother and B from the father. Writing the pair with B first does not reverse its parentage. In the lower-right cell, the same maternal b meets the paternal Y. That offspring has only b at the tracked locus, so it is affected. Both columns together account for ¼ + ¼ + ¼ + ¼ = 1.

Which offspring belong in the counted group?

A joint probability concerns two requirements together, such as being a son and affected. With all offspring in the denominator, only the lower-right cell meets both requirements: ¼, or 25%. An affected child also has probability ¼ in this particular cross, because no daughter is affected. Those two descriptions happen to select the same cell here; they need not do so for different parents.

A is calculated within a group already specified. “What fraction of sons is affected?” restricts us to the Y column. The two son outcomes have equal weights, and one is affected, so the answer is ½, or 50% of sons. In probability notation, P(event) means “probability of that event.” We can check the changed denominator explicitly: P(affected given son) = P(affected and son) ÷ P(son) = ¼ ÷ ½ = ½.

Do not multiply by another ½ when the question already says the child is a son. That would put daughters back into the denominator. Likewise, do not use the conditional ½ as the fraction of all offspring who are affected sons. Read “a child who is an affected son” as two requirements and “a son who is affected” in context: if the son category is already given, the question is conditional. State the denominator in words when the wording is ambiguous.

Among daughters, neither of the two entries is affected, so the affected fraction is 0. Half of daughters are carriers, but carrier daughters are ¼ of all offspring. A carrier result describes an inherited allele without the recessive phenotype; it must not be counted as an affected result.

Match an expected count to that denominator

Imagine a collection of 200 offspring records from parental combinations with these same genotypes and assumptions. This is a hypothetical set of records, not the size of one family or an observed dataset. An expected number multiplies a category's probability by the size of the counted group. The expected number of affected sons is 200 × ¼ = 50. The expected number of sons is 200 × ½ = 100, and 100 × ½ = 50 checks the calculation.

For a different collection of 200 sons, use the conditional probability instead: 200 × ½ = 100 affected sons. The groups are different even though the number 200 appears in both descriptions. The four quarter-weight categories of the all-offspring collection have expected counts 50, 50, 50 and 50, totalling 200. They are expectations, not a promise that any particular set will contain those exact counts. Independent offspring outcomes do not compensate for earlier outcomes to complete a ratio.

The sample already excludes sons. Half of daughters are XBXb, so the expected carrier count is 80 × ½ = 40. Using ¼ incorrectly applies an all-offspring probability to a daughters-only sample. Neither daughter genotype is XbXb, so the affected-daughter expectation is 0 under these parents. Chromosome transmission and gene expression answer different questions

Trace the paternal contribution in either square. A daughter receives the father's X and a son receives his Y in the typical model. Therefore an X-linked allele does not travel directly from father to son along that chromosome path. A father and son can nevertheless share an X-linked phenotype if the son receives the relevant allele from his mother. Similar appearances alone do not identify the transmitted chromosome.

The chromosome path is the same for recessive and dominant X-linked alleles. What changes is the expression rule after the offspring genotype has formed. A fully penetrant model means that the associated phenotype is expressed in every individual with the specified genotype under the stated conditions. It is an assumption about expression, not a different way for chromosomes to segregate.

Change the expression rule deliberately

For an explicitly hypothetical X-linked dominant trait, let XD carry the phenotype-associated allele and Xd the alternative. In this model, XDXD, XDXd and XDY express the trait; XdXd and XdY do not. An XDXd mother is affected, rather than an unaffected recessive carrier. The meaning of a heterozygote depends on the expression rule.

An affected XDY father and unaffected XdXd mother make daughters XDXd and sons XdY. Every daughter inherits the father's D-bearing X and expresses the trait under complete penetrance; no son receives that X. Half of all offspring are expected to be affected daughters with the equal-sex-category assumption. An affected heterozygous mother, by contrast, can pass XD to either a daughter or a son. Do not transfer the “all daughters” prediction from an affected father to every affected parent.

A hypothetical Y-linked allele follows the opposite paternal branch: an XY father contributes his Y to each son and not to daughters. For a locus found only on Y, an XX daughter does not acquire it from her father and cannot pass that paternal Y to her own sons. This is a chromosome-location rule. It is not evidence that a particular real trait in a practice scenario is known to be Y-linked.

His X-bearing sperm carries d. The mother supplies D or d with equal probability, whichever paternal chromosome meets her egg.

Compare the complete solution

Eggs are XD and Xd, each ½. Sperm are Xd and Y, each ½. The four quarter-weight genotypes are XDXd (affected daughter), XdXd (unaffected daughter), XDY (affected son) and XdY (unaffected son). Each sex category is half affected, and the all-offspring affected probability is ¼ + ¼ = ½. Not every daughter is affected because the mother can contribute either X. Explain this maternal choice rather than reusing the affected-father rule.

A patchwork coat reveals expression in different cell lineages

Look at the cat's orange and black fur. In a typical XX cat heterozygous for the X-linked orange/non-orange coat-colour locus, the two X chromosomes carry different versions. Why does the adult coat contain patches instead of every cell necessarily using both X chromosomes in the same way?

In typical XX mammalian somatic cells, one X becomes largely inactive early in development. Somatic cells are the body cells rather than the reproductive cells transmitting chromosomes. The mostly inactive X is compacted into a structure called a Barr body. Inactivation reduces the expression of many X-linked genes from that chromosome; it does not remove its DNA from the cell. Some genes escape inactivation, so “the entire chromosome is completely switched off in every cell” is too absolute.

In the usual random-inactivation account, different early cells may inactivate different X chromosomes. Once a cell has made that choice, its descendants generally maintain the same inactive X. As those descendant cells divide and form regions of skin, one region mainly uses the orange-associated version and another the non-orange version. In a cat whose other pigmentation genes support black fur, those regions give orange and black patches. The coat is a mosaic: populations of cells differ in which X is active.

Read the photo from one orange patch to an adjacent black patch. The boundary illustrates neighbouring cell populations with different expression histories; it is not a boundary between two new species of cell or proof that the allele sequence changed at the edge. The white muzzle and paws are not explained by a third choice of active X. Other genes influence white spotting. The photograph itself does not establish this particular cat's chromosome complement.

Such orange/black mosaics are usually seen in XX cats because two different X-linked allele versions are needed for the simple explanation. Rare male tortoiseshell or calico cats can occur through chromosome or other genetic exceptions. “Usually” is therefore more accurate than “only females.” This also shows why a simplified rule about an unaffected XX carrier is an initial expression model, rather than a promise that every heterozygote has identical expression in every tissue.

Explain the patch without changing the DNA

In your own notes, explain why an orange patch and a black patch in a hypothetical heterozygous XX cat can have the same two inherited allele versions. A classmate proposes that each cell randomly chooses a new X to silence every time it divides. Would that account produce the same explanation for stable patches?

Compare your explanation

Both patches can retain the same two X-linked versions in their DNA. Their early ancestor cells inactivated different X chromosomes, and descendants maintained those choices. That creates groups with different active versions and therefore different pigment outcomes. Randomly choosing anew at every division is not the maintained-lineage mechanism described here; it would not explain the same stable clonal regions in that way. A good explanation separates inherited genotype from expression, places the initial choice early in development, and connects persistence through cell divisions to patches.

Worked example

Change the father and rebuild the prediction

Return to the recessive colour-vision model, with B and b defined as before. A carrier XBXb mother now has an affected XbY partner. What offspring genotypes and phenotypes are possible? Compare affected offspring, affected sons among all offspring, and affected offspring among sons. The father has changed, so the earlier square cannot simply be relabelled with its old answers.

1. Derive the gametes from the given parents

The mother's genotype is unchanged. Her eggs carry XB or Xb with probability ½ each. The father's sperm carry Xb or Y with probability ½ each. His X-bearing contribution now contains b instead of B. His Y-bearing contribution has not acquired an eye-vision allele. The assumptions remain equal segregation, random fertilisation, equal X/Y sperm contributions, reliable phenotype expression and no differential loss before scoring.

2. Combine the chromosomes before deciding the phenotype

Put the eggs down the left again. Compare the X-bearing sperm column with the previous square. It is the column for daughters that changes; the Y column retains the same two maternal possibilities.

XBXb × XbY: each offspring pairing has probability ¼
Egg ↓ / sperm →Xb, ½Y, ½
XB, ½XBXb: unaffected carrier daughterXBY: unaffected son
Xb, ½XbXb: affected daughterXbY: affected son

The lower-left daughter receives b from both parents and expresses the recessive phenotype. The upper-left daughter receives maternal B with paternal b and is an unaffected carrier under the model. Both daughters inherit the affected father's b allele, but they do not both have the same phenotype. Inheriting an allele and expressing a recessive phenotype are different claims.

3. Group the right cells and check the totals

All four genotypes are distinct and have probability ¼. There are two affected categories, an affected daughter and an affected son, which are mutually exclusive. Add their probabilities: ¼ + ¼ = ½ affected offspring. The two unaffected categories also total ½, checking that the phenotype groups account for all offspring.

Only one cell is both a son and affected, so P(affected son among all offspring) = ¼. Within sons, one of the two equally weighted cells is affected, so P(affected given son) = ¼ ÷ ½ = ½. Among daughters the affected fraction is also ½, while carrier daughters occupy the other half. In this cross, “affected child” and “affected son” no longer have the same all-offspring probability because an affected daughter is now possible.

For a hypothetical set of 120 offspring records, expect 120 × ¼ = 30 in each of the four genotype-and-sex categories, totalling 120. Affected offspring total 30 + 30 = 60; affected sons alone total 30. If the question instead supplies 120 sons, expect 120 × ½ = 60 affected sons. The changed father has altered the daughters' expression outcomes but has not changed which X-linked allele sons receive from their mother.

4. Use a changed mother to test the mechanism

Use the red/white fruit-fly model and work in your own notes. Compare two crosses: a homozygous red-eyed female with a white-eyed male, and a white-eyed female with a red-eyed male. Derive the distinct gamete contributions, predict each offspring sex category, and decide whether reversing the parents preserves the result. Explain the answer using chromosomes, not a rule that offspring resemble the parent of the same sex.

Hint: there is only one distinct egg type in each cross

Each female is homozygous at the tracked locus. Write the allele on that one distinct X-bearing egg type. Then combine it once with the father's X-bearing sperm and once with his Y-bearing sperm.

Compare the two crosses

For XRXR × XrY, every egg is XR. The sperm are Xr or Y, each ½. Daughters are XRXr, red, and sons are XRY, red. Each category has probability ½, so all offspring are red in the model.

For XrXr × XRY, every egg is Xr. Sperm are XR or Y, each ½. Daughters are XRXr, red, and sons are XrY, white. All sons are white, but white sons are half of all offspring. The two ½ categories total 1. The reciprocal crosses differ because sons receive their only eye-colour X from the mother. The second cross does not say that a son's eye colour is determined by his father's eye colour.

This is a rehearsal of the chromosome-contribution method. Check that your work retains X and Y, separates the two maternal genotypes, gives weights to each sperm type and distinguishes an all-offspring fraction from a sons-only fraction.

5. Work backward from offspring evidence

A breeder has a red-eyed female of unknown genotype and a red-eyed male. Their correctly recorded offspring include a white-eyed son. Under the stated fly model, identify the mother's genotype and the allele source in that son. Then choose a mating involving an available white-eyed male that could produce a white-eyed daughter. State what a finite sample without white-eyed offspring would fail to prove. Explain your reasoning in your own notes before opening the comparison.

Compare the inference and the next cross

The son is XrY. His Y came from the father, so his Xr came from the mother. Her red eyes require R in the stated model, and the observed r contribution forces her genotype to be XRXr. This does not depend on how many red-eyed offspring occurred before him.

A white-eyed daughter needs Xr from both parents. Crossing that heterozygous female with an XrY male permits XrXr daughters. Her r-bearing egg has probability ½ and his r-bearing sperm probability ½, giving ¼ white-eyed daughters among all offspring, or ½ among daughters. The other quarter-weight outcomes are a red-eyed carrier daughter, a red-eyed son and a white-eyed son. Their probabilities total 1.

If an unknown red-eyed female produced only red-eyed offspring in a finite sample with a suitable male, that alone would not prove she lacked r: a heterozygote can happen to transmit R repeatedly. A positive white-eyed-son observation establishes a maternal r contribution under the model; absence of that outcome in a small sample does not establish its impossibility. A strong response identifies the forced allele, justifies the parental genotype, supplies the two contributions needed for a white-eyed daughter, and states the evidence limit.

Answer the opening question

White eyes could return in the F2 sons because an F1 female retained r and could pass its X chromosome to a son who received Y from his father. The chromosomes explain which alleles are available; the expression rule explains what is seen; and the question determines which offspring belong in the denominator. Keep those three jobs separate when reading the supported questions and the lesson check.

If you choose the chromosome-mapping questions at the start of the optional reading on printed page 601, return to those calculations after studying linked genes and chromosome maps. They require the relationship between parental combinations, recombination and map distance. The sex-linked questions can already be approached by tracing the contributions developed here.

Consider XBXb × XBY. The four equally likely combinations are XBXB, XBXb, XBY and XbY. One of four children is expected to be affected. Among sons only, one of two is expected to be affected. The probability of “a son who is affected” is 1/4 among all children. The probability that “the next son is affected” is 1/2 conditional on being a son.
