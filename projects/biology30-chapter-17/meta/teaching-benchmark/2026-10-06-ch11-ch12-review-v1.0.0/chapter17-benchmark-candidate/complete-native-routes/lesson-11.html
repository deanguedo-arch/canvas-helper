Sex-linked pedigrees and testing a model

How can you test an inheritance explanation against an entire family?

Learning goal

Use chromosome transmission to evaluate X-linked models and distinguish consistency from proof in small pedigrees.

Before you begin

In the typical XX/XY model, fathers pass X to daughters and Y to sons. A small family may not show every possible outcome.

Whose X chromosome could an affected son have inherited?

Look at the family below. An unaffected couple has an affected son, and an unaffected daughter in the next generation also has an affected son. The shaded individuals shown happen to be male. Is that enough to establish that the trait is X-linked, or must we ask how the allele could have reached each person?

Read the top couple first. Follow the vertical line to their four children: an open circle, a filled square, an open square and another open circle. The extra squares at the far left and right are the daughters’ partners, not additional children of the top couple. Follow those partnership lines down to the grandchildren. The diagram has no individual numbers, so the descriptions in this lesson identify people by their relationship and position.

For inheritance, the locus is on X. In the typical XX/XY model used here, an XX mother supplies an X in each egg. An XY father supplies either X or Y in a sperm. A daughter receives one X from each parent; a son receives his mother’s X and his father’s Y. The father therefore does not directly pass an X-linked to a son. An affected father and an affected son can nevertheless occur in the same family if the son receives the relevant allele from his mother.

Keep the chromosome attached to the allele symbol

For the recessive model, let XB carry the allele associated with an unaffected phenotype and Xb carry the recessive allele. Write the chromosomes in every genotype. XBXb is an unaffected heterozygous female carrier; XbXb is an affected female. XBY is an unaffected male and XbY an affected male. XBXB is an unaffected non-carrier female.

Inherited haemophilia A and B are familiar X-linked-recessive examples. Our fictional trait lets us test the same chromosome routes without inferring anyone’s medical status.

One recessive copy on the single X is enough for an XY individual to express the trait, while an XX individual needs a recessive copy on both X chromosomes in this simple model. This helps explain why X-linked-recessive traits can be more frequent among males without making them male-only.

For this locus, Y does not carry a corresponding B or b allele. Writing “bb” for an affected male would invent a second allele copy; writing “bY” without its X can conceal the chromosome route. The notation XbY in some plain-text questions means XbY. “Carrier” here describes an unaffected heterozygote; a male with XbY expresses the recessive trait under the complete-penetrance model.

Now test the diagram under a fully penetrant model, ordinary chromosome transmission, no new mutation and accurate parentage. The top couple’s affected son is XbY. His father supplied Y, so Xb came from his mother. Because she is unaffected, her must also contain XB: she is XBXb. Such a person is an obligate carrier, meaning the carrier genotype is required by the evidence under this model.

The top father is XBY. His unaffected son is XBY. The daughter on the right must be XBXb because her affected son received Xb from her, while her unaffected phenotype requires XB. Her unaffected partner is XBY. Their two unaffected daughters each received XB from him but could have received either maternal X: each remains XBXB or XBXb.

The daughter on the left also received her father’s XB, but her mother could have supplied XB or Xb. Her two unaffected sons do not settle which she carries: a carrier mother can pass XB to two sons by chance. Leave her as XBXB or XBXb. Her unaffected partner and their unaffected sons are XBY; their daughter could be XBXB or XBXb. Every shown person now has a compatible genotype or explicitly retained alternatives.

We have demonstrated that X-linked recessive inheritance fits this whole family. We have not proved it uniquely. Autosomal recessive inheritance can also fit: assign Aa to the top parents, aa to their affected son, Aa to the daughter on the right and her partner, and aa to their affected son. Compatible AA or Aa assignments can be made for the remaining unaffected people. The sex imbalance is a useful clue, but the same limited observations allow more than one model.

The chromosome route stays the same when dominance changes

The daughter always receives her father’s X in this model. Whether she expresses the associated trait depends on the allele’s relationship to the allele on her other X. Keep transmission and expression as separate steps.

In an model, let XD carry the dominant allele producing the trait and Xd the alternative. An affected father is XDY; an unaffected mother is XdXd. Every daughter receives paternal XD and maternal Xd, becoming XDXd and affected. Every son receives paternal Y and maternal Xd, becoming XdY and unaffected.

For X-linked recessive inheritance, change the phenotype rule and use B/b again. An affected father XbY with a unaffected mother XBXB has XBXb daughters and XBY sons. Each daughter is an unaffected in the simple model. The chromosome route is unchanged; the differs between dominant and recessive models.

In the dominant cross, “all daughters affected” means probability 1 within the daughter subgroup. If X- and Y-bearing sperm are equally likely, affected daughters are 1/2 of all offspring. In the recessive cross above, no offspring are affected, although half of all offspring are expected to be carrier daughters. A daughter inherits an affected father’s X in either X-linked model, so paternal X transmission by itself does not distinguish dominance.

Read the dominant family one branch at a time

In the next diagram, the stated model is X-linked dominant, and the printed numbers 1–11 run across the whole family. First examine the upper mother, individual 1. Then follow the lower partnership between 6 and 7. What does each branch tell us about an X contribution?

Individual 1 is affected, so she has XD. Her unaffected son 5 received Xd from her, forcing her other chromosome to be Xd. Thus 1 is XDXd. Father 2 is XdY. Their affected daughter 4 is XDXd, not XDXD, because her father supplies only Xd. Their sons 3 and 5 are XDY and XdY; unaffected daughter 6 is XdXd.

For 6 and affected father 7, use the first row of the comparison table. Daughters 8 and 10 are XDXd; sons 9 and 11 are XdY. If that pair has another daughter, her probability of being affected is 1 under the stated model. If they have another child without specifying sex, the probability is 1/2. An unaffected daughter of father 7 would contradict a fully penetrant X-linked-dominant model because she must receive his XD.

A greater number of affected males can suggest an X-linked-recessive candidate, while affected daughters of affected fathers can suggest an X-linked-dominant candidate. Neither a sex count nor one matching branch proves the chromosome location. Small autosomal families can show these patterns by chance. Conversely, a required allele contribution that is impossible under a model gives a specific reason to reject it.

Guided comparison: change the father, then recalculate

For the following probability calculations, assume equal segregation, random fertilisation, equal chances of X- and Y-bearing sperm, and no selective loss of a genotype group before scoring. A particular egg–sperm combination then has probability 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 when both parents have two equally likely gamete types.

The daughter probability changes from 0 to 1/2 because daughters now receive his Xb.

This changed case also shows how a female can express an X-linked-recessive trait: she receives Xb from an affected father and Xb from a mother who carries it. Under the simple model, a carrier mother can supply that allele without herself expressing the trait. Sex-linked does not mean male-only.

Check your reasoning: retain X and Y, give gamete weights, count all four offspring combinations, and change the denominator when the question specifies sons or daughters. A father and son can both be affected here, but the father did not transmit his X to that son.

Test a candidate against the entire family

A is evidence for testing a candidate model, a possible inheritance explanation. First translate that model into allele and chromosome rules. Then look for a way to assign genotypes that fits every person and parent–offspring connection. One impossible contribution rejects a simple model; one compatible assignment shows that it remains possible. Neither step requires every small family to reproduce a large-sample ratio.

Read the unnumbered diagram below. The top affected mother and unaffected father have four children along one sibling line: an affected son, an affected daughter, an unaffected son and an unaffected daughter. The affected son’s partner is at the far left; their son is affected. The unaffected daughter’s partner is at the far right; their daughter is unaffected. This is a complete fictional record for the following comparison.

For the dominant assignment, the top mother must be Dd because she has dd children; the dd father supplies d to all children. For the recessive assignment, the aa mother supplies a to all children, so unaffected children must be Aa, and the father must supply both a and A across the observed children. On the left, an aa father and an unaffected Aa mother can have an affected aa son. Thus both autosomal models survive the whole record.

Now test the sex-linked alternatives. Under X-linked recessive inheritance, the top affected mother would be XbXb. All her sons must receive Xb, so the unaffected son is a contradiction. Her affected daughter with an unaffected father is another contradiction. Under X-linked dominant inheritance, the left affected grandson would need XD from his mother, yet his mother is unaffected and therefore XdXd. His affected father contributes Y to him. This branch rules out that simple X-linked-dominant assignment even though other branches fit it.

Conclusion: among the four single-locus models tested, both autosomal models remain consistent, and both simple X-linked models are contradicted. “The trait appears in every generation” does not resolve dominance here. Nor does the affected father–son pair alone establish direct allele transmission. We used the mother’s phenotype and the chromosome rules to test that pair.

All four tests assumed complete , ordinary chromosome transmission, accurate observations and relationships, and no new mutations. Penetrance concerns the proportion of a genotype group that expresses the phenotype; it is not chromosome number. If expression is incomplete or age-dependent, an open symbol may not exclude an allele that could produce the phenotype later or in another carrier. Expressivity instead concerns how the phenotype appears or how severe it is among those expressing it.

State those limits without changing assumptions halfway through a solution. A real family may need additional records, ages at observation, molecular evidence or professional interpretation. Classroom pedigree reasoning does not establish a medical diagnosis or justify investigating classmates’ or relatives’ private information.

Before calculating a future child’s , identify the relevant parents and show how their genotypes were inferred. Then list their gametes and name the requested population: all children, daughters, sons, or individuals already known to be unaffected. Do not calculate from a parent you have only guessed, and do not convert a possible genotype into a certain one merely to make a convenient Punnett square.

Worked example

Use an impossible contribution to reject one model

Question. A fully expressed trait appears in a daughter whose father is unaffected. A student proposes X-linked recessive inheritance. Assume the typical XX/XY transmission model, complete penetrance, accurate records and no new mutation. Does that proposal fit?

  1. Translate the proposed model. Use Xb for the recessive trait allele and XB for the alternative. An affected daughter must be XbXb.
  2. Identify the two sources. A daughter receives one X from her mother and one X from her father. Therefore one required Xb must come from the father; the mother cannot ordinarily supply both of this daughter’s X chromosomes.
  3. Test the father against that requirement. An unaffected father in this model is XBY. His X-bearing sperm carry XB, not Xb. He cannot supply the required recessive X.
  4. Give the limited conclusion. The proposed fully penetrant X-linked-recessive model is inconsistent with this relationship. This is a zero-probability genotype conflict under the assumptions, not simply a surprising ratio in a small sample.
  5. Do not claim more than was tested. This relationship does not by itself prove an autosomal model. For example, an affected mother could supply an X-linked-dominant allele to a daughter of an unaffected father. We would need the mother’s phenotype and the rest of the family to test that and other candidates.

Independent application: one family, four candidates

A fictional three-person family has an affected mother, an unaffected father and an affected daughter. A later record adds an unaffected son of the same couple. No genotype measurements are supplied. Assume a single locus, complete penetrance at the observed ages, no new mutation and ordinary XX/XY transmission.

On paper, draw the family with a key. Test autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, X-linked dominant and X-linked recessive inheritance. For each surviving model, provide parental genotypes and show that both child phenotypes can be produced. For each rejected model, identify a specific impossible contribution. Then answer: would learning that the next child is another unaffected son distinguish all the surviving models?

Work on paper or in your own notes before opening the hint or explanation. This optional practice is not saved or graded by this page.

Compare after testing all four models

Autosomal dominant remains possible. The mother must be Dd because her unaffected dd son needed d from her; the father is dd. Eggs D or d meet sperm d, producing Dd affected or dd unaffected children, regardless of sex. The affected daughter is Dd and the unaffected son dd.

Autosomal recessive also remains possible. The affected mother is aa. The unaffected father must be Aa because he supplied a to the affected aa daughter and A to the unaffected Aa son. Maternal a gametes meet paternal A or a, giving 1/2 Aa and 1/2 aa among all children; either sex can occur in either genotype group.

X-linked dominant remains possible. The mother is XDXd, with Xd established by her unaffected son. The father is XdY. Maternal eggs XD and Xd pair with paternal Xd and Y, giving XDXd, XdXd, XDY and XdY, each 1/4 among all children. The observed affected daughter and unaffected son are possible.

X-linked recessive is inconsistent. An affected mother would be XbXb and an unaffected father XBY. Their daughters would be XBXb, unaffected carriers, and all sons would be XbY, affected. Both the affected daughter and unaffected son conflict with those predictions.

Another unaffected son would not distinguish the three surviving models: each allows one. Under the equal-sex-probability assumption, each of these particular parental crosses gives an unaffected son probability 1/4 among all children and 1/2 among sons. Repeated compatible outcomes can add evidence, but one more matching observation does not create a unique proof here. Further informative family relationships or appropriate genetic evidence could distinguish the models.

Marking guide: state all three surviving models, reject only X-linked recessive under these assumptions, give complete chromosome notation for sex-linked cases, identify both contradictions, and explain why the new observation does not resolve the remaining alternatives. Naming one familiar pattern without testing the other candidates is incomplete.

Return to the original question

Counting affected males and females can suggest a starting hypothesis. Following chromosomes tests it: a son’s X comes from his mother, a daughter receives an X from each parent, and dominance determines what a heterozygote expresses. An obligate-carrier conclusion is conditional on the inheritance model; a model that fits a small pedigree may remain one of several possibilities.

If you use the optional videos, pause at a parent–child connection and predict the required chromosome contribution before hearing the explanation. Afterwards, check whether the conclusion is “must be,” “could be,” or “inconsistent.” The reasoning taught here is sufficient even when a video cannot be played.

In the next lesson, the chromosome question changes again. Instead of asking whether a gene is on X or an autosome, we will ask what happens when two genes lie on the same chromosome. That relationship can change which allele combinations travel together in gametes.
