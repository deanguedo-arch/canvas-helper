Genetic testing, counselling and decisions

What can genetic information tell a person—and what can it leave uncertain?

Learning goal

Distinguish a genetic test result from a guaranteed phenotype, and identify consent, uncertainty and privacy considerations.

Before you begin

Genotype does not determine every complex phenotype on its own. Pedigree conclusions rely on assumptions and available evidence.

A test result answers a particular question

A fictional laboratory report says, “Variant V was detected.” A second sentence says, “This variant is associated with a greater chance of condition Q.” A reader concludes, “Then Q will definitely happen.” Before accepting that conclusion, notice the change in the claim. Detecting a DNA version, estimating a chance and predicting a person's future are different steps. We need evidence for each step.

A genetic test examines genetic material or related markers to answer a defined question. A variant is a difference in DNA sequence compared with a stated reference. Some tests look for a small list of known variants; others examine many positions. The test's scope is what it was designed and able to examine. A result only makes sense when we know that scope and why the test was done.

The table explains why “detected” does not automatically mean “will develop.” A test can be useful while leaving uncertainty. It may help identify a particular inherited variant or guide further investigation without predicting the timing or severity of a future condition.

Positive negative and uncertain results

A positive result means the test found the change or marker it was looking for. Its significance depends on the question: it might support a diagnosis, identify carrier status or indicate increased risk. Those outcomes should not be treated as interchangeable.

A negative result means the relevant change was not detected within the test's scope and detection limits. Imagine checking a book for spelling errors on only pages 10–12. Finding none there would not establish that every other page is error-free. In the same way, a targeted negative result cannot rule out every untested variant or every other cause of a phenotype. It can still answer the narrower question it was designed to address.

An uncertain result can occur when a variant is detected but there is insufficient evidence about its significance. Finding a difference in sequence and showing that it affects a phenotype require different evidence. Uncertainty should stay visible rather than being converted into either a definite diagnosis or a claim that nothing matters. These distinctions are explained in MedlinePlus Genetics guidance on test results.

Inheritance probability and expression probability

A Punnett square predicts allele combinations under its stated assumptions. is the chance of a specified outcome in a specified group. The chance of inheriting a variant and the chance of expressing a phenotype are not always the same outcome.

is the proportion of people with a specified genotype who express the associated phenotype under the conditions being considered. In a fully penetrant classroom model, all such individuals express it. With reduced penetrance, some do not. Age, other genetic factors and environmental conditions may affect expression. A study must therefore say which phenotype was measured and when. “No symptoms at age 20” and “never develops symptoms” are different observations.

Keep penetrance separate from the severity of expression. If all individuals with a genotype show a feature but its extent varies, that describes variation in expressivity. Neither word changes which allele a gamete contributed. They describe the relationship between genotype and the observed phenotype.

Make a risk statement precise enough to interpret

“Higher risk” gives a direction, but it does not yet give a size. In a fictional follow-up study, 12 of 200 participants with variant V developed condition Q during the stated observation period. In a comparison group without V, 4 of 200 developed Q. These numbers are invented for learning and do not describe a real condition or population.

Start by identifying what is counted. The numerator is the number who developed Q during the observation period. The denominator is the number followed in that group. For the V group, the observed proportion is 12 ÷ 200 = 0.06 = 6%. For the comparison group, it is 4 ÷ 200 = 0.02 = 2%. Neither result is a Mendelian offspring ratio. Both summarize observed groups.

Compare the same data in two ways

The ratio 6% ÷ 2% = 3 says that the observed proportion was three times as large in the V group. The difference 6% − 2% = 4 percentage points says how far apart the proportions were on the same percentage scale. “Three times” does not mean “three people in every four,” and neither calculation gives a 100% outcome.

Look across the V-present row. Most participants in that row did not develop Q during the study, despite the higher proportion compared with the second row. Now look down the Q column. Q also occurred in some people without V. Those two observations are enough to reject the statements “V guarantees Q” and “Q can occur only if V is present” for these data.

The table establishes an association: V and Q occur together more often in the observed group than in the comparison group. It does not, by itself, show that V caused the difference. The groups might also differ in age, exposures or other inherited variants. The follow-up period, sample size, recruitment method and reliability of the measurements all affect the interpretation. A genetic risk estimate applies to its stated evidence and conditions.

Use a counselling conversation to make the uncertainty usable

helps people understand inheritance information, what a test may establish, what remains uncertain and the options relevant to their situation. It can include emotional support as well as an explanation of numbers. A useful explanation separates the evidence from the person's values rather than prescribing the same choice for every family.

For this fictional report, a counsellor would need more than the word “positive.” Relevant questions include: What test was used? What is known about V? What phenotype and time period do the quoted numbers concern? What other information is relevant? How would receiving more information affect the person's choices? These questions help identify the missing evidence without pretending that a classroom table provides an individual diagnosis.

Genetic information may also be relevant to biological relatives because relatives share some inherited variants. That does not make one person's result a test result for every relative, and it does not remove each person's interests in privacy and choice. A careful discussion considers how information might be communicated, who wants to know, and what support may be needed. You do not need to disclose your own health or family history for any activity here.

In your own notes, analyse this separate fictional dataset: 15 of 300 participants in group M and 6 of 300 in group N show a measured outcome during the same period. Calculate each proportion. Name one additional fact you would want before applying the result elsewhere.

Hint for comparing the groups

Divide each outcome count by 300 first. Then compare the two percentages and also count the people in group M without the outcome.

Compare your reasoning

Group M has 15/300 = 5%; group N has 6/300 = 2%. The observed ratio is 5/2 = 2.5, while the difference is 3 percentage points. In M, 285/300 = 95% did not show the outcome during that period. The headline can describe the relative comparison while leaving out the low absolute proportions. Before extending the finding, you might ask about age, recruitment, other exposures, measurement methods or the observation period. A strong answer shows the denominators and explains why the additional fact could change the interpretation.

Understand the proposed choice before agreeing

Suppose a fictional research study offers to test a saliva sample. The invitation says that participants will receive a report and that the sample may be kept for later research. Giving a sample involves more than answering the scientific question “Can a variant be detected?” It also raises questions about what the person understands and permits.

Informed consent is agreement based on understanding the proposed activity, its possible benefits, risks, limits and alternatives. A signed form records a choice, but a signature alone does not show that the explanation was understood. Participants need a chance to ask questions and make a voluntary decision.

Separate three things in this invitation: the test being offered now, the results that may be returned, and the future use of samples or data. A person could find the first useful while wanting more information about the other two. The choices available depend on the actual study and its consent process; we cannot infer them from the fact that a tube was supplied.

Read down the left column and decide which question the invitation has already answered. Then use the right column to explain why an unanswered question matters. “Privacy is important” is a reasonable concern, but “I need to know who can connect this coded sample with my name and what access is allowed” identifies something concrete to investigate.

Canadian research-ethics guidance addresses the handling of samples, data, results and future uses. The exact requirements depend on the research context; this classroom discussion does not decide anyone's legal rights. The underlying issue is to make meaningful choices possible. See the TCPS 2 chapter on human genetic research.

Keep the scientific conclusion separate from the decision

Consider two fictional people who understand the same uncertain risk estimate. One wants to receive additional results because planning with more information matters most to them. The other prefers not to receive optional findings that cannot be interpreted clearly. Their preferences do not change the study's numbers. The same evidence can be considered alongside different values and circumstances.

In a classroom answer, you can evaluate whether the explanation is accurate and whether meaningful consent is possible. You can identify benefits, concerns and unanswered questions. You cannot use a Punnett square or a risk percentage to decide what every person ought to choose.

Before moving on, write one sentence containing a scientific claim and another containing a value-based choice. For example, “The observed proportion was 6% in the V group” describes evidence. “I would want a clearer explanation before receiving optional results” describes a fictional person's preference. Keeping the sentences separate prevents a numerical observation from quietly turning into a command.

Worked example

Decide what a fictional report establishes

A research test examines four specified variants. It detects V in a participant's sample and detects none of the other three. A fictional follow-up study of people carrying V reported Q in 18 of 300 participants over ten years. The report contains no information about the participant's age or other relevant factors. Its invitation also says, “Your coded data may be used in future approved studies.” A student concludes: “Q is certain, there are no other relevant variants, and coding guarantees that nobody can identify the participant.” Which parts of that conclusion are supported?

List the observations before interpreting them

The laboratory detected V and did not detect three other tested variants. The scope is four specified variants. The follow-up study observed Q in 18 of 300 V carriers over a stated period. The invitation proposes future use of coded data. None of these observations yet says that Q is inevitable or that every DNA position has been examined.

Calculate and name the denominator

The study proportion is 18/300 = 0.06 = 6%. The denominator is the V carriers followed in that study, not all people, all future children or every person with Q. The remaining 282/300 = 94% did not develop Q during the ten-year period. “Certain” would mean a probability of 1 or 100%, which the supplied observation does not support.

Check what would be needed for the individual claim

The observed 6% may be relevant evidence, but applying it to one participant requires knowing how well the study group and conditions match. The report leaves important information unspecified. It also describes one observation period rather than an entire lifetime. The responsible conclusion is limited: V was detected, and the supplied study associates it with a measured outcome frequency. The scenario does not justify an individual prediction of certainty.

Check the scope and privacy claims separately

Three targeted negative results say nothing directly about variants the test did not examine. The statement “there are no other relevant variants” extends beyond the test. Coding means direct identifiers are replaced with a code; whether a record can be linked back to a person depends on access to the key and other information. The invitation does not justify an absolute anonymity guarantee. These scientific and privacy questions require different evidence, so solving one does not solve the other.

Write a conclusion that preserves the useful information

A sound response is: “The test detected V among four specified variants. The fictional study observed Q in 6% of its V carriers over ten years, so it does not establish inevitability for this participant. Untested variants remain outside the result's scope. Before agreeing to future use, the participant needs clear information about access, allowed uses and practical privacy protections.” This conclusion uses the result without giving it more authority than it has.

Apply the method to a changed situation

Try this in your own notes before opening the response. A different fictional test reports no detected change at a single named site. A researcher says this means the person can never develop the complex trait being studied. The invitation clearly explains the present test but says nothing about what happens to the sample afterwards. Identify one unsupported scientific inference and one specific question about participation. Explain both.

Compare your explanation

The single-site result does not rule out untested variants, contributions from other genes, environmental effects or other causes of a complex trait. “Never” is therefore stronger than the supplied evidence. A participant could ask whether the sample will be stored, how long, and what future uses or access would be permitted. This matters because consenting to the present test does not, by itself, explain the scope of later research. A complete answer links each concern to a missing fact rather than simply declaring that all testing is useless or all research is unsafe.

A genetic result can answer an important question while leaving another open. The skill is to keep the observation, the model, the probability and the personal choice distinct. We can now bring that habit together with the chromosome and inheritance reasoning used throughout the chapter.
