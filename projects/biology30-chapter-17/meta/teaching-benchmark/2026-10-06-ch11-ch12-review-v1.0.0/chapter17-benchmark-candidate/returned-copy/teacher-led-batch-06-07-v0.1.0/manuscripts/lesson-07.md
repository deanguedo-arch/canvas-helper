# Multiple alleles and ABO blood groups

<!-- Authored teaching interval begins. Boundary/protection notes are in the separate handoff records. -->

## How can one blood-group system have four named types?

Blood-group records can identify people as A, B, AB or O. These are four phenotype categories in the ABO system. We have been following two allele copies at an ordinary diploid locus, one inherited from each parent. How can that two-copy inheritance account for this range of blood groups?

The first distinction is between the versions available across a population and the pair present in one individual. A **gene** is a DNA sequence that contributes to a functional product. An allele is a version of that gene. **Multiple alleles** means that more than two versions occur in a population. It does not increase the usual number of copies at the locus in one diploid individual.

For the standard **ABO blood-group** model, we will follow three allele versions: **I<sup>A</sup>**, **I<sup>B</sup>** and **i**. Read I<sup>A</sup> as one complete allele symbol, “I superscript A,” and I<sup>B</sup> as “I superscript B.” The superscripts distinguish versions; they are not powers in an arithmetic calculation or separate genes. The lowercase i is a third version. Plain-text records write these as IA, IB and i.

The ABO locus is autosomal, on a chromosome other than a sex chromosome. An ordinary diploid genotype records two copies at that locus, one from the egg-producing parent and one from the sperm-producing parent. I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup> therefore contains *two* alleles, not four. A normal gamete contributes one of the parent's two copies, not all three population versions.

### What distinguishes the blood-group phenotypes?

The difference we are following concerns molecular markers on red blood cell membranes. A cell membrane is the boundary surrounding a cell. An **antigen** is a molecular feature that the immune system can recognise; A and B identify the two antigen types relevant to this ABO model.

Use the illustration to locate the cells whose surfaces we are discussing. Look at the red, disc-shaped cells within the vessel. Their outlines show individual cells, but there are no labels or resolved molecular markers showing A or B antigens. The picture gives cell context; its red colour cannot identify an ABO group.

![An illustration of red, disc-shaped blood cells within a vessel. No A or B molecular markers or blood-group labels are resolved in the image, so it cannot identify the cells' ABO types.](../assets/teaching-batch2-v010/slide-68-image66.jpg)

In the standard model, I<sup>A</sup> is associated with A antigen expression, I<sup>B</sup> with B antigen expression, and i with neither A nor B antigen expression. Someone with I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup> expresses both A and B on their red cells, including on the same cells. Someone with ii lacks these two ABO markers. That does not mean their cells have no membrane molecules or no other antigens.

### Account for every allele pair

List the pairs systematically. There are three homozygous possibilities, using two copies of the same version: I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>A</sup>, I<sup>B</sup>I<sup>B</sup> and ii. There are also three heterozygous possibilities, using two different versions: I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup>, I<sup>A</sup>i and I<sup>B</sup>i. Reversing the order, such as iI<sup>A</sup> instead of I<sup>A</sup>i, records the same unordered genotype in this model.

Read the following table from genotype to expressed markers, then to the blood-group name. Compare I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>A</sup> with I<sup>A</sup>i, then compare I<sup>A</sup>i with I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup>. Those comparisons establish the dominance relationships.

I<sup>A</sup> and I<sup>B</sup> are **codominant alleles**: their two associated markers are both expressed in I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup>. The AB phenotype is not an intermediate marker halfway between A and B. Each of these alleles is also completely dominant to i for the ABO category being scored. I<sup>A</sup>i is type A like I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>A</sup>, and I<sup>B</sup>i is type B like I<sup>B</sup>I<sup>B</sup>.

A dominance relationship compares a particular pair of alleles and its phenotype. The same I<sup>A</sup> allele can be codominant with I<sup>B</sup> and dominant to i. This does not rank the alleles by value or population frequency, and it does not favour either copy during segregation.

The table is a catalogue, not the result of a single parental cross. Six rows do not make each genotype equally common. Four blood-group names do not make each phenotype equally likely for every family. To predict probabilities, we need the allele pairs in the actual parents.

## Follow the contributions in one ABO cross

The phenotype table also shows where **genotype** uncertainty remains. Type A could be I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>A</sup> or I<sup>A</sup>i, and type B could be I<sup>B</sup>I<sup>B</sup> or I<sup>B</sup>i. The AB and O categories each identify one genotype within this model. For our first cross, the genotypes are supplied: **I<sup>A</sup>i × I<sup>B</sup>i**. We are not inferring both parents to be heterozygous just from their A and B appearances.

The I<sup>A</sup>i parent contributes I<sup>A</sup> or i, each at ½. The I<sup>B</sup>i parent contributes I<sup>B</sup> or i, each at ½. Segregation concerns the two copies at this one locus. There are no gametes carrying both I<sup>A</sup> and i from the first parent, and no gametes carrying an I<sup>A</sup> allele from the second parent, which lacks that version.

Use the square's **top edge for the I<sup>A</sup>i parent's gametes** and its **left edge for the I<sup>B</sup>i parent's gametes**. The figure does not assign an egg or sperm role to either edge; either assignment gives the same autosomal genotype grouping when used consistently. Each interior cell joins a top allele to a side allele. Random fertilisation makes each particular pairing probability ½ × ½ = ¼.

The circled letters A, B, C and D identify **positions in the diagram**. They are not extra allele symbols, and a position letter does not necessarily match the blood-group name at that position. The key below the square is already visible. Trace the contributions first, then use that key to check the phenotype you infer.

At position **A**, top I<sup>A</sup> meets side I<sup>B</sup>, giving I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup>. Both antigen types are expressed, so the phenotype is **AB**. At position **B**, top i meets side I<sup>B</sup>, giving I<sup>B</sup>i and phenotype **B**. At position **C**, top I<sup>A</sup> meets side i, giving I<sup>A</sup>i and phenotype **A**. At position **D**, the two i contributions meet, giving ii and phenotype **O**.

Each of these four cells gives a different genotype and a different phenotype. Consequently, this cross predicts ¼ AB, ¼ B, ¼ A and ¼ O. The genotype ratio is 1 I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup> : 1 I<sup>B</sup>i : 1 I<sup>A</sup>i : 1 ii. The phenotype ratio, in the diagram's position order, is 1 AB : 1 B : 1 A : 1 O. Four equal quarters total 1, so the complete set is accounted for.

The two contributions explain why an **allele** can be inherited without being apparent in a parent's ABO category. Each heterozygous parent carries i, even though neither is type O. An ii offspring can receive one i from each. Conversely, an AB offspring needs I<sup>A</sup> from one parent and I<sup>B</sup> from the other. Read the needed contributions rather than assigning offspring a blend of the parents' blood-group names.

### Name the category before combining probabilities

Suppose the question asks for a type A *or* type B offspring from these parents. A single offspring cannot be both of those phenotype categories; type AB is a separate category. Add the mutually exclusive alternatives: ¼ + ¼ = ½. For an offspring expressing both A and B antigens, use only the AB cell at ¼. The phrase “A or B” does not automatically include the phenotype named AB.

These calculations assume the standard three-allele expression rules, ordinary segregation and random fertilisation. We also assume the phenotype is expressed and classified reliably, with no category selectively lost from the population being counted. The probabilities describe possible offspring from these genotypes. Four children are not required to occupy the four cells once each, and a previous child's group does not make another group due next.

## Change a parent and rebuild the prediction

Now consider a woman known to be **homozygous type A, I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>A</sup>**, and a man known to be **heterozygous type B, I<sup>B</sup>i**, in a hypothetical classroom cross. What offspring blood groups does their combination allow? The woman looks type A, just as the I<sup>A</sup>i parent did in the first square, but her second allele is different. That changes the gametes available.

The woman's eggs contribute only I<sup>A</sup>, with probability 1. The man's sperm contribute I<sup>B</sup> or i, each at ½. Combining I<sup>A</sup> with I<sup>B</sup> gives I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup>, type AB, at 1 × ½ = ½. Combining I<sup>A</sup> with i gives I<sup>A</sup>i, type A, at 1 × ½ = ½. We have derived the two possibilities before counting any cells.

In the image, the woman's I<sup>A</sup> egg contribution is written twice across the top, while the man's I<sup>B</sup> and i sperm contributions are down the left. Compare the two top labels carefully: they repeat one distinct allele type. Follow either column into the upper and lower cells, then check that the second column repeats those same genotypes.

![The cross IAIA × IBi appears above a square. IA is repeated in both top columns. The left rows are IB and i. The upper cells are both IAIB and the lower cells both IAi. Repeated IA columns represent one distinct egg allele type.](../assets/teaching-batch2-v010/slide-69-image56.png)

I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>A</sup> × I<sup>B</sup>i. Two repeated columns expand the display without adding a new maternal gamete type or a new offspring genotype.

The certain I<sup>A</sup> contribution has been split across two equivalent drawn columns. Giving each repeated column half the total weight makes each pictured cell ½ × ½ = ¼. The two I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup> cells together have probability ¼ + ¼ = ½, and the two I<sup>A</sup>i cells together also have probability ½. A compact table with one I<sup>A</sup> column at weight 1 and two sperm rows at ½ would give exactly the same result. Equal-sized boxes do not create different gamete types.

The genotype ratio is 1 I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup> : 1 I<sup>A</sup>i, and the phenotype ratio is 1 AB : 1 A. Their probabilities total ½ + ½ = 1. Type O would require i from both parents, but the mother cannot supply i. Type B would require an offspring pair without I<sup>A</sup>, while every offspring here receives I<sup>A</sup>. Those excluded groups have probability 0 under this cross, not one quarter because the population has four group names.

### A proportion, a count and an ordered event are different questions

An **expected number** multiplies a **probability** by the total number of scored outcomes. Across a hypothetical record of 60 offspring from parental combinations with these same genotypes, expect 60 × ½ = 30 type AB and 60 × ½ = 30 type A. The expected counts total 60. This describes an imagined collection of records, not one family's size or an observed dataset.

For the specified event “first offspring AB, second offspring A,” assume independent offspring outcomes under the same parental model. The first requirement has probability ½ and the second ½, so both in that order have probability ½ × ½ = ¼. For one AB and one A in either order, include the separate A-then-AB ordering as well. The two mutually exclusive orderings each have probability ¼, giving ¼ + ¼ = ½. The denominator for these events concerns possible two-offspring records, not the number of phenotype names in the population.

A finite record may differ from expected proportions, but earlier outcomes do not change the next outcome's probability if the model remains fixed. Likewise, a possible but unobserved group is different from a group that the parents cannot produce under the model. To interpret an unexpected record, check which of those situations applies before deciding what the evidence supports.

Worked example

### Use a family record before calculating

A hypothetical family record gives a woman type B blood and her father type O. Her partner has type AB blood. Predict the possible ABO groups of their offspring, then distinguish the chance of *a son with type B* from the chance of *type B among sons*. The two questions will use the same parental genotypes but different counted groups.

#### Identify the missing allele from a known contribution

Type B alone allows I<sup>B</sup>I<sup>B</sup> or I<sup>B</sup>i. Start with the most informative family fact: the woman's type O father has genotype ii in the standard model, so he could contribute only i. The woman therefore has an i copy. Her own type B phenotype requires I<sup>B</sup> as the other copy. Together those facts force **I<sup>B</sup>i**.

The same contribution logic made a **test cross** informative: a known recessive contribution lets us interpret another allele in the record. Here we are interpreting supplied family information. We have not assumed that everyone with type B is heterozygous. The conclusion depends on the stated parentage, correctly recorded groups and the standard expression model.

The partner's AB phenotype identifies **I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup>**. Both antigen-associated alleles are needed, and there is no i in that pair. We can now write the justified cross as **I<sup>B</sup>i × I<sup>A</sup>I<sup>B</sup>**. This step must come before the square: beginning with a guessed homozygous B parent would change the prediction.

#### Combine gametes, then group the genotypes

The woman contributes I<sup>B</sup> or i in eggs at ½ each. The partner contributes I<sup>A</sup> or I<sup>B</sup> in sperm at ½ each. Put the egg contributions down the left and sperm contributions across the top. Each interior pairing has probability ½ × ½ = ¼ under random fertilisation.

The upper-left cell joins I<sup>B</sup> from the egg with I<sup>A</sup> from the sperm, producing AB. The upper-right joins two I<sup>B</sup> copies, producing B. The lower-left joins i with I<sup>A</sup>, producing A. The lower-right joins i with I<sup>B</sup>, also producing B. The four genotypes are distinct and equally probable, so their genotype ratio is 1:1:1:1 in that cell order.

Type B, however, includes two different genotypes. Their probabilities add because an individual cannot be both I<sup>B</sup>I<sup>B</sup> and I<sup>B</sup>i: **¼ + ¼ = ½ type B**. Type A has probability ¼ and type AB has probability ¼. Type O requires ii and is impossible under these parents because the AB partner cannot supply i. Thus the phenotype ratio is **1 A : 2 B : 1 AB**, with O at 0, and ¼ + ½ + ¼ + 0 = 1.

Notice what an observed type B child would and would not reveal. That child could be I<sup>B</sup>I<sup>B</sup> or I<sup>B</sup>i; its appearance does not choose between them. A type A child from these parents would have to be I<sup>A</sup>i because the mother cannot supply I<sup>A</sup>. A genotype is **forced** when only one pair fits all the supplied evidence within the model. Otherwise, keep the remaining possibilities visible.

#### Which group is in the denominator?

The ABO square has so far counted **all offspring**, without separating sons and daughters. To include those categories, state an additional classroom chromosome model: an XX egg-producing parent contributes X, and an XY sperm-producing parent contributes X or Y with equal probability. In this simplified model, an X-bearing sperm gives the daughter category and a Y-bearing sperm gives the son category. The categories each have probability ½. This is a specified inheritance model, not a complete account of human sex development.

For the calculation, also assume that the sperm's ABO allele contribution is independent of its X/Y contribution, that fertilisation is random, and that neither sex category nor ABO group is selectively lost or differently represented when the records are counted. The ABO expression rules apply in both categories. Under these conditions, knowing that an offspring is a son does not change its ABO probabilities. Saying only that ABO is autosomal would not establish independence for every possible trait, event or selected sample.

Let **P(event)** mean the probability of the named event. The event “a son *and* type B” requires both properties in the same offspring. With all offspring as the denominator, **P(son and B) = ½ × ½ = ¼**. The first half selects the son category; the second half is the type B fraction within that category under the independent model.

The table shows all six non-zero sex-and-blood-group categories. Read down one column to see the ABO groups within that sex category. Read across a row to recover the ABO probability among all offspring. Each entry is a joint probability with all offspring as its denominator.

The son column totals ⅛ + ¼ + ⅛ = ½, as expected. The daughter column also totals ½. Adding the two columns gives 1, so no offspring category is omitted. Do not add the final “all offspring” column to them again: it already combines those same categories.

Now change the question to “What fraction *among sons* has type B?” We are given that the offspring belongs to the son category. This is a **conditional probability**, a probability within a specified subgroup. Only the son column belongs in the denominator. Type B sons have probability ¼ among all offspring, and all sons have probability ½ among all offspring. Dividing puts both quantities on the requested subgroup basis:

**P(B given son) = P(B and son) ÷ P(son) = ¼ ÷ ½ = ½.**

The answer is ½ because type B occupies half of the son column. Multiplying by ½ again would reintroduce the probability of being a son even though the question has already restricted us to sons. The joint answer ¼ and conditional answer ½ are compatible: one describes all offspring, and the other describes only sons. The same distinction applies to any question that selects a subgroup before asking for another property.

#### Use the same denominator when predicting counts

For a hypothetical collection of 64 offspring records from these parental genotypes and assumptions, the expected number of sons is 64 × ½ = 32. The expected number who are both sons and type B is **64 × ¼ = 16**. Equivalently, half of the expected 32 sons is 16. Both calculations begin with the same 64-record population.

If a different question supplies a sample of *64 sons*, the denominator has changed. The expected type B count is then **64 × ½ = 32**, using the conditional probability. Writing 64 × ¼ would apply an all-offspring probability to a sample that already excludes daughters. Always name the counted group next to the number.

Expected counts do not specify the actual counts in a finite family or record collection. With these known parents, four specified independent offspring could all be type B: each has probability ½, so the event has probability ½ × ½ × ½ × ½ = 1/16. Such a run would not remove type A or AB from the next offspring's possibilities. A missing group in a finite sample is not automatically an impossible group.

If parental genotypes were uncertain instead, first calculate what each candidate pair would permit. A finite record can be compatible with more than one pair. Comparing the probability of that record under two hypotheses is different from assigning probabilities to the hypotheses themselves; the latter needs additional information about the starting alternatives. We should not declare a genotype proven merely because one outcome was common.

#### Keep the conclusion within the evidence

For the supplied hypothetical record, the O grandfather's known contribution established the mother's i allele. The AB partner's genotype then established the possible gametes. Those particular constraints made the calculation possible. In a real record, an apparent contradiction is a reason to check the typing, information and model. A simplified ABO square cannot establish or exclude a family relationship definitively, and ABO inheritance by itself does not provide clinical transfusion instructions.

Use genetic vocabulary just as carefully. In a stated simple recessive-condition model, a **carrier** is an individual who has the recessive disease-associated allele without expressing that recessive condition. That description depends on the trait's inheritance and expression rules. It does not make every heterozygote an unaffected carrier or turn the ABO allele i into a disease allele. For ABO, recording I<sup>A</sup>i or I<sup>B</sup>i says exactly which versions are present.

Other multiple-allele systems need their own expression rules. A coat-colour problem may give a dominance hierarchy among several versions; that hierarchy cannot be replaced with the ABO table. More than two versions of *one* gene is also different from many genes contributing to one characteristic. Keep the number of loci, the versions available and the expression rule separate.

#### The two-copy rule still explains the four groups

We can now answer the opening puzzle. Three allele versions across the population can form six unordered pairs in individuals, and the expression relationships group those pairs into four ABO phenotypes. Each parent still contributes one allele at the locus. Known genotypes establish possible contributions; family evidence may resolve an unknown pair; and the question determines which genotype, phenotype or subgroup belongs in a probability calculation.

ABO let us use the same autosomal expression rules for sons and daughters under the stated model. A gene located on a sex chromosome can have a different pattern of available copies and transmission. To understand that next, we will keep each allele attached to its chromosome and ask which chromosome each parent can contribute.

## Meanings, examples and distinctions

<!-- ch17-concept-multiple-alleles; /course-data/practiceConcepts/24 -->

<details>
<summary>multiple alleles</summary>

<p><strong>Term:</strong> multiple alleles</p>

<p><strong>Meaning:</strong> More than two allele versions of one gene present in a population.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> The simplified ABO model includes IA, IB and i.</p>

<p><strong>Optional cue:</strong> Identify the structure, quantity or relationship described.</p>

<p><strong>Explanation:</strong> More than two allele versions of one gene present in a population. An individual still usually carries only two alleles at that autosomal locus.</p>

<p><strong>Claim to examine:</strong> Multiple alleles means every person carries three ABO alleles.</p>

<p><strong>Correction:</strong> More than two allele versions of one gene present in a population. An individual still usually carries only two alleles at that autosomal locus.</p>

</details>

<!-- ch17-concept-abo-blood-group; /course-data/practiceConcepts/25 -->

<details>
<summary>ABO blood group</summary>

<p><strong>Term:</strong> ABO blood group</p>

<p><strong>Meaning:</strong> A blood-group system modeled with IA, IB and i alleles producing A, B, AB and O phenotypes.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> IAIB expresses both A and B antigen types.</p>

<p><strong>Optional cue:</strong> Identify the structure, quantity or relationship described.</p>

<p><strong>Explanation:</strong> A blood-group system modeled with IA, IB and i alleles producing A, B, AB and O phenotypes. The classroom model omits rare variants and is not a transfusion decision tool.</p>

<p><strong>Claim to examine:</strong> ABO group alone is enough for every clinical transfusion decision.</p>

<p><strong>Correction:</strong> A blood-group system modeled with IA, IB and i alleles producing A, B, AB and O phenotypes. The classroom model omits rare variants and is not a transfusion decision tool.</p>

</details>

<!-- ch17-concept-codominant-alleles; /course-data/practiceConcepts/26 -->

<details>
<summary>codominant alleles</summary>

<p><strong>Term:</strong> codominant alleles</p>

<p><strong>Meaning:</strong> Alleles whose contributions are both expressed in the heterozygote.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> IA and IB are both expressed in the IAIB genotype.</p>

<p><strong>Optional cue:</strong> Identify the structure, quantity or relationship described.</p>

<p><strong>Explanation:</strong> Alleles whose contributions are both expressed in the heterozygote. Both are dominant over i in the simplified ABO model.</p>

<p><strong>Claim to examine:</strong> IA must completely mask IB.</p>

<p><strong>Correction:</strong> Alleles whose contributions are both expressed in the heterozygote. Both are dominant over i in the simplified ABO model.</p>

</details>

<!-- ch17-concept-carrier; /course-data/practiceConcepts/27 -->

<details>
<summary>carrier</summary>

<p><strong>Term:</strong> carrier</p>

<p><strong>Meaning:</strong> An individual who carries a recessive disease-associated allele without expressing the recessive condition in a specified simple model.</p>

<p><strong>Example:</strong> A heterozygote passes a recessive allele to offspring while showing the dominant phenotype.</p>

<p><strong>Optional cue:</strong> Identify the structure, quantity or relationship described.</p>

<p><strong>Explanation:</strong> An individual who carries a recessive disease-associated allele without expressing the recessive condition in a specified simple model. Use carrier only when the inheritance model and trait make the meaning appropriate.</p>

<p><strong>Claim to examine:</strong> Every person with a dominant disease allele is an unaffected carrier.</p>

<p><strong>Correction:</strong> An individual who carries a recessive disease-associated allele without expressing the recessive condition in a specified simple model. Use carrier only when the inheritance model and trait make the meaning appropriate.</p>

</details>
