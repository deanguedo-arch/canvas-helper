Why choose particular parents?

A breeder choosing dogs as parents is making a prediction about a future generation. The prediction matters only if some of the differences among the dogs can be inherited. Why might choosing particular parents influence their offspring, and how much can the parents’ appearance tell us?

Look along the row of dogs below. Compare the position of their ears, the texture of their coats and the outlines of their bodies. Several differences are visible at once. Keep those observations separate from an explanation of how each difference arose: a photograph cannot tell us which inherited information an individual carries, how it was raised, or how many genes contribute to a characteristic.

Selective breeding means choosing which organisms reproduce because of characteristics people want in later generations. If a difference has an inherited component, choosing parents can change how often that difference occurs over generations. It does not change an existing dog into another kind of dog. Nor does choosing two parents guarantee every feature of each offspring: offspring receive inherited contributions from both parents, and development also depends on conditions such as nutrition.

The dog comparison gives us the question, but it is too complicated to treat every visible difference as a single inherited unit. We need to distinguish the information passed on from the characteristic we observe. We will then use one clearly defined flower-colour model to follow that distinction closely.

What passes from parents to offspring?

DNA is the molecule whose sequence carries inherited information used by cells. A chromosome is a long DNA molecule packaged with proteins; it contains many genes. A is a DNA sequence that contributes to a functional product. Often that product is a protein, a molecule with a particular job in a cell. A product involved in making a pigment, for example, can contribute to an organism’s colour. A gene is part of the explanation of a characteristic; it is not a miniature copy of the characteristic itself.

An is a version of a gene. Versions can differ in their DNA sequence and in how they affect the product or its production. The gene identifies which inherited information we are studying; the allele identifies which version is present. A locus is the position of a gene on a chromosome. The plural is loci.

For the simple model we are about to use, consider an ordinary diploid body cell: it has two sets of chromosomes, one inherited from each parent. The gene is on an autosome, a chromosome other than a sex chromosome. The corresponding chromosomes from the two parents form a homologous pair. They carry the same kinds of genes at corresponding loci, although their alleles can differ.

Read the table across its rows. It follows one locus on the two chromosomes in a pair before those chromosomes have copied their DNA. The table is tracking the same gene twice, not two different genes.

There are therefore two allele copies at this locus in the diploid cell. Two copies do not necessarily mean two different versions. Both copies might be the same version, or each might be a different version. A population can contain more than two versions of a gene even though an ordinary diploid individual has only two copies at this autosomal locus.

An egg and a sperm are gametes, reproductive cells that normally carry one chromosome set. Each contributes one allele at the studied locus. When the gametes join at fertilisation, the new individual receives a pair again. The later account of chromosome separation will explain how a cell with two copies gives rise to contributions with one. For now, the consequence is enough: to describe the inherited combination in our diploid model, we must record one contribution from each parent.

Read an allele pair and the characteristic it produces

We will now leave the many differences among dogs and examine one flower-colour model. In this model, B and b name two alleles of one gene. Plants with BB or Bb have purple flowers; plants with bb have white flowers. These are the expression rules we will use. The letters refer to this flower-colour gene, and each letter in a pair represents one allele copy.

The is the allele combination at the locus or loci being considered. Here we are considering only one locus, so BB, Bb and bb are genotypes. The is the observable or measurable characteristic expressed in the organism’s biological context. Here the scored phenotype is flower colour.

Read the chart one row at a time, from the two letters on the left to the flower on the right. Then compare the first and second rows. The allele pairs change from BB to Bb, but the flower colour stays purple. Finally, compare those rows with bb and its white flower.

The two columns answer different questions. “Bb” reports inherited information at the studied locus. “Purple flowers” reports what we can observe. A complete genotype record can support a phenotype prediction when the expression rule is known. A phenotype observation does not always let us reconstruct a complete genotype record.

For two versions, B and b, the three possible unordered pairs are BB, Bb and bb. Writing bB instead of Bb does not create a fourth genotype in this ordinary autosomal model. It still records one B and one b. We usually write the capital first to make records consistent; that convention does not identify which parent contributed it.

Explain the shared purple phenotype

Complete dominance describes a relationship between alleles and a particular phenotype: the heterozygote has the same scored phenotype as one of the two homozygotes. In our flower model, Bb and BB are both purple. We therefore call B dominant to b for flower colour. The white phenotype associated with b appears when the plant is bb, so b is recessive in this relationship.

The B allele has not removed or changed b in a Bb plant. The plant still has both versions, even though its flower colour does not distinguish it from BB. Dominance concerns expression in the heterozygote; it does not give the dominant allele a greater chance of entering a gamete.

This chart shows a visible phenotype, but phenotype is broader than appearance. An enzyme’s measured activity is a physiological phenotype, and a measured behaviour can also be a phenotype. The relationship between inherited information and a measured characteristic can depend on other genes and environmental conditions. Our simple flower-colour model sets those complications aside by stating a reliable expression rule for the characteristic being scored.

Use the model before the letters

The letter B does not mean “purple” in every organism. It is a symbol defined for this problem. Using the same letter in uppercase and lowercase helps show that B and b are alleles of one gene. A different gene needs a different clearly defined symbol. Capital and lowercase notation is convenient for complete dominance; other allele relationships can require other notation.

“Dominant” also does not mean common, better, stronger or healthier. To establish dominance, compare the heterozygote’s phenotype with those of the homozygotes. To establish how common an allele is, count its occurrence in a population. Those are different kinds of evidence. The expression rule by itself supplies no population frequency.

Now read the relationship in reverse. A white-flowered plant must be bb under our stated model because BB and Bb are purple. A purple-flowered plant could be BB or Bb. When only its colour is known, write B_ to keep the unresolved second allele visible. The underscore is a placeholder: it could be B or b. It is not an additional allele and does not mean the plant has only one copy of the gene.

These conclusions depend on the model. We have assumed the stated genotypes reliably produce the stated colours under the conditions being considered. If a colour is affected by another gene or cannot be scored reliably, appearance may provide less information. We should check those conditions rather than force every organism into a purple-or-white rule. Human eye colour, for example, cannot be accurately explained as a simple one-gene B/b trait.

Before using a breeding record or drawing a table of possible offspring, establish which gene the symbols describe, which allele combinations the parents can have, and how those combinations relate to phenotype. Otherwise, a neat table can begin with an unjustified parent or assign the wrong characteristic to its offspring. Symbols keep a biological explanation visible; they cannot supply a missing explanation.

Worked example

Follow a contribution record before interpreting appearance

Consider three hypothetical offspring plants in the same flower-colour model. Their inherited contributions at the studied locus have been recorded. For Plant 1, the egg contributed B and the sperm contributed b. For Plant 2, the egg contributed b and the sperm contributed B. For Plant 3, both contributed b. All three records concern the same kind of flowering plant in the model.

The task is to interpret the records without making letter order stand for a different genotype. The givens are the contributions, the diploid one-from-each-parent rule, and the expression relationship BB or Bb → purple, bb → white. No probabilities are needed because these records state which contributions actually occurred.

  1. Combine the two contributions for each plant. Plant 1 has B from its egg and b from its sperm, so its genotype is Bb. Plant 2 has b from its egg and B from its sperm. Its ordered contribution record is different, but its allele pair is also Bb. Plant 3 has b from both, so its genotype is bb. We retain two symbols in every offspring record because fertilisation joins two contributions.
  2. Compare allele versions within each pair. Plant 1 and Plant 2 each have two different versions and are heterozygous. Plant 3 has two copies of the same version and is homozygous recessive. Two lowercase letters still represent two allele copies.
  3. Apply the expression rule after identifying the pair. Plant 1 and Plant 2 are purple because Bb has the purple phenotype in this model. Plant 3 is white because it is bb. Reversing which parent supplied B did not alter the allele pair or the stated phenotype.
  4. Check what an appearance-only record would lose. If Plant 1’s contribution record were lost and only “purple flowers” remained, BB and Bb would both fit that observation. Writing BB as a certainty would add information that the flower colour did not supply. The careful record would be B_ until other evidence resolved it.

What would breeding evidence need to show?

We can now return to the breeder’s question. Parents pass inherited information to offspring, but an observed characteristic is not a complete record of that information. Two organisms that look alike for a characteristic may carry different allele pairs, and a version can be present without producing a distinct heterozygote phenotype.

This raises an experimental question: when parents have contrasting forms of a characteristic, do the inherited versions blend into something new, or can a version remain available even when it is not apparent in the offspring? Mendel’s controlled crosses with pea plants give us evidence to examine that question. We will need to follow the starting parents and successive generations before explaining the pattern.
