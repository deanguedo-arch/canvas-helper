Follow an allele from the evidence to the prediction

A familiar ratio can be a useful check, but it cannot choose an inheritance model for you. If a cross produces four categories, you still need to know what those categories mean and why their probabilities would be equal or unequal. This review follows the reasoning that turns a biological observation into a justified prediction.

As you read, pause at each short case and decide what information does the work. Is it the phenotype rule, a known parental contribution, chromosome position, a counted subgroup or an observed sample? Naming that evidence is more useful than memorizing a sequence of ratios.

Genes alleles genotype and phenotype

A gene is a DNA sequence that contributes to a functional product. An allele is a version of that gene. At an ordinary diploid autosomal locus, an individual has two allele copies, one inherited from each parent. The genotype records the pair. The phenotype is the observed or measured characteristic under the conditions in which it is expressed.

Suppose a supplied model says NN and Nn produce a dark seed coat, while nn produces a pale coat. A dark seed therefore permits NN or Nn; it does not establish which one is present. Write N_ if the second allele is unresolved. The underscore means that either permitted allele could occupy that position. It is not a third allele, and it does not stand for a missing chromosome. Dominance describes the heterozygote's phenotype in this model, not which version is better, more common or more likely to enter a gamete.

Mendel's evidence and segregation

In the pea experiments, contrasting true-breeding parents produced an F1 generation with one of the parental phenotypes. A phenotype absent from the F1 appearance could return in F2. The important observation was that an inherited contribution could remain present without appearing in the heterozygote's phenotype. The versions had not blended irreversibly.

Connect that observation to chromosomes. Corresponding loci on homologous chromosomes can carry different alleles. After DNA is copied, homologues separate in meiosis I and sister chromatids separate in meiosis II, with no DNA replication between those divisions. A normal haploid contribution has one allele at the locus. In flowering plants, meiosis produces spores and later haploid stages form gametes. Fertilisation brings one allele contribution from each gamete together. Segregation explains separation; fertilisation explains restoration of the pair.

For a true-breeding NN × nn cross, the parental contributions are N and n, so F1 is Nn. When two Nn contributions are combined through reproduction, n remains available from both parents. An nn offspring can therefore appear without a new n allele having to arise. Generation labels describe relationships: they do not make every possible F1 heterozygous by definition.

A square organizes gametes already justified by the model

Take Nn × Nn under equal segregation and random fertilisation. Each parent contributes N or n at one half. The four contribution pairs are NN, Nn, nN and nn, each at ½ × ½ = ¼. Nn and nN belong to the same unordered genotype, so genotype probabilities are ¼ NN, ½ Nn and ¼ nn. Complete dominance groups the first two genotypes into the dark phenotype, producing ¾ dark and ¼ pale.

Read a square's edges as gamete contributions and its interior as offspring combinations. If a homozygous parent is drawn with two identical edge labels, those columns repeat the same gamete type. The number of drawn boxes does not create additional allele types or justify equal weights when the biological probabilities differ.

For mutually exclusive alternatives, add probabilities. For specified independent events that must both occur, multiply them. A probability of ¼ predicts an expected count of 80 × ¼ = 20 in a set of 80, not a guarantee that exactly 20 will occur. For two independent offspring, the chance that both meet that outcome is ¼ × ¼ = 1/16. Once one offspring's outcome is known, it does not make the next result due to compensate.

Use an informative contribution to resolve an unknown

A dark N_ plant crossed with nn makes the recessive parent's contribution certain: every tester gamete carries n. A pale nn offspring must also have received n from the dark parent. The dark parent is therefore Nn within this model. One informative pale offspring can rule out NN; it does not need to be the most common phenotype in the sample.

If all six observed offspring are dark, NN remains possible, but Nn is not eliminated. Under Nn × nn, each offspring has probability ½ of being dark, and six dark offspring in a specified sequence have probability (½)6 = 1/64. That is possible. It is the probability of these observations if the parent is Nn, not automatically the probability that the parent is Nn after the observations. The latter would require additional information about the competing possibilities.

Two loci add a chromosome question

An AaBb organism has A and a at one locus and B and b at another. Under independent assortment, its gametes are AB, Ab, aB and ab at one quarter each. Each gamete contains one allele at each locus. A gamete labelled Aa has two copies at the first locus and omits the second, so it is not a valid ordinary haploid contribution for this problem.

The independent product rule follows the same reasoning. In AaBb × AaBb with independent loci, P(aa) = ¼ and P(bb) = ¼, giving P(aabb) = ¼ × ¼ = 1/16. For two completely dominant phenotype rules, the familiar 9:3:3:1 grouping comes from multiplying the single-locus phenotype probabilities. Change a parental genotype, the expression rule or the independence assumption and you must rebuild the prediction.

Random fertilisation and independent assortment answer different questions. Random fertilisation concerns which gametes combine. Independent assortment concerns how alleles at different loci are distributed into gametes. Linked loci can violate the equal four-gamete expectation even when fertilisation is random.

Expression rules can change while segregation continues

With incomplete dominance, a heterozygote's measured phenotype is intermediate between the two homozygotes under the stated model. With codominance, both allele-associated forms or products are detectable in the heterozygote. An intermediate flower colour and the presence of two distinguishable antigen types therefore represent different expression relationships.

In either case, the two allele versions remain distinct and can segregate. Crossing two heterozygotes can give the familiar 1:2:1 genotype probabilities. If all three genotypes have distinguishable phenotypes, the phenotype grouping also remains 1:2:1. Do not automatically combine the heterozygote with one homozygote as a 3:1 phenotype ratio.

Population diversity does not add a third allele copy to a person

Multiple alleles means that more than two versions occur in a population. In the standard ABO model, the versions are IA, IB and i, while an ordinary diploid individual has two. IA and IB are codominant with each other, and each is dominant to i for the ABO phenotype being scored.

Type A permits IAIA or IAi; type B permits IBIB or IBi; AB is IAIB; O is ii. Start a cross with the parents' actual possible pairs. The existence of four blood-group names does not make four groups equally likely for every pair of parents. Family information may force one missing allele, but the classroom model does not establish real-world parentage or transfusion decisions.

The common method across these cases is now visible: state the model, infer the permitted parental genotypes, derive gametes, combine contributions, and only then group the offspring by the phenotype or event asked about.

Choose the model and the group being counted

The next set of ideas changes either the transmission route, the expression process or the evidence available. A correct calculation begins by recognising which change matters. Keep genotype evidence separate from phenotype observations, and name the denominator before reporting a probability.

Some questions ask what follows if a model is true. Others ask whether the observations support the model in the first place. A result that is possible under a model is consistent with it. That alone does not establish that no other model could fit.

X-linked inheritance follows a chromosome route

In the typical XX/XY model used here, an XX parent contributes X to every offspring, while an XY parent contributes X or Y. For a locus on X with no corresponding allele on Y, keep the allele symbol attached to X. XbY contains one tracked allele at that locus, not a recessive b paired with a dominant Y. This one-copy state is hemizygous.

For a fully expressed X-linked recessive model, XBXb can be an unaffected carrier and XbY expresses the trait. An affected father supplies his X to daughters and his Y to sons. Father and son can both express a trait without the father having transmitted his X-linked allele directly to the son; the son's X contribution comes from the mother.

Always distinguish a joint event from a conditional one. “The probability that the next offspring is a son and expresses the trait” uses all offspring as the denominator. “Expresses the trait, given that the offspring is a son” counts only among sons. Calculate the probability of the joint event, then divide by the probability of the specified subgroup when finding the conditional probability. The wording changes the counted group, not which chromosomes were transmitted.

Several genes and the environment can shape one measurement

Polygenic inheritance involves more than one locus contributing to a trait. A simple additive teaching model counts contributions from several alleles, producing several phenotype classes. More loci and environmental variation can make a measured distribution appear continuous. A bell-shaped distribution is not proof of a particular number of genes, and continuous variation does not mean inheritance has stopped operating.

Gene interaction can also produce discrete categories. If pigment must first be produced and then deposited, a genotype that prevents deposition can mask differences in the pigment-making step. Group offspring using the stated pathway rules after determining their allele combinations. Do not substitute the complete-dominance 9:3:3:1 ratio when several genotypes have the same visible result.

The environment can affect development or gene expression without changing the inherited DNA sequence. Expression concerns how genetic information is used and how much product is produced; mutation concerns a change in sequence. Compare genetically similar groups under controlled conditions before attributing a measured difference to an environmental variable. A comparison in which genotype and environment both change cannot separate their contributions by appearance alone.

Autosomal pedigrees constrain genotypes

A pedigree represents relationships and a specified phenotype. Read the key and connecting lines before assigning alleles. In a fully penetrant autosomal recessive model, an aa child needs a from each parent. If both parents are unaffected, each must also carry A, so both are Aa. This is a required contribution, not a guess based on whether the trait seems to skip a generation.

An unaffected child of Aa × Aa can be AA or Aa. Before conditioning, the probabilities are ¼ AA, ½ Aa and ¼ aa. The unaffected subgroup has probability ¾. The carrier fraction within that subgroup is (½) ÷ (¾) = 2/3. The denominator excludes the aa category because the question has told us that the child is unaffected. It does not change the parents' gamete probabilities.

For an autosomal dominant model, use a different expression rule. An unaffected dd child requires d from both parents. An affected parent of that child must therefore be Dd rather than DD under the simple model. Leave all other unresolved genotypes as alternatives until a relationship actually distinguishes them.

Test sex-linked models against every informative relationship

In a fully penetrant X-linked dominant model, an affected father passes the trait-associated X to every daughter. An unaffected daughter would contradict that simple model under the stated transmission and no-new-mutation assumptions. In an X-linked recessive model, daughters of an affected father and a homozygous unaffected mother inherit the same paternal route but are heterozygous carriers. The expression rule changes the shading pattern.

More affected males may be a clue, but a small autosomal family can also contain an uneven sex distribution by chance. Check all branches rather than choosing the first familiar pattern. State whether the evidence excludes a model, merely fits it, or leaves more than one model possible. Reduced penetrance, age at observation and new mutations can change real interpretations; do not add those exceptions only when convenient to rescue a classroom model.

Linked genes change gamete frequencies

AaBb records which alleles are present, but it does not record which alleles share a homologue. AB/ab means that A and B are together on one homologue; Ab/aB is a different phase. Parental combinations match the starting homologues. Recombinants differ from them. Capital letters alone cannot identify the parental classes.

During prophase I, crossing over can exchange corresponding segments between nonsister chromatids of homologous chromosomes. A crossover between the tracked loci can produce new allele combinations. A homozygous recessive tester contributes a fixed combination, allowing scored offspring to reveal the heterozygote's gamete contributions under the supplied phenotype rules. An excess of parental classes supports linkage when sampling and other explanations have been considered.

A linkage map estimates genetic distance

Recombination frequency uses both reciprocal recombinant classes in the numerator and all scored offspring in the denominator. Multiplying the resulting fraction by 100 gives a percentage. Over a short interval, that percentage approximates a distance in map units or centimorgans. A centimorgan is not a universal number of DNA base pairs.

Multiple exchanges can restore the parental outside-marker arrangement, so two-marker scoring misses some crossing over. The standard theoretical two-locus recombination fraction approaches 50%; a finite observed sample can fluctuate above 50%. A large map assembled from shorter intervals can exceed 50 cM without contradicting that limit. Check phase, counting and assumptions rather than turning every observed percentage directly into a reliable long-distance map.

With three short-interval map distances, test which pair is the sum of the other two. If P–Q = 4, Q–R = 7 and P–R = 11 cM, Q lies between P and R in the simple additive map. The reversed drawing gives the same order. With only two distances from one locus, a third locus may lie on either side; do not invent the missing comparison.

Genetic information supports decisions within limits

A laboratory result needs a defined testing scope and evidence about what a detected variant means. A negative targeted result does not rule out every untested cause. A risk-associated result does not automatically guarantee a phenotype. Counselling helps make the information and its limits understandable while respecting the person's choices.

Consent, storage, access and possible implications for relatives are additional questions. Scientific usefulness does not by itself settle how information should be shared. In this chapter's fictional cases, state what the evidence supports, what remains uncertain and what a participant would need to understand. There is no requirement to supply your own family information.

Recognise when an extended problem adds an assumption

Some inheritance problems distinguish fertilised offspring from surviving offspring. For a hypothetical model in which LL does not survive, Ll has a striped phenotype and ll is plain, an Ll × Ll cross gives ¼ LL, ½ Ll and ¼ ll at fertilisation. If LL offspring are absent from the later count and the other groups survive equally, the survivors total ¾ of the original expectation. Among survivors, the striped fraction is (½)/(¾) = 2/3 and the plain fraction is (¼)/(¾) = 1/3. The original segregation has not changed; the sample being counted has changed.

A striped Ll organism in that model cannot form a true-breeding striped line by producing only Ll gametes. Its gametes carry L or l, one allele at the locus. Crossing two striped individuals again permits LL and ll as well as Ll. This reasoning depends on the explicitly supplied survival and expression rules. It does not justify assuming that unusual ratios always result from lethal alleles.

Selective breeding uses inherited variation to change the characteristics represented in later generations. Choosing tall plants is informative only if some of their advantage is inherited under the growing conditions used. Compare offspring under similar conditions, and retain enough variation to assess useful combinations rather than assuming the tallest plant in a favourable location has the best inherited height potential. A breed or cultivated variety is not automatically a new species. Human genetics does not use experimental assignment of people to reproductive crosses; supplied pedigrees and appropriately consented studies provide evidence by other routes.

Two crop examples make the selection process concrete. Long-term selection contributed to maize with larger, more productive grain-bearing structures. Traditional breeding also produced canola varieties with low erucic acid in the oil and low glucosinolate content in the meal. In each case, breeders selected inherited characteristics over generations; the word “variety” does not automatically imply a separate species. The Canola Council's breeding history documents the second example.

A recessive allele can remain in unaffected heterozygotes even when individuals expressing the recessive condition do not reproduce. The allele is therefore not automatically eliminated in one generation. Similarly, mating related organisms can increase the chance that offspring receive two copies of an allele inherited from a shared ancestor. This can expose harmful recessive variants. It does not mean every purebred animal has a disorder or every mixed-breed animal is healthy; a breeding decision requires evidence about the actual population and traits.

If a fictional family-risk problem gives one person's pedigree but no information about the other parent's relevant genotype, keep that missing contribution visible. You may be able to calculate a conditional probability for the known side without calculating a single numerical probability for their offspring. Ask which genotypes remain possible and what further evidence would distinguish them. Do not assign an unknown partner a genotype simply to finish a square.

Prepare to explain rather than recognise

For any calculation, write the givens, define each symbol, explain the method, show the important steps and interpret the result. Include the assumption whose failure would change your answer. For a comparison, state the dimension you are comparing: chromosome route, allele expression, number of loci, evidence strength or denominator. That makes your explanation checkable by someone who cannot see what you were thinking.

Worked example

Follow a plant cross from family evidence to a map estimate

In a hypothetical plant, A produces purple stems and is completely dominant to a, which produces green stems. B produces smooth pods and is completely dominant to b, which produces ridged pods. A purple-stemmed, smooth-podded plant has an aabb parent. It is crossed with an aabb tester. The scored offspring are:

Constructed test-cross observations

Purple stem smooth pod96

Purple stem ridged pod6
Green stem smooth pod4

Determine the informative parent's genotype and phase, explain what the tester reveals, compare the observations with independent assortment, and calculate a short-interval map estimate. Assume ordinary segregation, random fertilisation, the stated expression rules, reliable scoring and no genotype-dependent survival differences in the counted offspring.

Use the known parent's contribution

The plant's aabb parent could contribute only ab. The plant therefore has a and b. Its purple stem requires A and its smooth pod requires B, so the remaining contribution contains A and B. Its genotype is AaBb. If these loci lie on the same chromosome, the inherited phase is AB/ab: one homologue carries the ab combination received from the known parent and the other carries AB. The family information alone does not establish physical linkage; we must compare the offspring counts with the independent-assortment expectation. Appearance alone would not have resolved even the AaBb genotype.

Translate each offspring back into a gamete

The tester contributes only ab. An AB gamete from the informative parent gives AaBb, purple and smooth. An ab gamete gives aabb, green and ridged. An Ab gamete gives Aabb, purple and ridged. An aB gamete gives aaBb, green and smooth. Thus the four phenotype counts report the four gamete contributions under the stated assumptions.

Read down the first column and compare it with the AB and ab contributions established from the family information. Those are the parental combinations; if the loci share a chromosome, they define AB/ab phase. We have not defined “parental” to mean “larger.” The data now let us compare the frequency of the parental combinations with the independent expectation.

Compare the independent expectation with the evidence

Under independent assortment, the four gametes would each have probability ¼. The total count is 96 + 94 + 6 + 4 = 200, so the expected count would be 200 × ¼ = 50 in each class. Instead, 190 offspring fall in the two parental classes and 10 in the two recombinant classes. The marked excess of parental combinations is consistent with linkage under the supplied model.

Finite samples do not need to match an expected ratio exactly. That explains why 96 and 94 need not be equal and why 6 and 4 need not be equal. It does not make every possible departure equally persuasive. Repeated, well-controlled evidence would strengthen the linkage inference and help exclude scoring or survival differences as explanations.

Calculate frequency using the whole sample

Both recombinant classes count: 6 + 4 = 10. Divide by all 200 scored offspring, including the parental classes: 10/200 = 0.05. Multiply by 100% to obtain 5% recombination. Dividing by 190 would compare recombinants with parentals rather than with the whole set, answering a different question.

For a short interval, the result supports an approximate distance of 5 cM. It does not specify a fixed number of DNA bases. The value is an estimate based on the scored loci and the assumptions above. Crossovers elsewhere on a chromosome do not automatically change these two-marker classes, and multiple exchanges can conceal some recombination across a longer interval.

Change the denominator without changing the observations

Of all 200 offspring, 96 are both purple and smooth: 96/200 = 48% of this sample. Among smooth-podded offspring only, the denominator is 96 + 4 = 100, so the purple fraction is 96/100 = 96%. The different percentages are both correct descriptions of this constructed dataset. They answer different questions. They are observed sample proportions, not a claim that the exact percentages must recur in every future cross.

Check whether you can transfer the method

In your own notes, explain what would change if the informative parent instead had known phase Ab/aB. Do not recalculate from the table as if the old counts belonged to that new cross. Identify the parental and recombinant gametes, explain why the tester would still be useful, and name the new evidence needed for a map estimate.

Hint for changing phase

Parental means the combinations present on the original homologues. Keep the tester's ab contribution fixed, then decide which of the informative parent's four combinations matches the new phase.

Compare your reasoning

With Ab/aB phase, Ab and aB are parental; AB and ab are recombinant. The aabb tester still contributes only ab, so offspring can reveal the other gamete under the same phenotype rules. The new cross needs its own scored counts before a recombination fraction can be calculated. Reusing 6 and 4 as recombinant counts would silently retain the old phase. A strong explanation changes the classification because of chromosome arrangement, keeps the tester mechanism unchanged, and refuses to invent missing data.

You are ready for the final check when you can explain why a method fits, carry it out with the correct contributions and denominator, and state what the result leaves unresolved. The chapter's models are tools for interpreting evidence. Their usefulness comes from knowing their conditions as well as their predictions.
