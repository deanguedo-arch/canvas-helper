Linked genes and recombinant offspring

Why do some two-trait crosses depart from independent assortment?

Learning goal

Identify linked allele arrangements, parental and recombinant combinations, and use a test cross to interpret gamete frequencies.

Before you begin

Independent AaBb gametes are expected in equal proportions. Crossing over can exchange segments between homologues.

Key terms

Words for this lesson

Use these definitions when you need help with a term.

linked genesGenes on the same chromosome that may be inherited together, especially when close.

parental combinationAn allele combination matching one of the original chromosome arrangements in the heterozygous parent.

recombinant combinationAn allele combination different from the parent’s original arrangement for the loci considered.

linkage phaseThe arrangement of alleles across the two homologous chromosomes.

First predict what independent assortment would produce

A two-trait cross can produce all four expected appearances and still give us a reason to question independent assortment. The useful evidence is not only which offspring appear. It is also how often each combination appears. We need a prediction before we can recognise a departure from it.

Use a fictional Smurf breeding model to keep that comparison concrete. Let H specify white hair and h blonde hair; HH and Hh give white hair, while hh gives blonde hair. Let N specify a large nose and n a small nose; NN and Nn give a large nose, while nn gives a small nose. These are stipulated complete-dominance rules for an imaginary organism, not a model of human hair or facial features.

The cross is HhNn × hhnn. One parent is heterozygous at both loci. The other is homozygous recessive at both. Assume ordinary equal segregation, random fertilisation, reliable expression and scoring of these phenotypes, and no selective loss of one offspring class before it is counted.

Explain the four-way expectation

If the loci assort independently, the HhNn parent's probability of contributing H is ½ and its probability of contributing N is ½. Independence means that receiving H does not change the chance of receiving N. The probability of an HN gamete is therefore ½ × ½ = ¼. The same reasoning gives Hn, hN and hn at ¼ each. Each label represents one allele at each of two loci in one haploid gamete.

The hhnn parent can contribute only hn, with probability 1. Crossing a heterozygote with a homozygous recessive individual is a two-locus test cross. Here the second parent's fixed contribution lets each offspring report which allele combination came from the heterozygote.

Read each row below from the two gametes to the offspring. Keep the H/h pair together and the N/n pair together. Apply the phenotype rules only after forming both allele pairs.

The four probabilities add to 1 and give a 1:1:1:1 phenotype ratio. Among 16 offspring, that model predicts an average of 16 × ¼ = 4 in each class; it does not guarantee four of each in every sample.

One possible explanation is that H and N tend to travel together because their loci are on the same chromosome. This small record illustrates the pattern; it does not by itself establish linkage. Chance and the assumptions about survival and scoring still need attention.

The genotype leaves a chromosome question unanswered

To investigate that explanation, switch to the general allele symbols A/a and B/b. AaBb tells us the two allele pairs present in a diploid individual. It does not tell us which alleles share a chromosome. AB/ab places A and B on one homologue and a and b on the other. Ab/aB places A with b and a with B. The slash separates the two homologous chromosomes. This arrangement is called linkage phase.

A homologous pair consists of corresponding chromosomes with the same kinds of genes at corresponding positions, or loci. The homologues can carry different versions of those genes. Genes occupying different loci on the same chromosome are linked genes; close loci often give the clearest excess of inherited original combinations.

A parental combination matches an allele arrangement already present on one of the heterozygote’s homologues. A recombinant combination differs from those original arrangements for the loci being followed. “Parental” refers to that chromosome arrangement, not to whether a label contains capital letters or resembles the whole diploid genotype of a parent.

In the figure, begin with the two horizontal chromosomes on the left. Read A and B along the blue chromosome and a and b along the orange chromosome. Compare those arrangements with the four gamete labels on the right before reading the bottom key.

The starting arrangement is AB/ab. Without crossing over, AB and ab are parental combinations; Ab and aB are recombinants. Schematic — not to scale.

The upper-right boxes contain AB and ab, so they match the starting homologues. The lower boxes contain Ab and aB, the other two combinations. The circled letters A, B, C and D are diagram-location labels, not extra alleles: locations A and B identify parental gametes, while locations C and D identify recombinant gametes. This is the chromosome information we would need for the Smurf record as well. If the heterozygote's breeding history establishes HN/hn, then HN and hn are parental and Hn and hN are recombinant. Merely being given HhNn would not establish that phase. Next we need to explain how new combinations can form without making new allele versions.

Follow an exchange between homologues

Imagine that A and B occupy two loci on one chromosome, with a and b at the corresponding loci on its homologue. If the chromosomes separate without exchanging the region between those loci, AB remains together and ab remains together. Rotating that whole homologous pair at metaphase I can change which cell receives which chromosome, but it cannot detach B from A on an unchanged chromosome.

Distinguish the four chromatids before following the exchange

DNA is copied before meiosis. The AB chromosome now has two sister chromatids carrying the copied AB arrangement, and the ab chromosome has two sisters carrying ab. Each joined pair is one copied chromosome. When the homologues pair during prophase I, the group contains four chromatids, called a tetrad.

The image below follows one crossover between the A and B loci. Locate the all-blue and all-red copied chromosomes in the top cell. Within each colour are sisters. One blue chromatid and one red chromatid belong to different homologues and are nonsister chromatids. Follow those inner chromatids down the diagram and watch the lower segment change colour.

One illustrated crossover between the tracked loci changes two of the four chromatids. Blue and red trace chromosome origins, not dominance.

The second cell is labelled the pachytene stage of prophase I. Here the paired nonsister chromatids exchange corresponding DNA segments. The label chiasma identifies the crossing-over connection in the simplified drawing. After the exchange, one originally blue chromatid carries A with b, and one originally red chromatid carries a with B. The other two chromatids retain AB and ab. The loci remain in the same order; the allele combinations have changed.

Continue through the cell labelled metaphase I to the two cells labelled metaphase II. Homologous chromosomes have separated at the first division, while sisters remain joined. There is no second DNA-replication step between the divisions. Meiosis II then separates the chromatids. At the bottom, the outside products are AB and ab; the two middle products are Ab and aB. These haploid products explain the allele combinations that can be transmitted to offspring.

One crossover in this tetrad produced two recombinant chromatids out of four. It did not make all four recombinant. That distinction will matter when we calculate frequencies: the percentage of recombinant gametes or offspring is not automatically the percentage of cells that experienced any crossover. Other meioses can have no exchange between these loci or a different pattern of exchanges.

Nor does every crossover anywhere on the chromosome change these particular labels. An exchange beyond both A and B leaves their relationship to each other unchanged. To separate their original combination with a single exchange, the exchange must fall between the loci. Recombination joins existing allele versions in a new arrangement; it does not require a mutation that turns A into a or creates a third allele.

Use a fixed tester to read the resulting contributions

Return to AaBb × aabb. The tester contributes ab in every normal gamete. Each offspring’s genotype therefore reveals the heterozygote’s contribution; its phenotype can reveal it too if A is completely dominant to a, B is completely dominant to b, both traits are reliably expressed, and the four appearances can be distinguished. Under independent assortment, those four contributions would each have probability ¼.

Read the next table in both directions. Left to right predicts an offspring from a gamete. Right to left uses a test-cross offspring to identify the heterozygote's gamete. “Dominant A form” and “dominant B form” name the stipulated appearances; neither phrase says that the allele is more likely to be transmitted.

For example, an aaBb offspring must have received aB from the heterozygote: the tester supplies one a and one b, leaving the other a and the B to be accounted for. The tester still contributes DNA and undergoes meiosis. Its value is that its contribution at the scored loci is fixed.

For an AB/ab heterozygote with linked loci, AB and ab are the original arrangements. When recombination between the loci is infrequent, those gametes and their corresponding AaBb and aabb offspring occur more often than Ab and aB gametes and their Aabb and aaBb offspring. This interpretation uses the specified phase as well as the counts.

Read the actual proportions, not just the presence of four categories. Linked genes can yield all four combinations when crossing over occurs. Four classes therefore do not establish independent assortment. Conversely, a two-class result with no observed recombinants is possible, but does not establish that exchange can never occur between the loci.

Change the arrangement while keeping AaBb

For Ab/aB, the original combinations are now Ab and aB. A crossover between the loci can yield AB and ab. A and B have not changed their dominance relationships; only which alleles began together has changed. The offspring mappings in the table remain valid, but the parental/recombinant labels attached to its rows reverse.

Try the phase change in your own notes or on paper. Start with an Ab/aB parent crossed with ab/ab. Without assuming equal gamete probabilities, identify the two parental offspring genotypes and the two recombinant offspring genotypes. Explain why the offspring with both dominant phenotypes need not be parental. This is practice with the phase distinction just taught.

Compare your phase reasoning

Ab and aB are parental gametes, so Aabb and aaBb are parental offspring classes in this test cross. AB and ab are recombinant gametes, so AaBb and aabb are recombinant classes. AaBb expresses both dominant forms, yet its AB contribution differs from either original homologue. Dominance determines appearance; the recorded chromosome arrangement determines parental status.

Check that your response names the starting phase, gives all four diploid offspring genotypes, and explains the changed classification. Calling AB parental merely because both letters are capital would answer a different starting arrangement.

Decide how much the offspring evidence supports

A crossover must occur between two loci to separate their original alleles with one exchange. A shorter interval generally offers fewer opportunities for such an exchange than a longer interval under comparable conditions. Close linked loci therefore tend to give fewer recombinant contributions. The relationship is not a universal physical ruler: recombination varies along chromosomes, and multiple exchanges can conceal some changes when only two loci are scored.

This chromosome account connects to two related ideas. The chromosome theory of inheritance places genes on chromosomes and connects their transmission with chromosome behaviour during meiosis. The more specific gene-chromosome theory places genes at particular loci arranged linearly along a chromosome. Linked genes can travel together because they occupy that same physical structure; their different recombination frequencies can then provide information about relative positions.

A small sample can differ from an expected ratio by chance. The fictional 6:2:2:6 record is consistent with an excess of the HN/hn parental combinations, but sixteen observations are not decisive proof that the loci are linked. A larger, repeated departure in the same direction provides stronger evidence, provided the cross, phase, phenotype scoring and survival assumptions are sound.

Suppose a recombinant genotype reduces survival before offspring are counted. A shortage of that class could then reflect selective loss rather than its original gamete frequency alone. Similarly, a phenotype that is sometimes misclassified can distort the record. The test cross connects offspring frequencies to gametes only when the offspring fairly represent the gametes we want to study.

When breeding history does not specify phase, two consistently large reciprocal classes can suggest the parental arrangements in the usual linked-gene test-cross model. State that inference and its assumptions. Do not treat “the two largest numbers” as the definition of parental, particularly in a small or nearly equal sample.

Keep the conclusion at the level of the evidence. Crossing over changes allele combinations within chromosomes; a test cross can reveal those combinations through offspring. A parental excess supports linkage under the stated model. Near-equal classes are consistent with independent assortment, but widely separated loci on one chromosome can also behave approximately independently. Counts alone do not always distinguish same-chromosome from different-chromosome locations.

What would a different tester hide?

Reason independently in your own notes or on paper. Keep an AaBb parent and the same complete-dominance rules, but replace aabb with AABB. List that new parent's gamete contribution. Combine it with all four possible AaBb-parent gametes. Would observing only the four offspring appearances still reveal the first parent's gamete frequencies? State whether direct genotype information could help.

Compare the changed-tester explanation

AABB contributes only AB. AB, Ab, aB and ab from the other parent would produce AABB, AABb, AaBB and AaBb respectively. Every genotype expresses the dominant form at both loci, because the tester always provides A and B. The phenotypes merge four different contributions into one appearance, so appearance counts cannot recover the gamete frequencies.

If the offspring genotypes were directly distinguished at both loci, their differences could identify the four contributions. The recessive tester is useful for the phenotype-based method because it avoids that masking under the supplied dominance rules. A fixed gamete alone is not sufficient to guarantee four visible classes. Check that your explanation includes both the tester's AB and the genotype-to-phenotype grouping; saying merely “AABB is the wrong parent” does not explain the lost information.

Worked example

Trace a test-cross record from offspring back to chromosomes

An AB/ab heterozygote is crossed with ab/ab. The supplied record reports 42 AB, 40 ab, 9 Ab and 9 aB gamete contributions inferred from its offspring. What are the parental and recombinant classes, and what does the distribution suggest? Use the same complete-dominance, reliable-scoring and equal-survival assumptions as before.

Those short labels describe the heterozygote's inferred contribution, not the entire diploid offspring. First reconstruct the actual offspring by adding the tester's ab. This prevents treating a gamete as though it carried two copies of each gene.

Reconstructing all one hundred scored test-cross offspring
Inferred contributionDiploid offspringCountClass relative to AB/ab
ABAaBb42Parental
abaabb40Parental
AbAabb9Recombinant
aBaaBb9Recombinant

The given phase, AB/ab, supplies the classification before any addition: AB and ab match the original homologues. Ab and aB do not. Adding the two parental classes gives 42 + 40 = 82. Adding both reciprocal recombinant classes gives 9 + 9 = 18. The four classes account for 42 + 40 + 9 + 9 = 100 offspring; 82 + 18 checks the same total.

Under independent assortment, each class would have probability ¼, giving an expected 100 × ¼ = 25 in each class. Combining the two parental classes would give an expectation of 50 parental and 50 recombinant offspring. The observed 82-to-18 division is a substantial parental excess. Under our stated assumptions, it supports linkage with recombination between the loci.

The nine offspring in each recombinant class do not contradict linkage. They are the evidence that some transmitted chromosomes have combinations generated by exchange. Nor do eighteen recombinant offspring mean that exactly eighteen meiotic cells experienced a crossover. We counted offspring classified at two loci, not every chromosome event in every cell.

Separate a changed sample from a changed biological explanation

Try this in your own notes or on paper. A second class receives an anonymous test-cross record: 24 AaBb, 26 aabb, 25 Aabb and 25 aaBb. The tester was aabb, but the heterozygote's phase and chromosome locations were not supplied. A student concludes, “All four classes occur, so the loci must be on different chromosomes.” Evaluate that conclusion. Give the gamete combinations supported by the record, explain what the proportions suggest, and name information the record does not establish.

Compare the evidence-based conclusion

The offspring identify AB, ab, Ab and aB contributions respectively. Their counts are close to the independent expectation of 25 each among 100 offspring, so the record is consistent with independent assortment. However, merely finding four classes would not establish that: a linked heterozygote can produce four classes too. Here it is their near-equal frequency that matters.

The record does not prove that the genes occupy different chromosomes. Loci far apart on the same chromosome can show approximately independent two-locus frequencies. It also does not reveal a clear parental phase from a strong pair of large classes, because the counts are nearly equal. A breeding history or other chromosome evidence would help. A good response separates observed offspring, inferred gametes, a supported frequency model and an unproved location claim.

The opening puzzle now has a mechanism. Independent chromosome-pair orientations justify equal gamete combinations for unlinked loci under the model. Linked loci can retain their starting combinations more often because they share a chromosome; crossing over between them can produce the other combinations. We can use the proportion of those recombinant offspring to estimate relative distances, provided we keep the counted quantity and its limits clear.
