<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l11-question"><h2>How do we know what a brain region does?</h2><p>A labelled diagram tells you where a structure is supposed to be. It does not, by itself, show what the structure does. To connect structure with function, researchers need observations that can support or challenge an explanation.</p><p>Start with three questions: <strong>What was measured? What conclusion does that measurement support? What does it leave uncertain?</strong> We will apply those questions to injury studies, stimulation, anatomical observations, and imaging.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l11-lesions"><h2>Injury can reveal a contribution, but not the whole system</h2><p>A <strong>lesion</strong> is an area of damaged tissue. Researchers compare the location of damage with changes in function. If damage involving a motor region is associated with a particular movement difficulty, that supports the idea that the region or its connections contribute to that movement.</p><p>Phineas Gage's frontal injury and reported behavioural changes became a well-known example. The useful scientific reasoning is that an injury can link damaged tissue with changes in function. The limits are also substantial: one case is not a controlled experiment, descriptions may be incomplete, and damage can interrupt connections beyond the visible region.</p><h3>Worked example: what does a lesion result justify?</h3><p>Suppose a model case describes a person who hears sounds but has difficulty understanding speech after damage near a language-comprehension region.</p><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Separate the observations.</strong> Detecting sound remains possible; understanding spoken language is impaired.</li><li><strong>Propose a focused explanation.</strong> The affected tissue or its connections contribute to comprehension rather than simply allowing sound to enter the ear.</li><li><strong>Avoid overclaiming.</strong> This does not show that all language is stored in that one region or that the person has lost all hearing.</li><li><strong>Seek additional evidence.</strong> More detailed assessments, anatomical information, and comparable cases can test the explanation.</li></ol></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l11-stimulation"><h2>Stimulation changes activity while researchers observe a response</h2><p>Wilder Penfield's work during surgery helped map sensory and motor functions by applying electrical stimulation to exposed brain regions and recording movements, sensations, and reported experiences. Unlike simply observing an existing injury, stimulation deliberately changes activity at a location.</p><p>If stimulation of a region repeatedly produces a movement, that supports a functional connection between the stimulated tissue and the movement pathway. It does not prove that the region normally acts alone. Electrical stimulation can activate nearby cells or connected pathways, and a response during stimulation is not a complete account of ordinary thinking.</p><p><strong>Compare the methods:</strong> an injury study asks what changes when tissue is damaged; stimulation asks what response follows an introduced change in activity. Agreement between different methods can strengthen an explanation because their limitations are not identical.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l11-anatomy"><h2>Anatomical observation answers “where?”</h2><p>Dissection, photographs, and models help locate structures and their relationships. A surface view can show cerebral lobes and the cerebellum. A sagittal section reveals internal structures such as the corpus callosum and thalamus. If a structure is absent from a surface view, it may simply be hidden beneath the surface.</p><p>For the <a class="text-link" data-book-page="34" href="#textbook-library">optional Investigation 11.D, p. 393</a>, the photographs provide a way to observe a mammalian brain without performing a dissection. First orient the view using the brainstem and cerebellum. Then locate the cerebrum and internal landmarks, sketch what is visible, and give one function for each labelled structure. Record whether each identification is directly visible or inferred from nearby landmarks.</p><p>A model simplifies the anatomy. A photograph shows a particular specimen and view. Neither a model's colour nor a still photograph directly measures neural activity. Follow the relevant supervised procedure if a physical specimen is used.</p><p>Using the supplied photographs is a source-image observation task. It does not establish that you performed a dissection. A physical specimen or dissection requires a separately assigned, teacher-approved procedure, materials and safety arrangements; a practical performance is not required to work through this lesson.</p></section>

<p>Read the three columns as a comparison of measurement types. The anatomy illustration concerns shape and position. The MRI illustration represents detailed structure. The PET illustration represents distribution of a labelled tracer. These schematic drawings explain the methods; they are not actual patient scans and should not be treated as experimental results.</p><p><strong>Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)</strong> uses strong magnetic fields and radiofrequency signals to obtain information from tissue and construct detailed images. A structural MRI can reveal brain anatomy and abnormalities. It does not require the ionizing radiation used by X-rays.</p><p><strong>Positron-emission tomography (PET)</strong> detects signals from an administered radioactive tracer. In the glucose-based example used here, tracer uptake provides evidence related to glucose use and metabolic activity. The scan's colours are a chosen scale for measured tracer information. Red is not a universal label meaning a particular emotion or thought.</p><p><strong>Functional MRI (fMRI)</strong> is a specialized MRI method commonly used to measure blood-oxygenation changes related to neural activity. It is an indirect measure, not a recording of each neuron's action potentials. This distinction prevents “MRI shows structure” from becoming the false claim that no MRI method can investigate function.</p><div aria-label="Scrollable comparison table" class="pro-table-wrap" role="region" tabindex="0"><table><thead><tr><th>Method</th><th>What it contributes</th><th>A limit to state</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Injury or lesion study</td><td>Links damaged tissue and altered function</td><td>Damage can affect several regions or their connections; cases differ.</td></tr><tr><td>Stimulation</td><td>Tests responses when activity is altered at a location</td><td>Stimulation can affect connected pathways, not just one isolated function.</td></tr><tr><td>Dissection or anatomical photograph</td><td>Shows structures and spatial relationships</td><td>A still anatomical view does not measure ongoing activity.</td></tr><tr><td>Structural MRI</td><td>Detailed soft-tissue anatomy</td><td>Structure alone does not establish how a person is thinking.</td></tr><tr><td>PET</td><td>Distribution of a tracer; meaning depends on the tracer</td><td>Glucose-based uptake is an indirect activity measure and requires an appropriate comparison.</td></tr><tr><td>fMRI</td><td>Blood-oxygenation changes associated with activity</td><td>An indirect physiological signal, not a direct picture of thoughts.</td></tr></tbody></table></div></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l11-method-choice"><h3>Two additional tools: EEG and CT</h3><p><strong>Electroencephalography (EEG)</strong> uses electrodes to record electrical activity at the scalp. It is useful for studying the timing of activity, but a scalp signal is the combined effect of many cells and does not pinpoint every deep source. <strong>Computed tomography (CT)</strong> combines multiple X-ray measurements to produce cross-sectional images. CT can be useful for studying brain structure.</p><p>A plain skull X-ray provides limited detail about soft brain tissue. That is why it is a weaker choice than MRI, PET, or appropriately applied stimulation in the broad comparison used here. Do not extend that comparison into “no X-ray-based technique is useful for the brain”; CT is a clear counterexample.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l11-worked-data"><h2>Work from a measurement to a restrained conclusion</h2><p><strong>Classroom model data, not a reported study:</strong> a glucose-based tracer investigation compares the same brain region during a quiet baseline and during a visual task. The scale is an arbitrary relative-uptake scale.</p><div aria-label="Scrollable comparison table" class="pro-table-wrap" role="region" tabindex="0"><table><thead><tr><th>Condition</th><th>Relative tracer uptake in the selected region</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Baseline</td><td>40 units</td></tr><tr><td>Visual task</td><td>64 units</td></tr></tbody></table></div><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Describe the evidence without interpreting it yet.</strong> Measured relative tracer uptake is 24 units higher during the task.</li><li><strong>Use the measurement's meaning.</strong> With this tracer and comparable conditions, greater uptake is consistent with greater glucose use in the region.</li><li><strong>Connect to a limited claim.</strong> The region's activity may be associated with processes involved in the task.</li><li><strong>Identify alternatives and missing controls.</strong> The task may also change attention, movement, or effort. A single comparison cannot establish which component accounts for the difference.</li><li><strong>Improve the investigation.</strong> Repeated measurements, multiple participants, and a comparison task matched for other demands could help separate the explanations.</li></ol><p>The reasoning stops well before “this coloured spot proves what the person was thinking.” A stronger conclusion requires evidence that rules out plausible alternatives.</p></section>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l11-application"><h2>Connect evidence to practical problems</h2><p>Understanding neural pathways can inform assistive technology. For example, a system can record activity associated with intended movement, convert a pattern into a control signal, and use it to move a robotic device. That engineering pathway is not identical to a biological motor neuron activating muscle. It also needs feedback about the device's actual position if movement is to be adjusted accurately.</p><p>Evaluating such a system requires more than showing one successful movement. Relevant evidence includes accuracy across repeated attempts, errors, fatigue, feedback, and which functions the user can control. Benefits and limitations both matter when judging whether a technology solves a practical problem.</p><p>Neurological disorders also connect biology to technology. Damage in myelinated pathways, movement circuits, or memory networks can produce different functional problems. A useful investigation links an identified biological change to a measured effect, then asks whether an intervention improves that effect. It should not assume that a treatment which helps a symptom has repaired every underlying cause.</p></section>
