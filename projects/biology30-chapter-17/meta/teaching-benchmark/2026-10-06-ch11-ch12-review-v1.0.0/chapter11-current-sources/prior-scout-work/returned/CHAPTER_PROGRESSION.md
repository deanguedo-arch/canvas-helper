## lesson-01 — Nervous communication

**Lesson question:** How can a change at one place produce a useful response somewhere else?

**Prerequisite handoff:** A stimulus is a detectable change; a response is what the body does. Basic cell, organ and system vocabulary from prior science.

**Concept order:** stimulus, receptor, effector → sensory input → integration → motor output → homeostasis and negative feedback → CNS/PNS; neurons, glia and nerve bundles.

**Evidence of understanding:** Separate the regulated condition from the act of responding, then explain a cooling or warming response causally.

**Forward connection:** The three jobs need cells with suitable structures; lesson 02 zooms into a neuron.

**Source support:** textbook p.366/PDF7, p.367/PDF8, p.368/PDF9, p.369/PDF10, p.370/PDF11, p.365/PDF6; teacher slides 6, 7, 15, 17, 44; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.1k, 30–A1.2k, 30–A1.3k.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l01-roles):**


> A stimulus is a change that can be detected. A receptor detects it and starts sensory signalling. An effector , usually a muscle or gland, carries out the response. These names refer to different jobs. Cold is a stimulus; a temperature-sensitive receptor is a detector; contracting muscle is part of a response.

**Why the passage matters:** A complete explanation starts with detection and connects each step to what the response accomplishes. The worked example does not begin with an unexplained command from the brain.


## lesson-02 — Neurons & myelin

**Lesson question:** How does a neuron’s structure support communication over distance?

**Prerequisite handoff:** The three communication jobs and the difference between one neuron and a nerve bundle (01).

**Concept order:** dendrite, soma, axon and terminal → simple action potential meaning before ionic detail → continuous axon; myelin segments and nodes → structure–function reasoning and failure location.

**Evidence of understanding:** Explain why branching and length are useful, then distinguish loss of insulation from failure of transmitter release.

**Forward connection:** Connected cells form a pathway; lesson 03 follows that pathway through a reflex.

**Source support:** textbook p.368/PDF9, p.370/PDF11, p.372/PDF13, p.374/PDF15, p.378/PDF19; teacher slides 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.1k, 30–A1.3k.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l02-worked):**


> Question: Why does a neuron benefit from having many short dendritic branches and one long axon? A useful answer must explain what the shape makes possible.
> 
> 1. Begin with reception. Many dendritic branches provide a large receiving surface. This allows many synaptic contacts rather than just one input.
> 2. Connect reception to integration. Inputs influence the cell body and the region where the axon begins. Combining those effects allows the outgoing signal to depend on more than one incoming message.
> 3. Connect length to distance. A long axon allows an action potential to reach a distant target while staying on one continuous cell membrane.
> 4. Account for the ending. Terminal branches provide sites where the neuron can release chemical messengers to its target cells.

**Why the passage matters:** The question demands what the shape makes possible. Each worked step connects a visible feature to a function, rather than asking students to memorize an anatomical list.


## lesson-03 — Pathways & reflexes

**Lesson question:** Why can a response begin before a conscious decision?

**Prerequisite handoff:** Neuron structure, continuous axons, terminals and the input–integration–output model (01–02).

**Concept order:** sensory/afferent, interneuron, motor/efferent → spinal withdrawal versus conscious processing → patellar and pupillary examples → cell morphology versus functional role; investigation design.

**Evidence of understanding:** Locate a break in a circuit and justify two different routes from an observed sequence. Keep the required tennis-ball response unworked.

**Forward connection:** The pathway is now clear; lesson 04 asks what permits a signal at the cell membrane.

**Source support:** textbook p.368/PDF9, p.369/PDF10, p.370/PDF11, p.371/PDF12, p.372/PDF13; teacher slides 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.3k, 30–A1.2s, 30–A1.4s.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l03-reflex):**


> Start at “stimulus,” beside the skin receptor. Follow the sensory route into the area marked spinal cord (CNS) . The boundary matters: the sensory neuron brings information into the CNS, and an interneuron processes it there. Follow the motor route back out to the muscle. The separate upward path carries information toward the brain; it is not a detour that must finish before the muscle responds.
> 
> 1. Detect the stimulus. A potentially damaging stimulus activates receptors in the skin. This starts sensory signalling rather than directly ordering the muscle to move.
> 2. Carry the information inward. A sensory neuron conducts action potentials toward the spinal cord. The direction makes it afferent.
> 3. Process within the spinal circuit. The sensory neuron communicates with interneurons that influence motor neurons. Several connections allow the response to coordinate muscles; the picture is a simplified account of that circuitry.
> 4. Carry output outward. Motor neurons signal the skeletal muscles that withdraw the body part. Because their output goes to an effector, they are efferent.
> 5. Explain the timing. This route does not wait for a conscious decision in the cerebrum. Information also ascends to the brain, allowing perception of the stimulus and further action.

**Why the passage matters:** A separate route to the brain prevents the misleading claim that a withdrawal reflex never informs the brain. The comparison must distinguish response type rather than simply response speed.


## lesson-04 — The resting neuron

**Lesson question:** How can a resting cell be ready to signal?

**Prerequisite handoff:** A continuous axon carries a signal; a membrane separates the cell from its surroundings (02–03).

**Concept order:** ions, voltage and reference outside → ion gradients versus membrane charge → selective permeability and K+ leakage → ATP-dependent 3 Na+ out : 2 K+ in pump; readiness.

**Evidence of understanding:** Interpret a negative voltage and predict an initial change using gradient, permeability and charge reasoning.

**Forward connection:** Lesson 05 changes channel permeability and follows one action potential.

**Source support:** textbook p.373/PDF14, p.374/PDF15, p.375/PDF16; teacher slides 21, 22, 23; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.1k, 30–A1.2s, 30–A1.3s.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l04-voltage):**


> Membrane potential is the voltage inside relative to outside. We use the outside as our reference. A reading of −70 mV means that the inside is 70 millivolts lower in electrical potential than the outside. It does not mean there are no positive ions inside, or that the entire cell is filled with a large net negative charge.

**Why the passage matters:** The explanation tells a student what the minus sign compares before asking them to reason with voltage. It separates a measured potential difference from wholesale concentration reversal.


## lesson-05 — Action potentials

**Lesson question:** How does a brief voltage change propagate without becoming a larger spike?

**Prerequisite handoff:** Membrane potential, gradients, channels, charge movement and the continuous pump (04).

**Concept order:** depolarization and threshold → Na+ entry/inactivation; delayed K+ exit → hyperpolarization and refractory intervals → local regeneration, saltatory conduction → frequency versus amplitude versus recruitment.

**Evidence of understanding:** Read voltage-over-time and distinguish changes in spike frequency from the number of active neurons; state the assumptions in a model.

**Forward connection:** Lesson 06 asks what changes when the impulse reaches a gap between cells.

**Source support:** textbook p.374/PDF15, p.376/PDF17, p.377/PDF18, p.378/PDF19, p.403/PDF44; teacher slides 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.1k, 30–A1.3s.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l05-graph):**


> In panel A, the horizontal axis is time in milliseconds and the vertical axis is membrane potential in millivolts. The resting line is about −70 mV and the dashed threshold line is about −55 mV. The peak in this illustration is labelled about +30 mV. Other examples may use +35 or +40 mV; these are representative values, not a different underlying process.
> 
> 1. Resting state: the gradients are already present. Voltage-gated channels are not producing an action potential, although leak channels and pumps continue working.
> 2. Rapid depolarization: Na⁺ permeability rises as voltage-gated sodium channels open. Na⁺ enters, making the inside less negative and then positive relative to outside.
> 3. The rise stops: sodium channels become inactivated. Their temporary inability to conduct is different from simply being closed and ready to open. Delayed opening of voltage-gated K⁺ channels also changes the balance of current.
> 4. Repolarization: K⁺ moves outward through those channels. Positive charge leaves, so the membrane potential falls toward its resting value.
> 5. Hyperpolarization: some K⁺ channels remain open briefly, allowing an undershoot below the resting potential. As they close and normal resting conductances dominate again, the voltage returns toward resting level.

**Why the passage matters:** The graph is read as causes in time at one membrane region, before propagation in space. This prevents the common mistake of reading the horizontal time axis as distance down an axon.


## lesson-06 — Synapses & summation

**Lesson question:** How does the signal cross a chemical synapse, and what decides whether the next neuron fires?

**Prerequisite handoff:** Action potential propagation and threshold (05); terminals and separate cells (02).

**Concept order:** presynaptic/post­synaptic and cleft → Ca2+, vesicle fusion, diffusion, receptor binding and clearance → acetylcholine versus cholinesterase → EPSP/IPSP and receptor context → spatial and temporal summation.

**Evidence of understanding:** Trace a release failure, distinguish it from a receptor failure, and explain why closely spaced inputs can reach threshold.

**Forward connection:** Lesson 07 changes one of these steps to predict a drug effect.

**Source support:** textbook p.378/PDF19, p.379/PDF20, p.380/PDF21, p.381/PDF22, p.382/PDF23, p.383/PDF24; teacher slides 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.1k, 30–A1.2s, 30–A1.3s.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l06-sequence):**


> Begin at the axon entering the top of panel A. The large arrow follows the arriving action potential toward the terminal. The terminal boundary separates the presynaptic cytoplasm from the cleft. The boundary below the gap belongs to the receiving cell. Small messenger dots cross the gap; the vesicles that held them do not travel into the next cell.

**Why the passage matters:** The conditional continuation distinguishes an arriving impulse from a guaranteed postsynaptic action potential. The worked additive model also states its limit rather than claiming a universal neuron equation.


## lesson-07 — Drugs at the synapse

**Evidence of understanding:** Predict downstream consequences, distinguish competing mechanisms and explain what an experiment does not yet establish.

**Forward connection:** Lesson 08 traces signals into different peripheral effectors.

## lesson-08 — Peripheral control

**Lesson question:** Why can one situation produce skeletal movement and several involuntary organ responses?

**Prerequisite handoff:** Signal direction and cell communication (01–07); skeletal, smooth and cardiac muscle distinctions are taught in this lesson.

**Concept order:** location versus functional classification → somatic and autonomic effectors → sympathetic and parasympathetic organ patterns → ganglia and transmitter paths → polygraph evidence limitations.

**Evidence of understanding:** Classify by the effector rather than by whether a movement felt automatic; compare organ changes and evaluate a non-specific stress measurement.

**Forward connection:** Lesson 09 follows incoming and outgoing routes through the CNS.

**Source support:** textbook p.367/PDF8, p.371/PDF12, p.396/PDF37, p.397/PDF38, p.398/PDF39, p.399/PDF40; teacher slides 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.2k, 30–A1.3k, 30–A1.2sts.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l08-effectors):**


> Somatic motor neurons activate skeletal muscles, such as the muscles that move your fingers and legs. Many such movements are voluntary, but skeletal muscles also participate in reflexes. A knee-jerk response is involuntary and still uses somatic motor output.

**Why the passage matters:** Automatic does not automatically mean autonomic. The worked foot/heart contrast and guided reflex comparison force students to use the actual target tissue.


## lesson-09 — Central control

**Lesson question:** How can the CNS relay, integrate and protect information processing?

**Prerequisite handoff:** CNS/PNS, pathways, synapses and myelinated axons (01–08).

**Concept order:** grey/white composition and location → ascending/descending routes, dorsal/ventral → local reflexes versus interrupted long pathways → bone, meninges, CSF and blood–brain barrier → sampling different compartments.

**Evidence of understanding:** Predict an injury’s possible route effects within explicit assumptions and distinguish mechanical protection from controlled exchange.

**Forward connection:** Lesson 10 locates specialized contributions within the brain.

**Source support:** textbook p.385/PDF26, p.386/PDF27, p.388/PDF29, p.389/PDF30, p.395/PDF36, p.402/PDF43; teacher slides 50, 51, 52, 53, 54; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.2k, 30–A1.3k, 30–A1.1sts.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l09-injury):**


> Sensory information can travel upward in ascending pathways . Commands from the brain can travel downward in descending pathways . Peripheral nerves connect these CNS pathways to body regions. In a simple spinal-root model, sensory fibres enter dorsally and motor fibres leave ventrally; a spinal nerve can carry both.

**Why the passage matters:** The prediction follows the paths that cross the damaged region, rather than treating a higher injury as magically more severe in every possible case.


## lesson-10 — The brain

**Lesson question:** How do specialized structures cooperate in a coordinated response?

**Prerequisite handoff:** CNS tissue, tracts, effectors and integration (08–09).

**Concept order:** hindbrain/midbrain/forebrain spatial map → cerebellum, medulla, pons, thalamus, hypothalamus → cerebrum, cortex and four lobes → motor/somatosensory cortex and crossing pathways → language networks and corpus callosum; optional structures.

**Evidence of understanding:** Use structure, evidence and pathway reasoning rather than a one-region-per-behaviour rule; integrate several functions in a Braille task.

**Forward connection:** Lesson 11 asks how these structure–function claims can be investigated.

**Source support:** textbook p.386/PDF27, p.387/PDF28, p.389/PDF30, p.390/PDF31, p.391/PDF32, p.392/PDF33, p.395/PDF36, p.402/PDF43, p.403/PDF44; teacher slides 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.2k, 30–A1.1sts.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l10-cortex):**


> Find the primary motor area in the frontal lobe, just in front of the central boundary. It contributes to voluntary movement. Just behind that boundary, in the parietal lobe, the primary somatosensory area processes information such as touch from the body. The areas lie close together, but receiving sensory information and producing motor output are different jobs.

**Why the passage matters:** The motor/sensory contrast locates each strip and explains what information it carries. Laterality is linked to crossing pathways, not asserted as a slogan about all brain functions.


## lesson-11 — Studying the brain

**Lesson question:** What does each investigation actually measure, and what can we conclude?

**Prerequisite handoff:** Brain structures and network contributions (09–10); source versus inference distinction developed throughout.

**Concept order:** lesion evidence and stimulation → surface/cross-section observation → structural MRI versus PET uptake → CT/EEG extensions; fMRI distinction → model data, comparisons, limitations and technology.

**Evidence of understanding:** Choose a method that answers the stated question, distinguish a supplied model value from a measured study result, and constrain an interpretation.

**Forward connection:** Lesson 12 reconstructs the whole chapter using this standard of explanation and evidence.

## lesson-12 — Chapter review and final check

**Lesson question:** Can we connect system, cell, mechanism and evidence in one explanation?

**Prerequisite handoff:** Lessons 01–11, including distinction between naming and explaining.

**Concept order:** integrated withdrawal pathway → relationships in the graphic organizer → single-neuron versus whole-system models → callosal communication; qualified clinical examples → fresh feedback task and deliberate review choices.

**Evidence of understanding:** Produce a causal explanation from receptor through CNS to effector and evaluate its limits; do not treat a completion flag as proof of independent mastery.

**Forward connection:** Optional lesson 13 applies mechanisms and evidence limits to broader claims; the required sequence is complete at 12.

**Source support:** textbook p.367/PDF8, p.370/PDF11, p.374/PDF15, p.377/PDF18, p.378/PDF19, p.379/PDF20, p.380/PDF21, p.385/PDF26, p.389/PDF30, p.391/PDF32, p.394/PDF35, p.401/PDF42, p.402/PDF43, p.403/PDF44; teacher slides 21, 25, 28, 32, 37, 44, 50, 57, 58, 66; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.1k, 30–A1.2k, 30–A1.3k, 30–A1.3s, 30–A1.4s.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l12-integrated):**


> A person accidentally touches a sharp object and rapidly withdraws the hand. Explain both the visible response and the unseen mechanism needed for it.
> 
> 1. State the stimulus and detection. The sharp stimulus activates sensory receptors in the skin. Detection starts signalling in an afferent pathway.
> 2. Follow the pathway into the CNS. Sensory neurons carry information to the spinal cord, where interneurons contribute to the withdrawal circuit. A separate ascending route also carries information toward the brain.
> 3. Explain conduction within a neuron. Existing ion gradients and selective permeability establish resting conditions. Reaching threshold produces rapid Na⁺ entry, followed by sodium-channel inactivation and outward K⁺ movement. Neighbouring ready membrane regenerates the action potential; myelination speeds conduction by allowing regeneration at nodes.
> 4. Explain a chemical junction. An arriving action potential opens terminal Ca²⁺ channels. Ca²⁺ entry triggers vesicle fusion and transmitter release. The transmitter crosses the cleft, binds receptors, and changes the receiving cell's activity. Its inputs must produce sufficient net excitation for that neuron to fire.
> 5. Follow output to the effector. Motor neurons activate skeletal muscles. ACh at the neuromuscular junction helps trigger muscle excitation and contraction, withdrawing the hand.

**Why the passage matters:** The review revisits the same response at different scales with full reasoning. It does not substitute the organizer or an optional video for explanatory teaching.


## lesson-13 — Stress, experience and addiction

**Lesson question:** How far can a nervous-system mechanism support a broader claim?

**Prerequisite handoff:** Peripheral regulation (08), brain networks (10), synaptic change (06–07), and evidence limits (11–12).

**Concept order:** perceived threats and autonomic response → experience, plasticity and association versus causation → addiction through learning and biological change → context, non-determinism and no personal disclosure.

**Evidence of understanding:** Improve an overclaim using mechanism, an alternative and an evidence limit, without diagnosing anyone or submitting personal history.

**Forward connection:** Return to chapter review or All My Work; no new required check and no next-chapter work is authorized.

**Source support:** textbook p.382/PDF23, p.383/PDF24, p.397/PDF38, p.398/PDF39, p.399/PDF40; teacher slides 49, 62, 63, 64, 65; curriculum p.52/PDF2 knowledge or STS and p.53/PDF3 skills: 30–A1.2sts, 30–A1.1sts, 30–A1.4s.

**Exact exemplar passage (#pro-l13-addiction):**


> Some drugs alter signalling in circuits involved in reward-related learning and motivation. Dopamine can contribute to reinforcement: outcomes and cues become associated with behaviour, influencing what is sought again. With repeated exposure, signalling and behaviour can change. That does not reduce addiction to a single chemical, a single learning event, or a judgement about a person's character.

**Why the passage matters:** The lesson makes students compare two incomplete explanations rather than choosing a moral label for a person. Evidence and individual prediction remain separate.
