<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l07-normal"><h2>A changed synapse is a test of the normal one</h2><p>If a drug changes behaviour or movement, “it affects the brain” is only a starting statement. A biological explanation identifies <strong>what step changes, how signalling changes, and what the affected pathway normally does</strong>. The same increase in chemical signalling can have different effects in an excitatory and an inhibitory pathway.</p><p>Reconstruct the normal sequence first: transmitter is synthesized and stored; an action potential and Ca²⁺ entry trigger release; transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds receptors; it is then cleared. A drug may alter any of these steps, or affect conduction along the axon before the signal even reaches a synapse.</p>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l07-mechanisms"><h2>Different mechanisms can produce similar-looking effects</h2><div aria-label="Scrollable comparison table" class="pro-table-wrap" role="region" tabindex="0"><table><thead><tr><th>Changed step</th><th>Possible mechanism</th><th>First consequence to explain</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Synthesis or storage</td><td>Less transmitter is made or stored in vesicles</td><td>Less may be available for later release.</td></tr><tr><td>Release</td><td>Release is increased or vesicle fusion is blocked</td><td>More or less transmitter reaches the cleft.</td></tr><tr><td>Receptor activation</td><td>An agonist activates a receptor; an antagonist blocks activation</td><td>The receiving cell responds differently even if normal transmitter release continues.</td></tr><tr><td>Clearance</td><td>Reuptake or breakdown is slowed; breakdown is increased</td><td>The chemical signal may last longer or be shortened.</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>An <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="agonist" data-bio-term-route="lesson-07" type="button">agonist</button></strong> activates a receptor. An <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="antagonist" data-bio-term-route="lesson-07" type="button">antagonist</button></strong> prevents its normal activation. A reuptake blocker need not activate the receptor itself: it can increase the time released transmitter remains available. These mechanisms should not be treated as interchangeable.</p><p><strong>Stimulant</strong> and <strong>depressant</strong> describe broad effects on nervous-system activity. A depressant is not simply a substance that makes someone feel sad. Nor does every receptor blocker depress the whole nervous system: blocking an inhibitory input can make a receiving neuron more likely to fire. Always establish what the affected pathway normally does.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l07-worked-reuptake"><h2>Worked example: slow the removal of dopamine</h2><p>Cocaine can block dopamine reuptake into the presynaptic neuron. Explain how that changes communication, without saying that it creates an extra-tall action potential.</p><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Name the normal process.</strong> Released dopamine binds receptors and is normally cleared partly by transporters that take it back up.</li><li><strong>Locate the change.</strong> Reuptake is blocked. This changes clearance, not the initial size of the action potential in the axon.</li><li><strong>Predict the immediate consequence.</strong> Dopamine remains available in the cleft longer and can continue influencing its receptors.</li><li><strong>Connect to the circuit.</strong> Dopamine participates in reward-related learning as well as other functions. Altered signalling can strongly reinforce behaviour, but the chemical name alone does not explain every effect of the drug.</li><li><strong>Limit the inference.</strong> A mechanism for reinforcement is not proof that every person develops addiction after one exposure. A hypothesis about a possible pathway needs evidence about repeated behaviour and other influences.</li></ol><p>This is how to handle an explanation question: follow the normal step, change that step, then follow the consequences. Keep broader claims proportional to the evidence.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l07-contrast"><h2>Contrast two ways to disrupt acetylcholine signalling</h2><p><strong>Botulinum toxin reduces acetylcholine release</strong> at peripheral cholinergic terminals, including skeletal neuromuscular junctions. The action potential may arrive, but the normal release process fails. The muscle receives less excitation and can become weak or paralyzed. This is a problem on the sending side; it is not best explained as toxin occupying the muscle's ACh receptor.</p><p><strong>Sarin and some insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase activity.</strong> ACh is not broken down normally, so stimulation can persist. Excessive cholinergic activation disrupts normal coordinated function and can ultimately produce weakness or paralysis as well as initial excessive activity. Similar severe outcomes do not establish that two substances act by the same mechanism.</p><p><strong>Worked reasoning:</strong> release failure means less ACh arrives at receptors. Breakdown failure means ACh remains available longer after it arrives. Both disturb normal movement, but one reduces the usual message while the other prevents it ending normally. A useful comparison names that difference before naming an outcome.</p></section>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l07-conduction"><h2>A drug can also affect the signal before the synapse</h2><p>Local anaesthetics provide a useful comparison: they can block voltage-gated sodium channels, preventing normal action-potential conduction along affected axons. With insufficient Na⁺ entry, the next section does not undergo the normal regenerative depolarization. A signal may therefore fail to reach a terminal even if the terminal's vesicles and the receiving cell's receptors are intact.</p><p>Nicotine, in contrast, activates certain acetylcholine receptors. Caffeine is a familiar stimulant that blocks adenosine receptors, reducing some signalling that normally promotes sleepiness. These examples show why “all stimulants release more transmitter” is too simple. You are not expected to infer a substance's exact action from its category or from a person's appearance.</p></section>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l07-inhibition"><h3>One last comparison: what if the normal input is inhibitory?</h3><p>Imagine a neuron receiving an inhibitory GABA input and several excitatory inputs. Reducing the inhibitory receptor response removes some opposition to firing. The neuron may now reach threshold more readily. This is <strong>disinhibition</strong>. It explains why a statement such as “a blocker always lowers nervous activity” is unreliable without identifying the target.</p><p>For the <a class="text-link" data-book-page="24" href="#textbook-library">optional Thought Lab 11.1, p. 383</a>, build your explanation around one mechanism, its predicted effect, and evidence that could test it. Broader addiction questions can also be explored in the optional extension. No personal experience is needed for either task.</p><p><strong>Carry forward:</strong> we can predict how cells communicate and how that communication fails. Next we will organize those cells into the peripheral pathways that control different effectors.</p></section>
