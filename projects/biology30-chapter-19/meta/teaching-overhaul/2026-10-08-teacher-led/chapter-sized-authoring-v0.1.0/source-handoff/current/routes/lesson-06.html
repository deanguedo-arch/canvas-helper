Natural selection and non-random mating

How do survival and mating success affect inherited variation?

Learning goal

Explain selection through differential reproductive success and distinguish it from mate-pairing effects.

Before you begin

Allele frequency describes copies in a population. A phenotype can affect survival or reproduction in a particular environment.

Key terms

Words for this lesson

Use these definitions when you need help with a term.

Differential reproductive success associated with heritable variation in a particular environment.
Reproductive contribution compared with other genotypes or phenotypes in the relevant setting.
Selection associated with differences in mating success.
Mating patterns that depend on characteristics related to the genotype being studied.

Follow reproduction, not intention

Individuals vary, and some variation is heritable. If a heritable trait affects survival or reproduction, the associated alleles may be represented differently in the next generation. describes that differential reproductive contribution.

The environment matters. A trait advantageous in one setting may have little benefit or a cost elsewhere. Fitness therefore refers to contribution to future generations in context, not simply strength, speed or size.

Selection does not make an organism deliberately produce a needed mutation. Variation must exist or arise independently before its consequences can affect reproductive success.

Distinguish survival from mating success

A trait can increase survival but reduce mating opportunities, or increase mating success while imposing a survival cost. focuses on differences in obtaining mates and reproducing.

For example, a conspicuous display might attract mates while also increasing exposure to predators. The overall evolutionary effect depends on the balance of reproductive consequences, not on whether the display appears efficient to an observer.

A claim that selection occurred needs evidence relating heritable differences to reproductive outcomes. A change in frequency alone can also arise through chance or movement between populations.

Pairing patterns and allele totals are different

changes who pairs with whom. It can change genotype frequencies, such as increasing homozygosity, without directly changing allele frequencies if all alleles retain the same overall reproductive contribution.

Sexual selection and non- are related but not identical statements. When mating success differs, some individuals contribute more offspring. When only pairing patterns differ, allele totals need not immediately change.

Use the information in the case to identify what varies: pairing, survival, number of offspring, or several together.

Worked example

A mating advantage with a survival cost

A hypothetical heritable display makes males more visible to predators but substantially increases successful mating.

  1. The survival effect is a possible cost.
  2. The mating effect is a possible advantage.
  3. Assess overall reproductive contribution rather than judging only survival.
  4. If display-associated variants contribute more descendants, sexual selection can favour them.
  5. The conclusion depends on measured reproductive outcomes in the stated environment.
