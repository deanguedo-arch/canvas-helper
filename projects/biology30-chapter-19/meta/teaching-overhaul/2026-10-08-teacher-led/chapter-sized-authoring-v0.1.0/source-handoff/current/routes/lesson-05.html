Using population data to test a prediction

How can the same allele frequencies produce different genotype patterns?

Learning goal

Compare observed and expected genotype counts without claiming that one mismatch identifies its cause.

Before you begin

Direct counts yield observed allele frequencies. The Hardy–Weinberg model predicts genotype proportions from those frequencies.

Calculate the observed allele pool first

Suppose a sample has 40 AA, 20 Aa and 40 aa individuals. There are 100 individuals and 200 allele copies. A copies total 80 + 20 = 100, so p = 0.50; q is also 0.50.

These allele frequencies are directly observed from genotype counts. Under Hardy–Weinberg, they predict 25 AA, 50 Aa and 25 aa individuals in a sample of 100. The observed sample contains fewer heterozygotes than that prediction.

A genotype mismatch is not a unique explanation

A shortage of heterozygotes could be associated with non-, structure or other processes. The counts alone do not uniquely establish which mechanism acted. Sampling and measurement also need consideration.

Notice that the observed sample and the predicted distribution have the same p and q. Genotype proportions can differ while allele frequencies remain the same. Non-random mating can alter genotype proportions without necessarily changing allele frequencies by itself.

Keep two questions separate: do genotype proportions fit the random-mating model, and have allele frequencies changed across generations? They require related but different comparisons.

Use consistent sampling and restrained conclusions

To compare generations, sample the same defined population and locus using comparable methods. A change in p or q supports allele-frequency change at that locus, but a different sampling location or age group can complicate interpretation.

Agreement with a prediction is not proof that every Hardy–Weinberg condition is perfectly satisfied. Opposing effects can sometimes balance, and finite samples have limited resolution. A visible mismatch is a reason to investigate, not permission to name a mechanism without evidence.

These activities use direct comparisons and arithmetic. Formal statistical testing may be useful in research, but no unsupported significance claim is needed here.

Worked example

Same alleles, different pairing

Population A has 25 AA, 50 Aa and 25 aa individuals. Population B has 50 AA, 0 Aa and 50 aa.

  1. Both populations have N = 100 and 200 allele copies.
  2. Both contain 100 A and 100 a copies, so p = q = 0.50.
  3. Population A matches the 1:2:1 genotype prediction for p = q = 0.50.
  4. Population B has the same allele frequencies but no heterozygotes.
  5. Allele totals alone do not establish random genotype pairing.
