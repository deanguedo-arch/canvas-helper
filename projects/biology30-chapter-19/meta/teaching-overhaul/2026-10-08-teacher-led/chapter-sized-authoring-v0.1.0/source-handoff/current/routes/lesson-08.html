Gene flow and mutation

How do alleles enter a population or arise for the first time?

Learning goal

Distinguish movement of existing alleles from the creation of new sequence variants.

Before you begin

A founder event samples a source population. Continued movement and new mutations can also alter genetic composition.

Track successful contributions, not movement alone

transfers alleles between populations. It can occur when individuals migrate and reproduce, or when gametes such as pollen move between populations. The important event is a genetic contribution to the recipient .

An animal passing through a habitat without reproducing does not necessarily alter the next generation’s allele pool. Movement can create an opportunity, but successful reproduction connects that movement with inherited change.

Combine copies with the correct weights

Suppose 80 resident diploid breeders have A frequency 0.25 and 20 immigrant breeders have A frequency 0.75. If all contribute equally, residents supply 160 copies including 40 A; immigrants supply 40 copies including 30 A. The combined frequency is 70/200 = 0.35.

Do not take a simple average of 0.25 and 0.75 when the groups differ in size. Weight each frequency by the number of allele copies contributed. The equal-contribution assumption also matters: different reproductive success would change the weights.

Gene flow can add variation within a population and reduce genetic differences between populations, although the actual effect depends on their starting allele pools and contributions.

Mutation supplies new variants

Mutation changes DNA sequence and can create new alleles. A heritable mutation must enter the reproductive lineage to affect later generations. Many mutations have little effect in a particular setting; others can be harmful or beneficial.

Mutation and selection play different roles. Mutation generates sequence differences without knowing future need. Selection can favour or remove variants according to their reproductive consequences. Drift can also alter their frequencies, especially when a new variant is rare.

Isolation and habitat fragmentation can reduce opportunities for gene flow. A conservation argument about connectivity should therefore look for movement, successful reproduction and genetic evidence rather than assuming that a structure’s presence proves its effect.

Worked example

Weight two contributing groups

A recipient population has 60 breeders at p = 0.20. Forty immigrants have p = 0.70. Assume equal contribution per diploid breeder.

  1. Residents contribute 120 allele copies, including 24 A.
  2. Immigrants contribute 80 copies, including 56 A.
  3. Combined A copies = 80 of 200, so p = 0.40.
  4. The larger resident group receives the larger weight.
  5. The result predicts the combined contribution pool under the equal-contribution assumption.
