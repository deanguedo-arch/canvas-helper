Populations, gene pools and genetic variation

What changes when a population evolves?

Learning goal

Define a gene pool and distinguish change in a population from change within an individual.

Before you begin

Individuals carry alleles. Inheritance passes alleles between generations, while population size can also change.

Define the population you are studying

genetics studies the genetic composition of populations and how that composition changes. A population consists of individuals of the same species within a defined place and time. Those boundaries matter: counting one breeding colony is not the same as counting every colony across a continent.

The is the collection of alleles present in that population. An individual carries only part of the population’s variation. At an ordinary diploid autosomal locus, each individual contributes two allele copies.

Track proportions across generations

is a change in allele frequencies across generations. An allele frequency is its share of all allele copies at the locus. It is a proportion, not simply the number of individuals showing a trait.

Suppose a population doubles in size while every allele’s proportion stays the same. The number of copies increases, but the allele frequencies have not changed. Conversely, frequencies can change even if the total population size remains constant.

An individual’s body can grow, develop or adjust to conditions. Those changes are not themselves evidence that a population’s inherited allele frequencies have changed.

Distinguish genetic and environmental differences

can influence phenotypes, but environment and gene regulation also matter. A plant grown in shade may differ from a genetically similar plant grown in bright light. That observation alone does not identify an allele-frequency change.

To study evolution at a locus, specify the alleles and measure or infer their frequencies with appropriate evidence. Later lessons explain when genotype counts permit direct counting and when a mathematical model is required.

Do not assume every genetic difference is advantageous, or that evolution always moves toward greater size or complexity. The mechanisms can include selection and chance.

Worked example

Count change versus frequency change

A population grows from 100 to 200 diploid individuals. Allele A rises from 60 of 200 copies to 120 of 400 copies.

  1. At the first sampling, A frequency is 60/200 = 0.30.
  2. At the second, it is 120/400 = 0.30.
  3. The number of A copies doubled but its proportion did not.
  4. These measurements show no allele-frequency change at this locus over the interval.
