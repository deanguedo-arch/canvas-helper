What changes when cells form germ layers?

During , cell number increased, and a developed a cavity, inner cell mass and outer trophoblast. Those events prepared different cell populations, but they did not yet make the full range of body tissues. The embryo now needs cells arranged in positions from which different tissues can develop.

During , cells move and reorganize to establish three primary germ layers: , and . Cells in the embryonic disc move inward and spread into new positions. Some establish the inner endoderm; others form mesoderm between that inner layer and cells remaining at the surface, which become ectoderm. The resulting three-layer embryo is called a gastrula. A germ layer is an early tissue-forming cell population; “germ” here does not mean a microbe. Ecto- suggests outer, meso- middle and endo- inner. The names help orient the embryonic layers, not locate every resulting tissue in an adult.

In human development, gastrulation begins in the third week after . An earlier two-layer embryonic disc, a flattened arrangement of early embryonic cells, forms during the second week. The textbook's p. 511 sequence and the teacher's week-two heading compress these changes; distinguish that earlier disc from the three definitive germ layers formed through gastrulation. This timing is counted from fertilization, not from the clinical dating of a pregnancy.

Gastrulation is therefore a change in organization and developmental pathways, rather than simply another increase in cell number. The three layers then participate in morphogenesis, the development of body form and structures. Later folding and movement bring their descendants into complex arrangements.
