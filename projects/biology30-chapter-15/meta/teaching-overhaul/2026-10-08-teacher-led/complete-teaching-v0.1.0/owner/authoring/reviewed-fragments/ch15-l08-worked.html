Worked example

Compare the stage before comparing the effect

Two hypothetical records describe exposure to the same agent at the same dose. In Record A, a named structure is being assembled. In Record B, that structure has formed but its growth and functional maturation continue. A learner says B must be harmless because it is later. Evaluate that conclusion.

  1. Identify what is held constant. Agent and dose are the same in the stated model. That makes timing a useful comparison, but it does not establish identical susceptibility.
  2. Identify the process in A. Parts of the structure are being organized. An interference at this stage could affect its formation or arrangement.
  3. Identify the process in B. Growth and maturation are continuing. An influence could affect size or function even though the initial structure already exists.
  4. Use the chart as a qualitative check. Some bands, particularly the central nervous system, extend into the fetal period. Later development is not a universal no-effect interval.
  5. Bound the conclusion. The records show why effects may differ with timing. They do not establish an actual injury, exact severity or harmlessness in either case.

Before applying that method to numbered weeks, align the age conventions. If an illustrative record gives gestational age of 14 weeks and explicitly assumes fertilization two weeks after LMP, the age after fertilization is 14 − 2 = 12 weeks. Check by adding back: 12 + 2 = 14. If the starting convention or offset is not supplied, do not silently assume an exact conversion. Then identify the process active at the stated developmental time, rather than using the week number alone as an answer.
