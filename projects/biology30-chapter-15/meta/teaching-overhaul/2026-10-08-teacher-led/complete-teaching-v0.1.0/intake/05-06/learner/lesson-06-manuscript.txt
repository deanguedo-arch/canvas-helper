Organogenesis and neurulation
How do tissue-forming layers become organized into organs?
Learning goal

Before you begin

Key terms

differentiationThe development of specialized cell structures and functions.morphogenesisThe development of the shape and organization of body structures.organogenesisThe formation of early organ structures from developing tissues.neurulationFormation of the neural tube from specialized ectoderm.More cells, different jobs and a changing shape
Cell division increases cell number, but number alone does not explain an organ. Cells also become specialized and arranged into structures. A sheet with the usual number of cells can still have the wrong shape if its cells do not move or fold appropriately.
During differentiation, cells develop specialized structures and functions. Different patterns of gene expression mean that cells use inherited information to make different functional products, including proteins. A developing muscle cell and nerve cell generally retain the same inherited genome but use it differently. Differentiation does not require another fertilization event or a new chromosome set for each type of cell.
The development of shape and organization is called morphogenesis. Cell movements, changes in cell shape and folding help turn tissue regions into organized structures. Organogenesis is the formation of early organs from developing tissues. These terms identify different aspects of development, but the processes overlap: cells may divide, specialize and change arrangement within the same developing organ.

How does an outer tissue become an internal tube?
During neurulation, a region of ectoderm becomes specialized as the neural plate. A nearby mesodermal rod called the notochord helps organize development through signals to surrounding tissue. In A, locate the notochord beneath the plate. The notochord and neural tube have different origins and roles; they are not two names for the future spinal cord.
In B, the edges of the neural plate rise as neural folds, leaving a groove between them. In C, follow the folds toward the centre. They meet and fuse, enclosing a hollow space. In the following panel, the neural tube has separated from the surface ectoderm above it. This folding and separation explains how tissue that began near the surface comes to lie underneath it.
The neural tube gives rise to the brain and spinal cord, the central nervous system. Its cavity is not the amniotic cavity around the embryo, and the tube is not a blood vessel. At this stage it is a developing foundation for the nervous system, not an already mature brain or spinal cord.
The source figure also shows cells near the folds that migrate elsewhere. These neural crest cells are a different population from the cells retained in the neural tube. They contribute to structures such as peripheral nerves, outside the brain and spinal cord, and some craniofacial bone in the head and face. The source label “bones and muscle” is too broad to use as a general origin rule for skeletal muscle: most skeletal muscle arises from mesoderm. This does not mean that neural crest can never contribute to any kind of muscle. For the neurulation sequence here, distinguish the migrating cells from the plate, folds and tube that we are following.
Human neural-tube formation occurs early, mainly in the third and fourth weeks after fertilization. Closure proceeds along the developing structure; it is not one simultaneous snap shut everywhere. A change in folding or closure can affect tissue organization, but its location and timing matter. The diagram does not establish a particular medical outcome from an unspecified disruption.
An early structure still has to mature
Basic organ structures become established during the embryonic period, through about eight weeks after fertilization. From the beginning of week nine until birth, the developing organism is called a fetus. Growth, differentiation and functional maturation continue. Saying that an organ has begun to form is different from saying that it can already perform all its later functions.
Consider an early lung bud. Its appearance shows that lung development has begun. It does not yet provide the mature gas-exchange surfaces and coordinated function needed for independent breathing outside the uterus. During prenatal development, oxygen reaches fetal blood through placental exchange and returns toward the fetus in the umbilical circulation. The developing lungs are not obtaining oxygen by breathing outside air.
The same evidence rule applies to other images. A limb bud is evidence of a developing limb, not proof of a fully coordinated hand. A visible eye structure is not proof of mature vision. Textbook p. 514's “eyes open” wording describes an early appearance before mature eyelids and visual function; do not read it as an embryo seeing normally. The source's broad organ-percentage claim likewise does not measure the percentage of adult function already achieved.
Developmental timelines use approximate landmarks. A diagram or source table may establish that a structure is visible at a stated stage, while leaving its functional ability unmeasured. Keep stage names, anatomical appearance and demonstrated function separate. This also prevents a normal-looking outline from being used as proof that every internal process is complete.

Worked example
Separate a visible change from a functional claim
A classroom observation record gives three findings: a region of tissue folds into a hollow tube; some cells begin making different proteins and develop new functions; and a small limb bud becomes visible. A student labels all three findings “growth” and concludes that development is complete. How should the record be interpreted?
Classify the folding. Changing a sheet into a tube is a change in shape and organization, so it illustrates morphogenesis. If the stated tissue is the neural plate and its folds close, that particular process is neurulation. Shape alone without tissue identity would not identify every tube as a neural tube.
Classify the new cell functions. Different proteins and specialized activities illustrate differentiation. They describe what cells do, not simply how many cells exist.
Interpret the limb bud. It establishes that limb formation is under way as part of organogenesis. It does not establish mature muscles, joints or coordinated movement.
Check the conclusion. The findings describe ongoing developmental processes. None proves completion, and later growth and maturation are still necessary.
Now apply the reasoning to the actual neural-tube figure. Follow surface ectoderm into a plate, raised folds and a tube below the surface. The changed position is the result of morphogenesis; it does not change the tissue's ectodermal origin. That tube can develop into the brain and spinal cord even though those structures are internal later.
