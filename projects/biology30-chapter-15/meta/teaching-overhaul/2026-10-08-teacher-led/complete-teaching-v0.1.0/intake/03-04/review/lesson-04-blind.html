What changes when cells form germ layers?

During , cell number increased, and a developed a cavity, inner cell mass and outer trophoblast. Those events prepared different cell populations, but they did not yet make the full range of body tissues. The embryo now needs cells arranged in positions from which different tissues can develop.

During , cells move and reorganize to establish three primary germ layers: , and . Cells in the embryonic disc move inward and spread into new positions. Some establish the inner endoderm; others form mesoderm between that inner layer and cells remaining at the surface, which become ectoderm. The resulting three-layer embryo is called a gastrula. A germ layer is an early tissue-forming cell population; “germ” here does not mean a microbe. Ecto- suggests outer, meso- middle and endo- inner. The names help orient the embryonic layers, not locate every resulting tissue in an adult.

In human development, gastrulation begins in the third week after . An earlier two-layer embryonic disc, a flattened arrangement of early embryonic cells, forms during the second week. The textbook's p. 511 sequence and the teacher's week-two heading compress these changes; distinguish that earlier disc from the three definitive germ layers formed through gastrulation. This timing is counted from fertilization, not from the clinical dating of a pregnancy.

The gaps separate labels for readability; they are not body cavities. The figure does not show the cell movements or the actual three-dimensional shape of a human embryo.

Gastrulation is therefore a change in organization and developmental pathways, rather than simply another increase in cell number. The three layers then participate in morphogenesis, the development of body form and structures. Later folding and movement bring their descendants into complex arrangements.

Follow tissue identity rather than adult position

Ectoderm gives rise to the epidermis, the outer cellular layer of skin, and to the nervous system. A spinal cord lies inside the body but still has an ectodermal origin. The layer's embryonic position does not mean that all of its descendants remain on the adult surface. Hair, nails and tooth enamel are other examples in the textbook's source list.

Mesoderm contributes to muscle, bone and other connective tissues, as well as blood, heart and blood vessels, kidneys and many reproductive structures. Connective tissue supports, joins or surrounds other tissues; bone, cartilage and blood are specialized examples. The dermis, the deeper connective-tissue part of the skin, is a different tissue from the epidermis.

Endoderm contributes much of the epithelial lining of the digestive and respiratory passages, along with important glandular tissues in associated organs such as the liver and pancreas. Epithelium is a closely packed cell layer covering a surface or lining a space. Naming an epithelial lining is more precise than saying that every part of an organ comes from endoderm.

Use the figure's examples to check the distinctions. Nervous tissue and epidermis share the A lineage despite different adult positions. Muscle and blood belong with B. Digestive and respiratory epithelial linings belong with C. An organ combines tissues that can come from different germ layers, so “inside the body” is not a sufficient classification rule.

How can related cells do different jobs?

Differentiation is the process through which cells become specialized in structure and function. For example, muscle cells develop machinery for contraction while nerve cells develop structures used for communication. They generally retain the same inherited genome, the complete set of genetic information, rather than acquiring a completely new chromosome set for each job.

What changes is how cells use that information. Gene expression means using the information in a gene to make functional products. Different patterns of gene expression, together with signals from surrounding cells and tissues, help cells take different developmental paths. Establishing cell positions during gastrulation matters because position influences which signals cells encounter.

Keep the processes connected but distinct. Cleavage divides the early structure into more cells. Gastrulation reorganizes cells into tissue-forming layers. Differentiation develops specialized cell types, and morphogenesis produces the shape and organization of structures. These processes interact during development; they are not four interchangeable names for cell division.

When a question names an organ, look for the particular tissue it asks about. Intestinal smooth muscle and intestinal epithelial lining have different functions and origins even though they lie beside each other. A developmental change affecting one contribution need not affect the other identically. Do not turn a tissue-origin explanation into a diagnosis or a prediction that every tissue from that germ layer must be damaged.

The embryo's own layers are also different from the tissues and membranes that support it. Having explained its tissue-forming organization, we will next examine the surrounding support structures and what each contributes.

Worked example

One skin sample contains several tissue origins

An illustrative tissue record names epidermal cells, deeper dermal connective tissue and sensory nerve cells in the same sample of skin from the trunk. A learner assigns the whole sample to ectoderm because skin is on the outside. Evaluate that claim by classifying each named tissue.

  1. Identify the tissue, not just the organ. Epidermis is the outer cellular covering. Its ectodermal origin fits the A examples in the schematic.
  2. Distinguish the deeper support tissue. Dermal connective tissue belongs with mesodermal support tissues in this classroom model. Calling it skin does not make it epidermis.
  3. Classify the nerve cells by function and lineage. They are nervous tissue, so they have ectodermal origin even though they extend beneath the surface.
  4. Check the claim against all the evidence. The sample includes ectodermal and mesodermal contributions. The location of the organ did not establish a single origin for everything in it.

Use the same method on another organ: first name the tissue, then find its developmental contribution. A passage lined with epithelium can also have muscle and connective tissue in its wall. The fact that its lining has one origin does not assign that origin to all the surrounding tissues.

There is no need to invent a new genome for each cell type to explain this diversity. Cells can specialize through different use of shared genetic information and developmental signals. The germ-layer classification describes developmental relationships, while differentiation explains how specialized cell structure and function emerge.
