Lactation and hormonal control after birth
Why are producing milk and releasing milk different processes?
Learning goal
Distinguish prolactin-driven milk production from oxytocin-driven milk ejection and connect both with suckling and post-birth hormone changes.
Before you begin
Oxytocin is released by the posterior pituitary. A tissue can contain a product without releasing it immediately.

Vocabulary help
Key terms
Words for this lesson
Use these definitions when you need help with a term.
lactation
Milk production and secretion by the mammary glands.
prolactin
An anterior-pituitary hormone that stimulates milk production.
milk ejection
Movement of milk toward the nipple through contraction of cells around mammary secretory structures.
suckling reflex
A neural and hormonal response initiated by sensory stimulation during feeding.
Separate making milk from moving milk
Prolactin
from the anterior pituitary supports milk production by mammary secretory cells.
Oxytocin
from the posterior pituitary promotes contraction of myoepithelial cells around the secretory structures, moving milk into ducts and toward the nipple. This is
milk ejection
or let-down.
The difference is like making a product and moving it out of a storage area. The analogy does not describe the anatomy exactly, but it prevents a common error: assigning both jobs to the same hormone simply because both are associated with feeding.
Lactation: production is not ejection
View larger
Suckling can stimulate two hormonal responses: anterior-pituitary prolactin supports milk production, while posterior-pituitary oxytocin supports milk ejection. Schematic — not to scale.
Trace the suckling response
Suckling stimulates sensory receptors associated with the nipple. Neural signals reach the hypothalamus and contribute to endocrine responses. Prolactin supports continued milk production, while oxytocin supports ejection of milk that is available.
The pathway uses both nervous and endocrine systems. The initial sensory information travels through neurons; hormones then circulate to target tissues. The milk does not travel through nerves to the brain, and a hormone is not itself a sensory impulse.
Continued suckling helps sustain the response. A reflex can also be influenced by other neural inputs. For Biology 30, focus on the named input, control centre, hormonal signal and tissue response.
Neural and hormonal control of milk ejection
View larger
Sensory input during suckling stimulates oxytocin release from the posterior pituitary. Myoepithelial contraction ejects milk; prolactin supports its production. Inquiry into Biology, Chapter 15, p. 526.
Connect the change at birth
During pregnancy, estrogen and
progesterone
help prepare mammary tissues, while their high concentrations limit copious milk secretion. Prolactin is already present during pregnancy. After placental delivery, estrogen and progesterone fall, allowing prolactin-supported milk secretion to increase.
Do not describe prolactin as a hormone that first appears only after birth. The change involves the balance and effects of hormones, not simply the existence of one hormone.
These mechanisms explain the distinction used in course problems; they do not diagnose an individual's feeding experience. Many factors influence feeding, and a hypothetical hormone model is not a personal treatment plan.
Worked example
Locate a changed function
A model mammary gland contains milk, but contraction of the cells around the secretory structures does not occur.
The presence of milk shows that production has occurred.
The missing step concerns movement of existing milk.
Identify the usual signal for that contraction: oxytocin.
Do not conclude that prolactin must be absent when the described defect is ejection.
State the limit: the model identifies a process, not a diagnosis in a person.
