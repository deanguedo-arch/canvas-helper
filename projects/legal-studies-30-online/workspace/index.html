How This Course Works

A repeatable learning process

  1. Read the brief and vocabulary. Identify the result you are working toward.
  2. Study the lessons and worked example. Revisit them when a decision becomes difficult.
  3. Try the practice decisions. Feedback explains the reasoning and you can retry.
  4. Create the required product in a document, presentation, drawing or recording. Use the response record to summarize decisions and identify your files.
  5. Check the rubric, revise, then choose Save to Portfolio. Print or download a backup when needed.

Plan your time

Each CTS code represents approximately 25 hours of access to instruction and practical learning. A suggested pattern is 6 hours of lessons and source investigation, 4 hours of examples and practice, 9 hours developing evidence, 4 hours testing and revising, and 2 hours evaluating and preparing submission. Adapt the pace with your teacher; there is no timer or automatic credit for hours.

Independent work and assessment

Use fictional customers and supplied data. You do not need to buy anything, travel, contact a business, recruit a partner or attend a live session. Written role responses and solo recordings let you practise applied skills. Some official outcomes require participation or performance evidence that a simulation alone may not demonstrate. Your teacher must validate those requirements before the affected module is released or awarded.

Project connections must refer to successfully completed eligible CTS courses, including one at the project’s level and in the same occupational area. Advanced projects connect to another intermediate or advanced course. Project and practicum codes cannot supply the connections. Teacher confirmation is separate from this page’s progress indicators.

Keep your work

LGS3010 · Property Law
LGS3010 · Learning module · Advanced


Create a fictional property-advice information file that separates ownership, use, obligations and possible dispute routes. This is legal education, not advice for a real dispute.
Learning focus: identify the right before choosing a remedy; read a transaction as a sequence of decisions; build a fair fictional case file.


1. Identify the right before choosing a remedy2. Read a transaction as a sequence of decisions3. Build a fair fictional case filePracticeYour work


Words to know
Real property
land and interests associated with land.
Personal property
property other than real property, including movable goods.
Bailment
possession of another person's goods for an agreed purpose, without transferring ownership.
Encumbrance
a claim or interest that may affect property rights.
1. Identify the right before choosing a remedy
Property law concerns relationships between people and things, not just who physically holds an object. A bicycle owner can lend possession to a repair shop without selling it. A tenant occupies a home under an agreement but does not therefore own the land. Intellectual property concerns legally protected creations and identifiers; buying a physical copy does not usually transfer the creator's rights. Make a rights table identifying the property, the people, the claimed right and the evidence supporting that claim.


Ownership is not unlimited permission. Contracts, legislation, neighbouring rights and registered interests may restrict use. Distinguish a lease from a licence to use space, an easement from ownership, and a mortgage securing a loan from an outright sale. These are starting distinctions, not substitutes for reading the actual instrument. Indigenous land rights and treaty interests cannot accurately be reduced to an ordinary private purchase; use authoritative, context-specific sources when researching them.
Next lesson
2. Read a transaction as a sequence of decisions
Before a fictional purchase, identify the parties, property description, price, deposit, deadlines, conditions and consequences if a condition is not met. A financing condition and an inspection condition address different risks. A title search helps identify registered interests; it does not replace an inspection of physical condition. Record which question belongs to a lawyer, a licensed real estate professional, a lender or an inspector rather than assuming one person guarantees everything.


For consumer goods, preserve the advertised claim, agreed specification, receipt and dated communications. A disappointment is not automatically an unlawful practice, and a store policy is not necessarily the whole law. Alberta's current consumer framework uses the Consumer Protection Act; do not rely on the booklet's old Fair Trading Act label. Use the provincial consumer guidance in Resources and distinguish a legal requirement from a voluntary warranty or return policy.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Build a fair fictional case file
Scenario: Rowan leaves a bicycle at Cedar Repairs for an agreed $80 tune-up. The signed work order says additional work requires approval. Cedar replaces a wheel and charges $160, saying the wheel was unsafe. Rowan says no one requested approval. The bicycle is still at the shop. List established facts separately from each party's allegation. Preserve the work order, the inspection note and a fictional call log; do not invent an approval.


Write two possible interpretations and the information needed to distinguish them. Explain why possession, ownership, authority to repair and any claimed right to retain goods are separate questions requiring appropriate sources. Suggest a proportionate first communication and a suitable information or dispute-resolution route. The Alberta Court of Justice hears eligible civil claims up to $100,000; dollar value alone does not establish jurisdiction. Check the current court guidance and never file your classroom document.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. The work order is direct evidence of the approval condition; the shop's statement that a repair was useful does not by itself establish approval.
  2. A fair information request asks for the time, method and record of authorization, while acknowledging the safety concern. It avoids accusing either party of dishonesty without evidence.
  3. A stronger conclusion is conditional: if no authorization or applicable legal justification is established, Rowan has a reason to dispute the extra charge. The file identifies the unanswered legal question instead of promising a win.

LGS3020 · Dispute Resolution
LGS3020 · Learning module · Advanced


Build and independently perform three scripted responses to a fictional dispute: negotiation, mediation and arbitration. Explain how process choice changes control, fairness and possible outcomes.
Evidence boundary for teacher review
The solo role-switching exercise does not establish actual participation with other parties. Teacher validation is required for participation and teamwork outcomes.
Learning focus: choose a process for the problem; prepare evidence and listen for interests; demonstrate three processes and evaluate fairness.


1. Choose a process for the problem2. Prepare evidence and listen for interests3. Demonstrate three processes and evaluate fairnessPracticeYour work


Words to know
Position
the result a party initially demands.
Interest
the underlying need or concern behind a position.
Mediation
facilitated discussion in which the mediator does not impose the settlement.
Arbitration
a process in which an authorized decision-maker determines a dispute under the applicable agreement and law.
1. Choose a process for the problem
A disagreement can concern facts, interpretation, resources or relationships. Start by describing the issue neutrally and asking what each party needs. Negotiation lets parties discuss a solution directly. Mediation adds a facilitator who helps communication and option development. Arbitration gives a decision-maker authority defined by the governing agreement and law. Litigation uses a court process. Cost, urgency, enforceability, privacy, safety and power imbalance all affect suitability.


Do not assume every process is voluntary, confidential or non-binding in every context. Read the applicable terms and law. A mediated discussion may lead to a binding settlement agreement even though the mediator does not decide the merits. An arbitrator's authority and review routes differ from a mediator's role. A safety threat or serious imbalance can make informal direct negotiation inappropriate; explain when professional support or a formal process is needed.
Next lesson
2. Prepare evidence and listen for interests
Scenario: a community studio rents a room to a fictional arts club for four evenings at $100 each. Two evenings are disrupted by building maintenance. The club demands $200 back; the studio offers a $100 future credit. The agreement promises access but says nothing about maintenance. The club needs affordable replacement space; the studio needs predictable cash flow. These interests create more options than repeating the initial demands.


Create a chronology, disputed-fact list, document list and possible alternatives if no agreement is reached. Write an opening that states the impact and desired discussion without threats. Summarize the other position accurately before proposing options. Compare a partial refund plus replacement access, a future credit with flexible dates and another justified option. Record objective criteria such as usable hours and documented costs rather than personal attacks.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Demonstrate three processes and evaluate fairness
Independently write and perform a short negotiation with both roles, using labelled turns. Then write a mediation sequence including the mediator's opening, issue summary, option generation and voluntary proposed agreement. Finally create an arbitration submission for each side and a reasoned fictional decision constrained by the stated facts. Keep the roles distinct: the mediator must not simply announce a winner.


For each process, identify who controlled the outcome, how evidence was used and whether consent or authority was established. Distinguish civil proof on a balance of probabilities from the criminal standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt; the criminal standard is not a universal test for every dispute. Evaluate limitations of scripted solo work honestly. A teacher must validate any required interpersonal participation before this evidence is treated as satisfying that outcome.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. The club's $200 refund demand is a position. Its need for usable, affordable space is an interest that a replacement-room offer might address.
  2. A mediator can ask each side to clarify which evenings were unusable and what options meet both needs. Declaring that the studio owes $200 would instead take a decision-making role.
  3. A fictional arbitrator explains the authority assumed for the exercise, weighs the agreement and disruption records, addresses both arguments and states a reasoned outcome without inventing missing facts.

LGS3040 · Negligence
LGS3040 · Learning module · Advanced


Prepare a fictional negligence claim and defence using a shared evidence file. Test each legal element and distinguish a supported inference from an assumption.
Learning focus: separate an accident from a legal finding; compare competing explanations; draft both sides and revise the remedy.


1. Separate an accident from a legal finding2. Compare competing explanations3. Draft both sides and revise the remedyPracticeYour work


Words to know
Duty of care
a legally recognized obligation to take reasonable care in a relationship.
Breach
failure to meet the applicable standard of care.
Causation
the required connection between conduct and harm.
Damages
legally compensable harm or a monetary remedy, depending on context.
1. Separate an accident from a legal finding
Negligence analysis commonly asks whether a duty existed, whether the standard was breached, whether that breach caused the injury and whether compensable damage resulted. A bad outcome alone does not prove every element. A person can take reasonable care and an accident can still occur. Conversely, an unsafe practice without the required harm and causal connection does not automatically establish the damages claimed.


Identify the relationship and relevant legal sources before applying a rule. The reasonable-person standard is contextual; foreseeability, likelihood and seriousness of harm and practical precautions can matter. Special relationships, statutory duties, professional standards and the circumstances of children require careful legal research. Do not assume every person is judged by an identical specialist standard or that a professional guarantees success.
Next lesson
2. Compare competing explanations
Fictional file: at 10:00 a recreation centre employee records a leaking bottle near an entrance. At 10:12 a visitor slips there and reports a damaged laptop. A photo taken at 10:14 shows water but no warning sign. An employee says a sign was placed at 10:03; another visitor says it was behind a plant. The laptop purchase receipt is $1,200 from two years earlier. No medical information is supplied or required.


Create an element matrix with evidence supporting the claimant, evidence supporting the defendant and missing information. The photo supports a location and later condition, not necessarily the exact earlier arrangement. The receipt proves an earlier purchase price, not automatically the current loss. Consider alternative causes and whether the visitor's conduct is relevant without blaming them merely because they were injured. Label any assumed fact explicitly.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Draft both sides and revise the remedy
A classroom claim identifies the parties, material facts, alleged duty and breach, causal reasoning and supported loss. A defence responds to the specific allegations and explains disputed elements or applicable defences. Neither should attack a person's character or turn an unsupported possibility into a fact. Contributory negligence can affect responsibility and damages where applicable; it is not simply a magic phrase that defeats any claim.


Create a reasoned damages schedule using a supplied fictional repair estimate of $380 and a replacement estimate of $700 for a comparable used device. Explain why double-counting repair and full replacement would need correction. Recommend what additional evidence would help. These are classroom documents, not court forms or filing advice. Source current Alberta legislation and court information, record dates, and state the limits of this simplified exercise.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. The claimant links the recorded leak, delay and poorly visible warning to a possible breach. The argument still has to establish causation and a supported loss.
  2. The defence can challenge whether reasonable precautions were taken and whether the claimed laptop damage resulted from this fall. It should not assert a competing cause without evidence.
  3. A revised loss schedule selects a supported measure and explains the reason. Adding both $380 repair and $700 replacement for the same loss would risk overcompensation.

LGS3060 · Controversy & Change
LGS3060 · Learning module · Advanced


Develop a balanced legal-reform brief about a fictional youth digital-privacy proposal. Compare perspectives, test evidence and explain a realistic route for change.
Learning focus: frame a question that can be investigated; compare impacts rather than counting opinions; explain how change could happen.


1. Frame a question that can be investigated2. Compare impacts rather than counting opinions3. Explain how change could happenPracticeYour work


Words to know
Legal reform
a proposed change to law or its operation.
Jurisdiction
the legal authority to act in a matter.
Stakeholder
a person or group affected by a decision.
Counterargument
a reasoned challenge to a position.
1. Frame a question that can be investigated
A useful reform question identifies a current problem, the people affected and the kind of change proposed. Our fictional proposal would require a youth recreation app to offer a clear data-deletion request process. The task is not to assume the proposal is already law. Separate the existing legal framework, the organization's voluntary policy and the proposed rule. Privacy responsibilities differ across public bodies, private organizations and federally regulated contexts.


Use primary legislation and regulator guidance to establish the baseline, then research competing arguments. Record the source, observation date, jurisdiction and whether it states law, guidance, research or opinion. A headline can suggest a question but cannot replace the underlying source. Define privacy, meaningful consent and data minimization carefully; do not collect classmates' personal experiences or sensitive information to make the project feel authentic.
Next lesson
2. Compare impacts rather than counting opinions
Supplied fictional consultation: youth participants want deletion within 14 days; a small operator says backups take 30 days to cycle; a safety officer says certain incident records may need retention; an accessibility advocate wants requests available without a telephone call. These are attributed scenario positions, not real survey findings. Build an impact matrix covering benefits, burdens, risks and possible safeguards for each group.


Consider alternatives: clearer notice, an accessible request form, a retention schedule, independent review or a staged implementation. Test unintended consequences and distinguish technical difficulty from a complete justification for unlimited retention. A persuasive brief represents the strongest reasonable objection and answers it with evidence or a modification. It does not dismiss people as uncaring because their priorities differ.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Explain how change could happen
Legislatures can enact or amend statutes; authorized bodies can create regulations within their authority; courts interpret law and decide cases; organizations can change policies within the law. These routes are not interchangeable. Identify which route fits your proposal and who would need to act. Public education and advocacy can influence a debate, but a petition does not itself amend a statute.


Write a concise brief with background, current baseline, options, recommended change, implementation and evaluation. Distinguish your recommendation from a statement of existing law. Explain what new evidence would change your position. Alberta's former FOIP framework was replaced in 2025 by separate access and privacy legislation for public bodies; verify the applicable current source instead of assuming that a school, airline and shop all operate under one privacy statute.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. The initial slogan 'delete everything immediately' overlooks required retention and technical constraints. A stronger proposal defines eligible data, exceptions, a response deadline and review rights.
  2. The matrix preserves the accessibility concern as a design requirement: a web or written route must accompany any phone process. This is more specific than saying the proposal should be inclusive.
  3. The recommendation states its jurisdictional uncertainty and names the source that must resolve it. It avoids describing the fictional 14-day proposal as a current legal deadline.

1. Read a judgment with a question in mind
A judgment is not a news story. Begin with the court, date, citation and procedural history, then identify the facts material to the legal issue. Separate the parties' arguments from the court's conclusions. A case brief should distinguish issue, result and reasoning; copying a headnote without understanding those distinctions can conceal the very reasoning the task assesses.


Use the official Supreme Court of Canada decisions database in Resources. Locate R. v. Stinchcombe, [1991] 3 S.C.R. 326, not the inaccurate 'Stinchcomb (1995)' reference in the source booklet. Preserve the citation and link. Read the official reasons or an accessible text version, and paraphrase in your own words. Do not assume that every sentence carries the same precedential weight or that an older ruling answers every current factual variation.
Next lesson
2. Work through a disclosure decision
In Stinchcombe, the dispute concerned Crown disclosure of witness statements to the defence. The Supreme Court recognized a broad disclosure obligation relevant to the accused's ability to make full answer and defence, with limits and judicial supervision. The decision is about fair access to relevant information in criminal proceedings; it is not a rule that every private record must be publicly posted.


Build a two-column before/after explainer of the issue and the principle established. Identify the fairness rationale and a practical consequence for preparing a defence. Then consider a fictional variation: a student claims the case gives the public unrestricted access to every police file. Test that claim against the actual subject, parties and scope of the decision rather than extending a memorable slogan beyond its context.

3. Trace influence without exaggerating it
To explain why a decision is landmark, show its legal significance and an identifiable effect. Search later authoritative judgments or official guidance that discusses the principle. Record the later source's date and whether it applies, distinguishes or develops the earlier rule. A citation alone does not mean the later court accepted every argument made about the case.


Create a timeline separating the original dispute, the judgment and later developments. Add one independently researched Canadian decision approved for your context and compare its issue, reasoning and impact. Avoid a ranking based only on fame. Your original explainer should have a clear audience, a precise claim, a source note and a limitation. Revise any statement that treats the historical case as a complete guide to current legal procedure.

Build a fictional criminal-procedure decision file that separates offence elements, evidence, rights and possible procedural stages without deciding guilt from a headline.
Learning focus: identify elements before judging a person; follow the process and identify safeguards; distinguish youth justice and evaluate a decision.


1. Identify elements before judging a person2. Follow the process and identify safeguards3. Distinguish youth justice and evaluate a decisionPracticeYour work


Words to know
Actus reus
the prohibited conduct or other external elements of an offence.
Mens rea
the required mental element, where applicable.
Presumption of innocence
the accused is not required to prove innocence.
Disclosure
provision of relevant prosecution information to the defence subject to applicable limits.
1. Identify elements before judging a person
Criminal law defines offences and the conditions for responsibility. The prosecution must prove the required elements beyond a reasonable doubt; suspicion or disapproval is not enough. Many offences involve both prohibited conduct and a mental element, but the precise requirements must be read from the applicable law and cases. Do not apply a single simplified intent rule to every offence.


Fictional scenario: a library laptop disappears after closing. A camera shows Sam carrying a similar case. Sam says it contained a personal computer. The inventory record may have been updated late. List what the evidence establishes, what it suggests and what remains unknown. A similar case is not automatically proof of taking the library device or of the required mental element.
Next lesson
2. Follow the process and identify safeguards
A simplified pathway can include investigation, a charging decision, first appearance, release or detention decisions, disclosure, a plea, trial and, after a finding of guilt, sentencing. Cases do not all follow an identical route. Charges are allegations, a bail decision is not a trial verdict, and sentencing does not occur merely because a person was arrested. Read current official sources before describing a specific procedural rule.


The Charter protects rights relevant to investigation and trial, including protections concerning searches, detention, counsel and a fair hearing. A classroom exercise should identify the right and the factual question that would require legal analysis, not promise that every disputed search automatically ends a case. Keep roles clear: police investigate, prosecutors exercise public responsibilities, defence counsel represents the accused, and an impartial court decides matters within its authority.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Distinguish youth justice and evaluate a decision
Canada's Youth Criminal Justice Act applies to young people aged 12 to 17 at the time of an alleged offence. Youth justice is distinct from the adult system; it is not simply adult procedure with smaller penalties. Use the Department of Justice overview and current legislation to research accountability, rehabilitation, reintegration and available responses. Do not publish identifying information about real young people or seek personal criminal histories.


Create a decision file for the laptop scenario with an elements-and-evidence chart, a procedural timeline and two reasoned next-step options. Explain which additional evidence could fairly test both accounts. Discuss the purposes and proportionality of a response only within the facts and law you have verified. This is an educational simulation, not advice about an actual investigation, charge or sentence.

2. Plan manageable work and monitor changes
Break the project into stages: investigate, design, produce, test, revise and evaluate. Estimate effort for each stage and identify dependencies. A dependency is work that must happen before something else can begin: the audience brief must be clear before a final design can be judged. Reserve time for revision rather than allocating all available time to the first version.


List required resources and a no-cost alternative. Use fictional records instead of personal or customer information. Track milestones in your external project document with date, intended result, actual result and next action. If the plan changes, explain why and whether the change affects the agreed scope. Document a problem you solved rather than presenting a flawless story with no evidence of how you worked.

3. Test a product and evaluate honestly
Use repeatable checks that relate to the proposal. Test three reader questions against the toolkit, audit every legal claim against a primary source and revise one misleading or overly broad statement. A self-test is acceptable for practice if labelled honestly; do not invent feedback from customers or participants. Keep original work, observations, revised work and an explanation of the change together. Testing may reveal that your original idea is less useful than an alternative; a justified revision is evidence of learning.


Evaluate both the product and the process. Compare results with every success criterion, explain a limitation, and identify a realistic next step. Discuss how you used information, numbers, communication, responsible work habits and problem-solving. Identify teamwork or live-performance evidence that this independent project does not demonstrate, so a teacher can assess the boundary accurately.
