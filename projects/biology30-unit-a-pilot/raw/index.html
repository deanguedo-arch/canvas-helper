Begin with evidence

1. Stability is dynamic, not motionless

A resting body is active. Cells consume oxygen and glucose, release carbon dioxide and heat, move ions, and exchange water. A sprint increases ATP demand and heat production. Cold air increases heat loss. A sudden alarm changes attention, heart activity, breathing, and fuel availability. The values do not stay perfectly fixed; they move within ranges compatible with cell function.

Homeostasis is the coordinated maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite internal and external change. The word relatively matters. Body temperature, blood glucose, blood pressure, osmotic concentration, calcium concentration, and carbon dioxide levels fluctuate. Regulation limits the disturbance, often by shifting several effectors at once.

A regulated variable is a measurable condition the system monitors or controls. Sweating is not the regulated variable in temperature control; body temperature is. Sweating is one response that can increase heat loss. Similarly, insulin is not the regulated variable in glucose control. Blood glucose is the variable, and insulin is one signal involved in responses that oppose a rise.

Build the system

2. A control pathway connects change to response

A useful model begins with a disturbance or stimulus that changes a variable. A sensor detects information related to that variable. A coordinating centre compares or integrates the input and organizes an output. A signal travels to an effector, such as a muscle, gland, or target tissue. The effector's response changes the variable or changes the disturbance acting on it.

These roles can be distributed. In thermoregulation, temperature-sensitive receptors occur in the skin and within the body, while the hypothalamus integrates information. Skeletal muscles can produce heat by shivering, skin blood vessels can alter heat transfer, and sweat glands can increase evaporative cooling. No single arrow explains the whole response.

A response becomes homeostatic when its effect opposes the original departure from a workable range. A response that merely follows a stimulus is not automatically feedback. To identify feedback, ask a precise question: after the response occurs, does the response reduce the change that triggered it, amplify it, or leave it unaffected?

Compare the coordinators

3. Nervous and endocrine signals solve different timing problems

The nervous system sends information along neurons as electrical changes and across synapses using chemical neurotransmitters. Connections are anatomically directed, so many effects can begin in milliseconds and end quickly. This pattern is useful for reflexes, movement, rapid changes in gland secretion, and immediate adjustment of heart or breathing activity.

The endocrine system releases hormones into the blood. Circulation distributes a hormone widely, but only cells with suitable receptors and response machinery react. Hormonal effects often begin more slowly and last longer because release, transport, receptor binding, and changes in cell activity take time. Endocrine control is well suited to metabolism, growth, long-duration stress responses, water balance, and coordinated changes across several organs.

Speed is not an absolute rule. Some hormone effects are rapid, and some neural regulation produces lasting change. More importantly, the systems work together. The hypothalamus receives neural information and controls endocrine pathways. The adrenal medulla receives sympathetic neural input and releases epinephrine into blood. A whole-body response often contains a fast neural phase and a slower hormonal phase.

Apply the model

4. Read a phenomenon as a chain of evidence

After a sprint, heat production and carbon dioxide increase. Temperature receptors and chemical receptors provide input to coordinating centres. Neural outputs alter breathing, heart activity, skin blood flow, and sweat-gland activity. Hormonal signals help mobilize fuel and adjust circulation. The observed breathing and sweating are evidence of regulation, but they are not proof that every variable has already returned to baseline.

In cold conditions, reduced skin blood flow limits heat transfer and shivering can increase heat production. In an unexpected alarm, sympathetic neural activity rapidly changes organ function while adrenal epinephrine supports a broader response. Different disturbances recruit overlapping effectors. The response that is useful in one context can become costly if it continues after the disturbance has passed.

Scientific models simplify. The control-system diagram does not show every receptor, brain pathway, hormone, delay, or interaction. Its value is that it forces each claim to name a variable, a route, an effector, and an effect. Later lessons will add membrane voltage, synapses, sensory receptors, endocrine glands, and interacting feedback loops to this first map.

Follow the loop

1. Negative feedback is defined by direction of effect

A feedback loop exists when the output of a system influences the condition that drove the system. In negative feedback, the response reduces the original departure. If body temperature rises above its usual range, increased heat loss opposes the rise. If blood glucose falls, responses that increase glucose availability oppose the fall. The word negative describes the direction of the correction, not the value or importance of the response.

The set point is often better understood as a regulated range rather than one unchanging number. It can shift with time of day, activity, development, illness, or other signals. Regulation also has delay. A response may continue briefly after a variable begins moving back toward its range because sensors, signals, and effectors do not update instantly.

Positive feedback amplifies an initiating change and is useful in bounded processes such as blood clotting or the contraction phase of childbirth. It does not usually maintain a variable near a stable range. A one-way chain—stimulus followed by response—cannot be classified until the effect of the response on the original change is known.

Separate the events

2. Hormone signalling is more than release

An endocrine gland or endocrine cell releases a hormone into extracellular fluid and then blood. Circulation transports the hormone. Reaching a tissue is not the same as affecting it: target cells must express an appropriate receptor. Receptor binding starts intracellular events that alter transport, enzyme activity, gene expression, secretion, or another cell response.

Hormone concentration depends on release, distribution, breakdown, and removal. Target response also depends on receptor number and downstream machinery. Therefore, a high hormone concentration can coexist with a weak response if receptors or intracellular pathways respond poorly. Conversely, an unusually low concentration can produce a strong effect if a tissue is highly sensitive.

Feedback may act at more than one level. A target-gland hormone can reduce signals from the pituitary and hypothalamus. A changing blood variable can act directly on endocrine cells, as glucose does on pancreatic islets. To reason accurately, name what is detected and where the return signal acts.

Compare pathways

3. Neural and endocrine loops share logic but use different routes

Both systems need information, integration, signalling, a responsive target, and a consequence. Nervous control uses membrane potentials, action potentials, synapses, and connected effectors. Endocrine control uses secretion, circulation, receptors, and target-cell responses. A diagram should not place a hormone arrow directly from a gland to an outcome while hiding transport and receptor binding.

The hypothalamus is an important bridge. It receives neural information about the internal and external environment and controls autonomic outputs. It also controls pituitary hormone release. During stress, sensory and cognitive input can activate sympathetic neural pathways quickly while hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal signalling supports a slower cortisol response.

Control can fail at different points. Damaged sensory receptors reduce input. A blocked axon disrupts neural transmission. Low hormone production weakens a circulating signal. A receptor defect changes target response even when hormone release is normal. These failures can produce similar outward patterns, so concentration data and target-response evidence must be interpreted together.

Work an example

4. Calcium regulation shows why paired evidence matters

Suppose blood calcium falls. Parathyroid glands can increase parathyroid hormone release. PTH supports responses in bone, kidneys, and indirectly the intestine that raise blood calcium. As calcium rises toward its regulated range, the stimulus for PTH release decreases. The complete loop includes the variable after the response, not just the gland and its targets.

Now imagine that PTH is high while blood calcium remains low. That pattern does not support simple underproduction. It suggests that calcium supply, kidney response, vitamin D-dependent absorption, receptor action, or another part of the pathway may be limiting. The example illustrates a general rule: hormone values must be interpreted beside the regulated variable and target response.

Models are representations, not miniature bodies. A useful model makes its assumptions visible. In this course, a feedback diagram must identify the variable, direction of change, sensor or endocrine cell, signal route, target, response, and return effect. Later models will add interacting hormones and measured data.

Build the cell

1. Neuron structure supports directional communication

Dendrites and the cell body receive chemical and electrical influences from other cells. The cell body contains the nucleus and much of the machinery needed to maintain the neuron. Inputs are integrated near the beginning of the axon. If the membrane reaches threshold, an action potential begins and travels along the axon toward its terminals.

Axons can be very short or extend a metre or more. Branches near the terminal allow one neuron to influence several targets. At an axon terminal, an arriving action potential triggers calcium-dependent neurotransmitter release. The next cell may be another neuron, a muscle fibre, or a gland cell.

Direction is functional, not determined by which side of a page contains the dendrites. Information generally moves from receptive regions toward the axon terminal, but neurons vary greatly in shape. A labelled structure is meaningful only when its role in the pathway is explained.

Place the cell in a pathway

2. Sensory, interneuron, and motor describe information roles

A sensory neuron carries information from a receptor toward the central nervous system. The receptor may be part of the neuron or a separate specialized cell. An interneuron lies within the central nervous system and integrates or relays information between neurons. A motor neuron carries output from the central nervous system toward an effector such as skeletal muscle or a gland.

A simple pathway can be written as receptor → sensory neuron → interneuron or central integration → motor neuron → effector. Real pathways may contain many interneurons, branching routes, and feedback from higher brain regions. The categories describe the neuron's role in a particular circuit; they do not imply that all neurons in a category have the same shape.

Nerves are bundles of axons in the peripheral nervous system, supported by connective tissue and blood vessels. A nerve can contain sensory axons, motor axons, or both. In the central nervous system, bundles of axons are commonly called tracts. A whole nerve is not one giant neuron.

Increase conduction speed

3. Myelin changes where current can cross the membrane

Myelin is a lipid-rich insulating wrapping formed by glial cells around segments of some axons. Gaps called nodes of Ranvier contain high densities of voltage-gated channels. Local current spreads rapidly beneath myelin and the action potential is regenerated at successive nodes. This saltatory conduction is faster and more energy-efficient than continuous regeneration along every adjacent membrane region.

Schwann cells form myelin around peripheral axons, with one cell supporting one segment of one axon. Oligodendrocytes form central nervous system myelin and can extend processes to segments of several axons. Loss of myelin disrupts the timing and reliability of conduction because current leaks and the next node may not reach threshold.

A common oversimplification says that peripheral neurons regenerate while central neurons cannot. Outcomes are more nuanced. Some damaged peripheral axons can regrow if the cell body survives and supporting pathways remain aligned, but recovery may be slow or incomplete. Central axon regrowth is strongly limited by the injury environment and inhibitory signals, yet research documents plasticity and restricted repair. Location alone does not guarantee an outcome.

Use structural evidence

4. Structure predicts the pattern of disruption

Damage near dendrites or the cell body can alter how inputs are integrated or threaten the entire cell. Damage to an axon can interrupt communication with distant targets even when the cell body remains alive. Damage to myelin may slow conduction, desynchronize signals, or produce conduction failure while leaving the axon initially intact.

Multiple sclerosis involves immune-mediated damage within the central nervous system, including myelin and sometimes axons. The location and timing of lesions help explain why symptoms vary. It is inaccurate to reduce the condition to one broken wire or to assume a single predictable course. A scientific case description should connect evidence to affected pathways without diagnosing an individual.

When comparing tissue images, look for the structure being measured: cell bodies, axons, myelin, nodes, or supporting cells. State what the image can show and what it cannot. A still image can reveal anatomy, but conduction speed and signal reliability require functional evidence.

Action Potentials: Ions into Information

Not saved yet

Build the mechanism

1. Resting potential is a maintained imbalance

A neuron at rest is not inactive. Its membrane maintains a voltage difference, commonly near −70 millivolts, meaning the inside is negative relative to the outside. The exact value varies among neurons. Voltage exists because ions are distributed unequally and the membrane is selectively permeable.

Sodium ions (Na⁺) are more concentrated outside the axon, while potassium ions (K⁺) are more concentrated inside. At rest, the membrane has many potassium leak channels, so K⁺ tends to diffuse out. Large negatively charged molecules remain inside. These movements and trapped charges help make the interior negative.

Read the model

The sodium-potassium pump uses ATP to move three Na⁺ out and two K⁺ in. This maintains the concentration gradients over time. It does not single-handedly create the rapid voltage change of each action potential; ion channels produce those fast phases.

2. Threshold starts a regenerative event

A stimulus first produces a local change in voltage. If that change does not reach threshold, the membrane returns toward rest without producing a full action potential. If threshold is reached, often modelled near −55 mV, enough voltage-gated sodium channels open to begin a rapid positive-feedback cycle.

Na⁺ enters through open channels, making the inside less negative. That depolarization opens more voltage-gated sodium channels, which allows more Na⁺ to enter. The rising phase therefore regenerates itself until sodium channels inactivate near the peak. A larger threshold-crossing stimulus does not create a proportionally taller action potential: the event is all-or-none.

Why the signal does not fade

Current from one depolarized region brings the next region to threshold. New voltage-gated channels open there, producing a new full-sized action potential. The signal is repeatedly regenerated along the axon rather than passively leaking to the end.

3. Repolarization resets voltage, then channels recover

Near the peak, voltage-gated sodium channels become inactivated and delayed voltage-gated potassium channels are open. K⁺ moves out of the axon, taking positive charge with it. The membrane potential moves back toward a negative value: this is repolarization.

Potassium channels close slowly, so the voltage often becomes more negative than resting potential for a short time. This is hyperpolarization. As channel states return to their resting condition, the membrane returns toward its usual resting voltage.

During the absolute refractory period, inactivated sodium channels cannot reopen, so another action potential cannot begin in that region. During the following relative refractory period, some channels have recovered but the membrane is hyperpolarized, so a stronger-than-usual stimulus may be needed. Refractory behaviour limits firing rate and helps propagation continue forward.

4. Intensity changes the pattern, not the height

Once threshold is crossed, individual action potentials have a similar amplitude. A stronger stimulus is commonly represented by a higher frequency of action potentials. In many sensory pathways, stronger stimulation can also recruit additional neurons. The nervous system reads the timing and population pattern, not a series of spikes that simply become taller.

This distinction matters when interpreting graphs. One action-potential trace explains the voltage mechanism of one event. A train of traces over a longer time interval can carry information about stimulus intensity through the spacing between events.

Trace the sequence

1. A chemical synapse translates signals twice

When an action potential reaches a presynaptic terminal, depolarization opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium enters down its electrochemical gradient. This rise in terminal calcium triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release neurotransmitter by exocytosis.

Neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane. Some receptors are ion channels; others activate intracellular pathways. The result can make the postsynaptic membrane more likely to reach threshold, less likely to reach threshold, or change another cell function. The effect depends on the receptor and cell, not on a transmitter being universally excitatory or inhibitory.

Signal termination is essential. Neurotransmitter may be broken down by enzymes, transported back into the presynaptic cell, taken up by supporting cells, or diffuse away. Without termination, the postsynaptic receptor would continue receiving a message after the relevant presynaptic activity had ended.

Name the chemicals

2. Acetylcholine and norepinephrine act in specific circuits

Acetylcholine is released at the neuromuscular junction, where receptors on skeletal muscle help start contraction. It is also used at autonomic synapses and in the central nervous system. Acetylcholinesterase in the synaptic cleft rapidly breaks acetylcholine into products that can be recycled. Cholinesterase activity therefore limits the duration of the signal.

Norepinephrine acts as a neurotransmitter in many sympathetic postganglionic pathways and in central circuits involved in attention and arousal. Its effect varies with receptor subtype. Much released norepinephrine is removed by reuptake into the presynaptic terminal; enzymes also contribute to metabolism.

The same chemical can have different consequences in different tissues because receptors couple to different ion channels or intracellular pathways. A scientifically complete statement names the transmitter, receptor or target, and resulting response. Saying that a chemical simply turns the nervous system up or down hides the mechanism.

Interrogate a case

3. A toxin's location predicts the evidence pattern

Consider an instructional case in which a compound inhibits acetylcholinesterase. Acetylcholine is released normally but is not cleared at its usual rate. At first, acetylcholine receptors may be stimulated repeatedly. Continued signalling can disrupt coordinated muscle and autonomic function. The mechanism is not that the toxin creates extra action potentials in every neuron; it prolongs acetylcholine action where those synapses and receptors occur.

A different compound might block acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction. In that case, acetylcholine can be released and broken down normally, but the muscle response is reduced because receptor activation is blocked. Measuring transmitter release alone would not distinguish these two mechanisms. Receptor response and clearance evidence would be needed.

Mechanistic evidence supports explanations, not personal diagnosis. Similar symptoms can arise from different neural, muscular, metabolic, or toxic causes. Scientific reasoning should state which observations fit the proposed site of action and which additional tests would be required.

Science, technology, and society

4. Interventions reveal both capability and trade-offs

Medicines can alter transmitter synthesis, storage, release, receptor activation, reuptake, or breakdown. That capability can reduce symptoms or restore function, but the same pathway may operate in several tissues. Benefits and adverse effects therefore depend on dose, receptor distribution, timing, metabolism, and the person's health context.

Scientific explanations develop as researchers compare hypotheses with evidence. A receptor-binding result, an electrical recording, and a behavioural observation answer different questions. Agreement across methods strengthens a mechanism. A result that does not fit should trigger revision rather than being hidden.

Technology aims to solve practical problems, but it also creates decisions about access, safety, monitoring, and unintended effects. A strong evidence brief distinguishes what a mechanism predicts, what a study measured, and what remains uncertain. It does not treat one diagram or one correlation as final proof.

Organize the system

1. Central and peripheral name anatomical divisions

The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord. It integrates information, constructs responses, and supports perception, memory, emotion, and conscious action. The peripheral nervous system includes nerves and ganglia outside the brain and spinal cord. It carries sensory information toward the CNS and motor commands toward effectors.

The sensory division includes information from the body surface, special senses, muscles, joints, and internal organs. The motor division includes somatic pathways to skeletal muscle and autonomic pathways that regulate smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands. These functional divisions overlap with anatomy but are not the same classification.

The autonomic system includes sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions. Sympathetic activity often supports mobilization during demand; parasympathetic activity often supports maintenance and restoration. Both can be active in a coordinated pattern, and their effects depend on the organ and receptors. They are not simple on and off switches for stress and rest.

Use the spinal cord

2. The spinal cord is a pathway and an integration centre

Ascending tracts carry sensory information toward the brain, and descending tracts carry motor commands toward spinal circuits. Spinal grey matter contains many neuron cell bodies and synapses; surrounding white matter contains myelinated axon pathways. Spinal nerves connect these central circuits to peripheral receptors and effectors.

Spinal segments serve different regions, but a symptom rarely maps to one simple line on a diagram. A lesion can affect several tracts, and pathways may cross from one side to the other at different levels. Clinicians combine sensory, motor, reflex, imaging, and history evidence to localize damage.

The spinal cord can organize reflexes without waiting for conscious processing, yet information can still travel to the brain. This arrangement supports a rapid protective response and later awareness. Lesson 7 will use this organization to design a reflex investigation.

Map the brain

3. Brain regions contribute to networks

The cerebral cortex supports perception, voluntary movement, language, planning, and many forms of reasoning. The frontal lobe includes motor and executive networks; the parietal lobe integrates body sensation and spatial information; the temporal lobe contributes to hearing, memory, and language; the occipital lobe is central to visual processing.

The cerebellum compares intended and actual movement, supports timing and coordination, and contributes to motor learning. The medulla and pons participate in vital autonomic and relay functions. The hypothalamus links neural information to autonomic and endocrine control. The thalamus relays and organizes much incoming sensory information for cortical processing.

Functions emerge from networks. Damage to one region can disrupt connected areas, and other networks may partly compensate through plasticity. A brain-region model is useful for prediction, but a single label does not fully explain consciousness, personality, language, or behaviour.

Correct a neuromyth

4. Hemispheres specialize, but people are not left- or right-brained types

Some functions show hemispheric specialization. For many people, aspects of language rely more strongly on left-hemisphere networks, while some spatial and attentional processes rely more strongly on right-hemisphere networks. The corpus callosum and other pathways allow continual exchange between hemispheres.

This evidence does not support sorting people into logical left-brained and creative right-brained personalities. Complex tasks recruit distributed networks in both hemispheres. Individual variation, experience, task demands, and development matter. A colourful hemisphere chart is not a personality test.

Scientific knowledge changes when claims are tested against better evidence. Brain imaging measures signals related to activity; it does not photograph thoughts. Lesion evidence can support localization but may involve network disruption. A responsible explanation triangulates methods and states the limits of each.

Build the circuit

1. A reflex arc is an organized neural pathway

A receptor detects a stimulus and changes sensory-neuron activity. The sensory neuron carries input into the central nervous system. Within the spinal cord or brainstem, the pathway may synapse directly on a motor neuron or pass through one or more interneurons. The motor neuron carries output to an effector, producing a response.

In a withdrawal reflex, interneurons can activate flexor muscles and inhibit opposing muscles. Other pathways send information upward, supporting pain perception and later decisions. The response can begin before conscious awareness because the spinal circuit does not need to wait for cortical processing.

The patellar reflex is a stretch reflex involving sensory information from muscle spindles and motor output to the same muscle group, with additional inhibitory circuitry. The pupillary light reflex involves the brainstem and autonomic motor output. Different reflexes share a control logic but not identical anatomy.

Define the investigation

2. Reaction-time tasks model response evidence, not a clinical reflex

A ruler-drop task measures a voluntary response to a visual cue. Light must be detected, information processed, and a movement initiated. It is useful for studying repeated measurement, attention, anticipation, and variability, but it is not the same as a monosynaptic stretch reflex.

A testable question might ask whether the median catch distance changes between a single-task condition and a distraction condition. The independent variable is the condition. Catch distance or converted reaction time is the dependent variable. Ruler orientation, release height, hand position, number of trials, and rest interval should be controlled.

A hypothesis must predict a direction and give a mechanism. For example: if attention is divided, median catch distance will increase because additional processing competes with detecting the release and initiating the motor response. This explanation can be evaluated with data; it does not claim that one measurement reveals a person's general ability.

Classify the input

1. Receptors transduce particular forms of energy or chemistry

Sensory receptors convert stimulus energy or chemical binding into changes in membrane potential. Photoreceptors respond to light. Mechanoreceptors respond to deformation such as pressure, stretch, vibration, sound, or fluid movement. Chemoreceptors respond to molecules in air, food, or internal fluids. Thermoreceptors respond across temperature ranges, and nociceptors respond to potentially damaging mechanical, thermal, or chemical conditions.

Proprioceptors in muscles, tendons, and joints provide information about body position and movement. Vestibular hair cells respond to head motion and orientation. Internal receptors monitor conditions such as blood-gas chemistry, pressure, and osmotic concentration. The nervous system combines these inputs rather than reading one receptor in isolation.

A receptor category describes the kind of stimulus transduced, not the conscious sensation by itself. Mechanoreceptors in skin can contribute to touch, while mechanoreceptors in the cochlea contribute to hearing. The pathway and brain processing help determine what the activity becomes perceptually.

Explain adaptation

2. Receptor and central responses can change during a steady stimulus

Some receptors respond strongly when a stimulus begins or changes, then fire less during a steady condition. This sensory adaptation emphasizes new information. Slowly adapting receptors continue signalling features such as sustained pressure or stretch. The rate and degree of adaptation vary by receptor and pathway.

Perceptual adaptation can also involve central filtering and attention. Failing to notice a familiar background odour does not prove that olfactory receptors have stopped responding completely. A sudden change, a new context, or deliberate attention may restore awareness.

Adaptation is useful, but it does not make harmful exposure safe. Odour fatigue cannot establish that air is free of hazardous chemicals. Reduced thermal sensation cannot establish that tissue is protected. Safety decisions must rely on appropriate instruments and procedures, not sensation alone.

A stronger procedure mixes one-point and two-point trials so the participant cannot infer the answer from the sequence. Use blunt swabs, light comfortable contact, consent, and several trials at each separation. Do not mark skin or continue after discomfort. An image is not perception

A camera and an eye both focus light, but an eye is living sensory tissue connected to a brain. Name one step after focusing that a camera analogy can hide.

Control the light

1. The front of the eye refracts and regulates incoming light

The sclera is the tough outer coat. At the front it becomes the transparent cornea, which provides much of the eye's refraction. Aqueous humour fills the front chambers and supports pressure and nutrient exchange. The iris contains muscle that changes pupil diameter, regulating how much light enters.

The lens changes shape to adjust focus for different object distances. Ciliary muscle and suspensory structures alter lens curvature during accommodation. The lens fine-tunes focus; it is not the only refracting surface. Behind it, vitreous humour helps maintain shape and transmits light toward the retina.

The choroid contains blood vessels and pigment that absorbs stray light. The retina is neural tissue containing photoreceptors and several layers of processing cells. Light must reach this tissue before the visual signal can be converted into neural activity.

Transduce the image

2. Rods and cones contribute different evidence

Rods are highly sensitive in low light and support night and peripheral vision, but they do not provide colour information. Cones operate best in brighter conditions and support colour and fine spatial detail. Humans typically have three cone classes with overlapping sensitivities.

The fovea centralis contains densely packed cones and supports highest visual acuity. Peripheral retina contains more rods and is sensitive to dim light and movement. Eye movements continually place important details on the fovea; vision is not one frozen full-resolution image.

Photoreceptors communicate through bipolar and ganglion-cell pathways. Ganglion-cell axons form the optic nerve. Where the optic nerve exits, no photoreceptors are present, creating the physiological blind spot. The brain usually fills the missing region using surrounding information and input from the other eye.

Follow the central pathway

3. Visual fields are reorganized before cortical processing

Light from each side of visual space reaches corresponding parts of both retinas. At the optic chiasm, some optic-nerve fibres cross. This arrangement sends information from the left visual field toward right-hemisphere visual pathways and information from the right visual field toward left-hemisphere pathways.

Signals relay through the thalamus and reach visual cortex in the occipital lobe, then interact with networks that process form, motion, colour, location, memory, and attention. The brain does not merely flip an upside-down retinal picture. Perception is an active interpretation of patterned neural activity.

A lesion at the retina, optic nerve, chiasm, tract, or cortex produces different field patterns. A field pattern can help localize a pathway, but appropriate clinical testing is required. An on-screen blind-spot observation cannot diagnose retinal or neurological disease.

Apply technology

4. A useful technology targets a defined problem

In myopia, the optical system focuses distant light in front of the retina when accommodation is relaxed. Diverging corrective lenses or appropriately shaped contact lenses shift the focus onto the retina. In hyperopia, focusing tends to fall behind the retina; converging correction can assist, depending on the person's eye and accommodation.

Other technologies address different boundaries. Cataract surgery replaces a clouded lens. Retinal imaging provides structural evidence. Screen readers convert visual text into speech for users who need nonvisual access. Each technology solves a particular practical problem and has trade-offs involving access, training, cost, risk, and the information it can provide.

Evaluate a technology by linking design to mechanism. A high-resolution image is not automatically useful if it does not measure the affected structure. A strong claim names the target problem, the evidence of benefit, limitations, and who may be excluded by the design.

One inner ear, two kinds of information

Transmit the wave

1. Outer and middle ear structures deliver mechanical energy

The pinna collects sound and helps provide location cues. Sound travels through the auditory canal to the tympanic membrane, causing it to vibrate. The malleus, incus, and stapes transmit the vibration through the air-filled middle ear and concentrate force onto the oval window.

The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the throat region and helps equalize pressure across the tympanic membrane. It is not part of the sound-transduction chain. Middle-ear pressure differences or fluid can change how efficiently vibration is transmitted.

Movement at the oval window creates pressure waves in cochlear fluid. The round window allows pressure displacement. This route transfers energy from air to membrane, bone, and fluid before receptor cells convert it to neural information.

Transduce in the cochlea

2. Hair-cell deflection changes receptor signalling

Within the cochlea, fluid movement produces a travelling wave along the basilar membrane. Different frequencies produce maximum displacement at different locations. High frequencies are represented nearer the base and lower frequencies nearer the apex. This tonotopic organization continues through auditory pathways.

Hair cells in the organ of Corti bend relative to surrounding structures as the basilar membrane moves. Deflection opens mechanically gated channels, changing the receptor potential and neurotransmitter release onto auditory-nerve fibres. Hair cells do not send sound waves through the nerve; they translate mechanical movement into neural activity.

Frequency relates mainly to where and how auditory fibres respond. Intensity relates to vibration size, recruitment, and firing patterns. Hearing perception also depends on bilateral pathways, timing, context, attention, and cortical processing.

Maintain equilibrium

3. Vestibular receptors encode motion and orientation

The three semicircular canals are oriented in different planes and respond primarily to angular acceleration. When the head rotates, fluid movement bends a gelatinous structure and hair cells in the canal's ampulla. The direction of bending changes receptor signalling.

The utricle and saccule contain otolith organs that respond to linear acceleration and head position relative to gravity. Small calcium carbonate crystals add inertia and weight to a gelatinous layer over hair cells. The brain integrates vestibular information with vision and proprioception.

A mismatch among these sources can contribute to motion sickness or dizziness, but classroom activities cannot identify the cause of symptoms. The cerebellum and brainstem use vestibular input to coordinate posture, eye movements, and balance.

4. An audiogram shows thresholds, not a complete diagnosis

An audiogram plots the quietest tones detected at several frequencies under standardized conditions. Lower hearing-level values near the top indicate quieter thresholds; larger values lower on the graph indicate that a louder tone was required. Air- and bone-conduction patterns can help separate transmission from inner-ear or neural patterns, but expert assessment uses additional evidence.

Hearing technologies solve different problems. Hearing aids amplify and process sound when usable hair-cell and neural function remains. Cochlear implants bypass damaged hair-cell transduction and electrically stimulate auditory-nerve pathways for selected candidates. Neither restores typical hearing in every context, and outcomes depend on individual factors and support.

Build an evidence chain

1. Observation, mechanism, and conclusion are different claims

An observation describes what was measured: threshold changed, response time increased, or activity occurred at a particular time. A mechanism proposes how receptors, pathways, or effectors could produce the pattern. A conclusion states how strongly the evidence supports that mechanism within the design.

A strong evidence chain also names alternatives. Changes in attention, prior experience, equipment calibration, order, and response criterion can produce sensory differences. An alternative is not an excuse to ignore the pattern; it identifies the next evidence needed.

Interpret photoperiod

2. Light timing can provide biological time information

Photoperiod is the duration of light and darkness across a day. Retinal light information reaches brain timing pathways, including the suprachiasmatic nucleus. These pathways influence daily rhythms and signals involving the pineal gland and melatonin. Melatonin typically rises during the biological night; it is a timing signal, not a general sleep switch.

In many organisms, seasonal changes in day length influence reproduction, migration, coat growth, or flowering. In humans, light timing affects circadian alignment, alertness, and sleep timing. Individual response varies, and social schedules, age, latitude, prior light exposure, and behaviour also matter.

A correlation between shorter days and a later activity pattern does not prove that photoperiod acted alone. A stronger design controls clock time, light intensity and spectrum, temperature, and social schedule, or uses repeated measurements across seasons.

Widespread route, selective response

Map the glands

1. Endocrine organs are distributed but coordinated

The hypothalamus is nervous tissue that monitors and integrates information and controls pituitary function. The pituitary has anterior and posterior regions with different relationships to the hypothalamus. Together they coordinate several endocrine axes.

The thyroid lies in the neck and releases thyroxine; small parathyroid glands on its posterior surface release PTH. Adrenal glands sit above the kidneys and contain a cortex and medulla. Pancreatic islets are endocrine cell clusters within an organ that also has digestive exocrine functions.

A gland's location does not explain its effect. The relevant pathway includes the stimulus for release, hormone, blood transport, target receptors, cell response, and return effect. Many organs also produce hormones beyond the principal glands named in this unit.

Follow the signal

2. Receptors convert concentration into cell response

Water-soluble hormones generally bind receptors on the cell membrane and activate intracellular signalling pathways. Lipid-soluble hormones can cross cell membranes and bind intracellular receptors that influence gene expression. These categories are useful, but response timing and complexity depend on the specific pathway.

Target cells can alter receptor number or signalling sensitivity. A hormone may cause rapid changes in membrane transport and slower changes in protein production. The same hormone can have different effects in different tissues because receptor types and downstream machinery differ.

Hormones are removed or inactivated through metabolism and excretion. Declining release and hormone clearance help end the signal. A feedback model that shows release but no transport, receptor, response, or removal is incomplete.

Organize feedback

3. Endocrine pathways use direct and multi-level feedback

Pancreatic islet cells respond directly to blood-glucose conditions. Parathyroid cells respond to blood-calcium conditions. In these pathways, the regulated variable directly influences endocrine release and the resulting target responses feed back by changing the variable.

Other pathways use an axis. The hypothalamus releases a controlling signal to the anterior pituitary, the pituitary releases a tropic hormone, and a target gland releases another hormone. The target-gland hormone can reduce hypothalamic and pituitary signals. This multi-level negative feedback stabilizes the pathway while allowing central regulation.

Feedback should be tested by direction. High thyroxine normally reduces TSH drive; low thyroxine normally permits more drive. High cortisol normally reduces CRH and ACTH signalling. The terms high and low must be interpreted relative to context, time, and the regulated pathway.

Interpret paired values

4. Hormone concentration and target evidence belong together

Suppose a target-gland hormone is low while its pituitary tropic hormone is high. The pituitary appears to be signalling strongly, but the target gland output remains low. That pattern is consistent with a target-gland limitation. If both are low, hypothalamic or pituitary signalling becomes a candidate, although timing and other causes still matter.

If a hormone is high while its regulated variable remains far from range, poor target response is one possibility. The same pattern could also reflect an overwhelming disturbance, measurement timing, altered clearance, or another pathway. One pair of values narrows hypotheses but does not establish a diagnosis.

A robust explanation states the expected negative-feedback direction and then compares it with the observed values. Contradictory evidence is useful: it shows which simple model must be revised.

1. The anterior and posterior pituitary are controlled differently

Hypothalamic neurosecretory cells release controlling hormones into a portal blood system that carries them directly to the anterior pituitary. Anterior-pituitary endocrine cells respond by releasing hormones such as hGH, TSH, and ACTH into general circulation.

The posterior pituitary is neural tissue connected to hypothalamic neurons. ADH and oxytocin are synthesized in the hypothalamus, transported down axons, and released from terminals in the posterior pituitary. The posterior pituitary stores and releases these hormones; it does not synthesize them.

Calling the pituitary a master gland can hide this dependence on hypothalamic control and target-gland feedback. The hypothalamus-pituitary complex is better understood as an integrated neural-endocrine interface.

Regulate growth

2. hGH acts directly and through growth factors

Human growth hormone supports growth, protein synthesis, and metabolic adjustments. Many growth effects are mediated through insulin-like growth factors produced in response to hGH, especially by the liver. Secretion is pulsatile and varies with sleep, age, nutrition, activity, and other signals.

Excess hGH before growth plates close can produce gigantism; excess after closure can produce acromegaly. Insufficient hGH during development can reduce growth. Adult height or one hormone sample cannot identify the cause because genetics, nutrition, thyroid function, chronic illness, timing, and other factors also influence growth.

Feedback from hGH-related signals helps regulate hypothalamic and pituitary output. Interpreting an hGH case requires timing and growth-factor evidence, not a simple high-or-low label from one random sample.

Conserve water

3. ADH changes kidney water permeability

Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus respond when body-fluid osmotic concentration rises. Hypothalamic neurons increase ADH release from the posterior pituitary. ADH acts mainly on kidney collecting ducts, increasing aquaporin insertion and water reabsorption. Less water is lost in urine, helping lower the osmotic disturbance.

A decrease in blood volume or pressure can also increase ADH through additional receptor pathways. Thirst and kidney responses cooperate. As osmotic concentration and volume move toward workable ranges, ADH drive decreases. Urine volume and concentration are consequences of the response, not the regulated variable itself.

Caffeine can modestly affect urine output in some contexts, especially for people who are not habituated, but describing it simply as shutting off ADH is inaccurate. Fluid balance depends on dose, usual intake, kidneys, hormones, and total fluid consumed.

Interpret a disorder pattern

4. Diabetes insipidus is a water-balance disorder

Central diabetes insipidus involves insufficient ADH production or release. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus involves reduced kidney response to ADH. Both can produce large volumes of dilute urine and increased thirst, but their hormone patterns differ.

In a synthetic case, high plasma osmotic concentration with low ADH suggests inadequate central release. High ADH with persistently dilute urine suggests weak kidney response. These are mechanism patterns, not a complete diagnosis; kidney function, medicines, fluid intake, and additional testing matter.

Diabetes insipidus is unrelated to blood-glucose regulation in diabetes mellitus. The shared historical word refers to increased urination, but the regulated variables and hormones differ. One concerns water balance and ADH; the other concerns glucose regulation and insulin-related pathways.

Regulate metabolism

1. TSH and thyroxine form a multi-level axis

Hypothalamic signals stimulate anterior-pituitary release of thyroid-stimulating hormone. TSH acts on the thyroid gland, supporting thyroxine synthesis and release. Thyroxine increases metabolic activity in many tissues and is important for growth and nervous-system development.

Thyroxine feeds back to reduce hypothalamic and pituitary drive. If thyroid output falls in an otherwise intact axis, TSH usually rises. If both thyroxine and TSH are low, inadequate hypothalamic or pituitary signalling becomes a candidate. Values must be interpreted with timing, health context, and appropriate clinical reference ranges.

Iodine is required for thyroid-hormone synthesis. Inadequate iodine can limit output and increase TSH drive, contributing to thyroid enlargement. Goitre describes enlargement and can occur in more than one thyroid condition; it does not by itself state whether hormone output is high or low.

Describe imbalance

2. Thyroid disorders change physiology in different directions

Hypothyroidism means insufficient thyroid-hormone action for the body's needs. Possible effects include fatigue, cold intolerance, slowed processes, and changes in growth or development. Hyperthyroidism means excessive thyroid-hormone action and may involve heat intolerance, increased heart activity, and metabolic changes. Symptoms overlap with many conditions, so laboratory and clinical evidence are required.

Graves disease is an autoimmune cause of hyperthyroidism in which antibodies stimulate the TSH receptor. TSH can be low because high thyroid hormone suppresses pituitary drive even while receptor-stimulating antibodies keep the thyroid active. This pattern shows why a gland can be overactive despite a low pituitary signal.

Untreated congenital hypothyroidism can seriously affect growth and nervous-system development. Historical sources used a stigmatizing term that is not appropriate learner language. Current instruction uses congenital hypothyroidism and focuses on early screening and treatment as health technologies.

Regulate calcium

3. PTH and calcitonin act on overlapping targets

Low blood calcium increases parathyroid hormone release. PTH supports calcium release from bone, calcium reabsorption by kidneys, and activation of vitamin D pathways that increase intestinal calcium absorption. These responses raise blood calcium and reduce the original stimulus for PTH release.

Calcitonin is released by thyroid C cells when blood calcium is high and can reduce bone resorption. In adult human calcium regulation, PTH and vitamin D pathways are generally more central to minute-to-minute control than calcitonin. Calling the two hormones perfectly equal opposites oversimplifies their physiological importance.

Calcium is required for synaptic release, muscle contraction, blood clotting, and many intracellular signals. Regulation must balance blood concentration with bone storage and organ function. A short-term rise in blood calcium does not necessarily mean total body calcium increased.

Case C shows low calcium with high PTH, which indicates that parathyroid sensing and release are responding, while target response, vitamin D, kidney function, or calcium availability may be limiting.

Build the mechanism

1. Pancreatic islets connect a variable to a hormone signal

The pancreas has both exocrine and endocrine roles. Its exocrine tissue releases digestive substances through ducts. Small clusters called pancreatic islets release hormones directly into blood. Within the islets, beta cells secrete insulin and alpha cells secrete glucagon.

These cells respond to blood-glucose conditions. When glucose rises, beta-cell insulin release increases. When glucose falls, insulin release decreases and alpha-cell glucagon release increases. Hormones travel through the bloodstream, but only cells with the appropriate receptors and response machinery react in a particular way.

A needed correction: insulin does not simply “open every cell”

Insulin strongly supports glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue and changes glucose storage and production in the liver. Several tissues—including the brain and red blood cells—use glucose transport that is not dependent on insulin in the same way. The regulation is tissue-specific.

2. Insulin and glucagon oppose the disturbance

Read the two branches

Each branch reduces its initiating disturbance. A rise increases insulin signalling and responses that lower the rise. A fall increases glucagon signalling and liver glucose output, opposing the fall. As the variable returns toward its regulated range, the original hormonal drive decreases.

4. Urinalysis reveals clues—and important limits

The kidneys filter blood and normally reabsorb nearly all filtered glucose. When blood glucose and the filtered glucose load become high enough, transporters may be unable to reabsorb all of it, so glucose appears in urine. In a person with diabetes, high ketones together with relevant symptoms can signal diabetic ketoacidosis, which requires urgent medical care. In this course, ketone values are only synthetic evidence for reasoning about fuel use.

Separate adrenal regions

1. The medulla and cortex are different endocrine tissues

The adrenal medulla receives sympathetic neural input and releases epinephrine and norepinephrine into blood. These hormones support rapid changes in heart activity, airway function, blood distribution, and fuel availability. The response can begin quickly because preganglionic neurons directly activate medullary cells.

The adrenal cortex responds to different signals. ACTH from the anterior pituitary stimulates cortisol release from the cortex. Cortisol supports longer-duration fuel mobilization, influences immune and cardiovascular function, and helps the body respond to sustained demand. The cortex also releases aldosterone, regulated mainly through kidney-related renin-angiotensin signals and blood potassium, rather than ACTH alone.

Stress is not one hormone or one pathway. Sensory appraisal, sympathetic nerves, adrenal medulla, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, behaviour, and recovery systems interact. A useful model separates timing and route before integrating the phases.

Regulate water and sodium

2. ADH and aldosterone alter different kidney processes

ADH increases water permeability in kidney collecting ducts, allowing more water to be reabsorbed. Its release responds strongly to osmotic concentration and also to volume or pressure signals. The immediate effect is water conservation and more concentrated urine when the kidneys can respond.

Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion in distal nephron regions. Water can follow retained sodium, supporting extracellular-fluid volume and blood pressure. Aldosterone is part of a broader renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activated by kidney evidence of reduced perfusion or sodium delivery.

The hormones are related but not interchangeable. ADH primarily changes water permeability; aldosterone changes sodium transport. Drinking excess water can lower osmotic concentration and suppress ADH without necessarily suppressing aldosterone in the same pattern. Blood and urine evidence must be read together.

Epinephrine rises sharply at the startle point and returns quickly. Cortisol rises more slowly. Later, plasma osmotic concentration and ADH rise while urine volume falls and concentration increases.

Aldosterone rises modestly when volume-related signals increase. The data are consistent with layered regulation: neural-adrenal medulla activity for the rapid phase, an HPA-axis contribution over a longer interval, and kidney-hormone responses during water conservation.

Evaluate technology

4. Hormone technologies expand capability and create responsibilities

Hormone technologies include recombinant hormone production, insulin-delivery systems, continuous glucose monitoring, receptor agonists and antagonists, replacement therapy, and laboratory hormone assays. Each addresses a defined physiological or measurement problem. Evidence of biochemical effect is not automatically evidence of long-term benefit for every user.

Evaluation should include scientific accuracy, clinical outcome evidence, safety, access, user burden, privacy, cost, and unintended use. A growth-related hormone can be medically appropriate for specific diagnosed conditions while non-medical use creates different risks and ethical questions. A monitoring device can improve trend awareness while producing alarms, data overload, or inequitable access.

Science and technology have intended and unintended consequences. A balanced brief identifies the need, mechanism, evidence, affected groups, trade-offs, and next question. It avoids advertising language and does not tell an individual to start, stop, or change treatment.

Meet the case

1. A changing environment recruits overlapping control systems

A synthetic learner walks from a warm building into cold air, hears a sudden traffic signal, then continues a long walk without a drink. Before leaving, the learner ate a meal containing carbohydrate. The case includes pupil observations, heart activity, blood-glucose trends, hormone signals, plasma osmotic concentration, and urine data.

Cold receptors and auditory hair cells transduce different forms of energy. Sensory neurons carry patterned activity into central pathways. The brain integrates the information, organizes voluntary action, and changes autonomic output. Sympathetic pathways rapidly influence pupils, heart, vessels, and adrenal medulla.

At the same time, pancreatic islets respond to changing blood glucose, and hypothalamic pathways respond to osmotic and volume evidence. The case is not one chain. It is a network of regulated variables, signals, targets, delays, and feedback loops.

Read before explaining

2. Separate observations by time and variable

At five minutes, heart activity and epinephrine are high while cortisol has changed little. This timing supports rapid sympathetic and adrenal-medulla involvement. At forty-five minutes, epinephrine is closer to baseline while cortisol is elevated. The later pattern supports a slower HPA-axis contribution.

Blood glucose rises after the meal, insulin signalling rises, and glucose trends back toward baseline. During continued walking, glucose begins to fall and glucagon signalling increases. The variable and hormone move in directions consistent with feedback; a hormone value alone would not reveal the full pattern.

After two hours without fluid, osmotic concentration and ADH are elevated while urine volume is low and concentrated. This is consistent with water conservation. The response reduces water loss but cannot immediately replace water already lost. Homeostasis limits disturbance; it does not make resources unlimited.

Test hypotheses

3. Contradictory evidence improves the model

A first hypothesis might claim that cortisol caused the immediate heart response. The five-minute evidence weakens that claim because epinephrine and autonomic output change rapidly while cortisol has barely risen. The hypothesis should be revised: the immediate phase is primarily neural and adrenal-medulla supported, while cortisol contributes later metabolic support.

A second hypothesis might claim that rising ADH caused the osmotic concentration to increase. The causal direction is reversed. Water loss raised osmotic concentration, which increased ADH release; ADH then reduced further water loss. The return effect is the defining feedback link.

A third hypothesis might claim that insulin acts identically on every tissue. The course model instead identifies tissue-specific responses: muscle and adipose uptake, liver storage and reduced output, and tissues with glucose transport that is not insulin-dependent in the same way. Precision prevents a useful simplification from becoming a factual error.

Evaluate mastery

5. Transfer means applying the model to a new context

Mastery is more than naming structures. You should be able to infer a pathway from a data pattern, predict the result of a disruption, distinguish a signal from a regulated variable, and identify evidence that would change your conclusion.

For nervous control, connect receptor transduction, neuron structure, action potentials, synapses, central integration, and effectors. For endocrine control, connect release, transport, receptor binding, target response, clearance, and feedback. For both, identify timing, uncertainty, and interaction.

When a question provides unfamiliar data, begin with what changed. Name the variable and unit, compare conditions, and identify timing. Only then select a mechanism. If two hypotheses fit, state the additional evidence that would distinguish them.

Unit A glossary

accessibility

Design that lets learners perceive, operate, understand, and navigate required content using different devices and access needs.

acetylcholine

A neurotransmitter used at neuromuscular junctions and in many autonomic and central nervous system pathways.

ACTH

Adrenocorticotropic hormone; an anterior-pituitary hormone that stimulates cortisol release from the adrenal cortex.

ADH

Antidiuretic hormone; synthesized in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary to increase kidney water reabsorption.

adrenal cortex

The outer adrenal tissue that produces steroid hormones including cortisol and aldosterone.

adrenal medulla

The inner adrenal tissue activated by sympathetic neurons to release epinephrine and norepinephrine into blood.

agonist

A substance that activates a receptor or increases the effect of its normal signalling pathway.

aldosterone

An adrenal-cortex hormone that increases kidney sodium reabsorption and potassium secretion, influencing fluid volume and pressure.

antagonist

A substance that blocks a receptor or reduces the effect of its normal signalling pathway.

antagonistic hormones

Hormones whose physiological effects move a regulated variable in opposing directions, such as insulin and glucagon in a simplified glucose model.

audiogram

A graph of the lowest sound levels detected at selected frequencies under standardized conditions.

autonomic

The peripheral motor division that regulates smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands, and other largely involuntary functions.

axon

A neuron process that carries action potentials away from the main receptive and integration regions toward terminals.

calcitonin

A hormone released by thyroid C cells that can reduce bone resorption when blood calcium is high.

central nervous system

The brain and spinal cord, where extensive neural integration occurs.

chemoreceptor

A receptor that responds to a chemical substance or chemical condition such as dissolved gas concentration.

cholinesterase

An enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine and helps terminate its synaptic action.

claim

A specific conclusion that answers a scientific question and can be supported or revised using evidence.

cochlea

The spiral inner-ear structure in which sound-driven fluid and membrane movement stimulates auditory hair cells.

cone

A retinal photoreceptor that supports colour and high-acuity vision, especially in brighter light.

control centre

A component that integrates input and organizes an output in a control-system model.

controlled variable

A condition kept as consistent as possible so an investigation can isolate the intended comparison.

cornea

The transparent front surface of the eye that provides much of the eye's refraction.

corpus callosum

A large tract of axons connecting the cerebral hemispheres and supporting interhemispheric communication.

cortisol

A steroid hormone from the adrenal cortex that supports longer-duration stress responses, metabolism, and other regulatory functions.

dendrite

A branched neuron process specialized to receive synaptic or sensory input.

depolarization

A change that makes membrane potential less negative, and sometimes positive, relative to its resting value.

diabetes mellitus

A group of disorders involving persistent blood-glucose dysregulation caused by problems with insulin production, action, or both.

effector

A muscle, gland, or target tissue that changes activity in response to an output signal.

endocrine gland

A gland that releases hormones into extracellular fluid and blood rather than through a duct.

epinephrine

A catecholamine released by the adrenal medulla that supports rapid whole-body responses to sympathetic activation.

evidence

Relevant observations or measurements used to support, challenge, or revise a claim.

Eustachian tube

A passage connecting the middle ear with the throat region that helps equalize pressure across the tympanic membrane.

feedback loop

A control relationship in which a system's response changes the condition that initiated the response.

fovea centralis

A small retinal region with very high cone density and high visual acuity.

general adaptation syndrome

A historical model describing broad phases of physiological response to persistent stress; current stress biology is more variable and context dependent.

glucagon

A pancreatic alpha-cell hormone that supports increased blood glucose, especially through liver glucose release.

glycogen

A branched glucose-storage polymer found mainly in liver and skeletal muscle.

hGH

Human growth hormone; an anterior-pituitary hormone that supports growth and metabolism directly and through growth factors.

homeostasis

Coordinated maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite internal and external change.

hormone

A chemical signal released into body fluids that changes the activity of responsive target cells.

hyperglycemia

Blood glucose above the expected physiological range for the defined context.

hyperpolarization

A membrane-potential change that makes the inside more negative than its usual resting value.

hypothalamus

A brain region that integrates internal and external information and coordinates autonomic, behavioural, and pituitary responses.

insulin

A pancreatic beta-cell hormone that supports glucose uptake in responsive tissues, storage, and reduced liver glucose output.

integration

The processing and combination of inputs to produce a coordinated response or scientific explanation.

interneuron

A neuron located within the central nervous system that integrates or relays information between neurons.

iris

The coloured muscular structure that changes pupil diameter and regulates how much light enters the eye.

islet cell

An endocrine cell in a pancreatic islet, including insulin-releasing beta cells and glucagon-releasing alpha cells.

lens

A transparent, flexible eye structure that changes shape to fine-tune focus on the retina.

limitation

A feature of evidence, design, model, or method that restricts what a conclusion can establish.

lipid-soluble hormone

A hormone that can cross cell membranes and often binds an intracellular receptor to influence gene expression.

mechanism

A causal account of the structures, signals, and processes that produce an observed pattern.

mechanoreceptor

A receptor activated by physical deformation such as pressure, stretch, vibration, or fluid movement.

motor neuron

A neuron that carries output from the central nervous system toward a muscle or gland effector.

myelin

A lipid-rich glial wrapping that insulates axon segments and supports rapid, efficient conduction.

negative feedback

Feedback in which a response reduces the original departure of a regulated variable.

neuron

An excitable cell specialized to receive, integrate, and transmit information.

neurotransmitter

A chemical released by a neuron that binds receptors on another cell across a synapse.

nociceptor

A receptor that responds to potentially tissue-damaging mechanical, thermal, or chemical conditions.

node of Ranvier

A gap between myelin segments where voltage-gated channels regenerate an action potential.

norepinephrine

A catecholamine used as a neurotransmitter in many sympathetic pathways and as a circulating adrenal-medulla signal.

oligodendrocyte

A central nervous system glial cell that can form myelin around segments of several axons.

optic nerve

The bundle of retinal ganglion-cell axons carrying visual information from the eye toward the brain.

organ of Corti

The sensory structure on the cochlear basilar membrane that contains auditory hair cells.

osmotic pressure

The pressure associated with a difference in solute concentration across a water-permeable boundary; in this course it represents the drive for water movement.

ossicles

The malleus, incus, and stapes bones that transmit middle-ear vibration to the oval window.

parasympathetic

An autonomic division that supports functions such as digestion, energy conservation, and recovery in many contexts.

parathyroid

One of the small glands near the thyroid that releases PTH in response to low blood calcium.

peripheral nervous system

Nerves and associated structures outside the brain and spinal cord.

photoperiod

The duration of light and darkness within a daily cycle, which can provide seasonal timing information.

photoreceptor

A receptor cell that transduces light, such as a retinal rod or cone.

pinna

The external ear structure that collects sound and contributes to location cues.

pituitary

An endocrine and neural-endocrine organ controlled by the hypothalamus, with distinct anterior and posterior regions.

proprioceptor

A mechanoreceptor that provides information about body position, muscle length, tension, or movement.

PTH

Parathyroid hormone; a hormone that raises blood calcium through coordinated effects on bone, kidneys, and vitamin D pathways.

pupil

The opening in the iris through which light enters the eye.

reasoning

The explanation that connects evidence to a claim using scientific principles and mechanisms.

receptor

A sensory structure that detects a condition or a molecular protein that binds a signalling substance, depending on context.

reflex

A rapid, predictable response organized through a neural circuit, often before conscious processing is complete.

refractory period

The interval after an action potential when another spike is impossible or requires a stronger stimulus because channels are recovering.

regulated variable

A measurable internal condition that a control system monitors and keeps within a workable range.

releasing hormone

A hypothalamic hormone that travels through portal blood to control anterior-pituitary secretion.

repolarization

The return of membrane potential toward a negative value after depolarization, driven largely by potassium efflux.

resting potential

The maintained voltage difference across an unstimulated cell membrane, commonly near −70 mV in a model neuron.

retina

The neural tissue lining the back of the eye that contains photoreceptors and early visual-processing circuits.

reuptake

Transport of released neurotransmitter back into a presynaptic neuron or supporting cell, helping end the signal.

rod

A highly light-sensitive retinal photoreceptor that supports dim-light and much peripheral vision but not colour vision.

Schwann cell

A peripheral nervous system glial cell that can form one myelin segment around one axon.

semicircular canal

One of three inner-ear ducts whose hair cells respond mainly to rotational acceleration.

sensory adaptation

A reduced receptor or perceptual response during an unchanging stimulus.

sensory neuron

A neuron that carries information from a receptor toward the central nervous system.

set point

A reference value or regulated range around which control responses are organized; it can shift with context.

somatic

The peripheral division associated with skin and skeletal-muscle sensory input and skeletal-motor output.

stimulus

A detectable internal or external change that can initiate receptor or control-system activity.

sympathetic

An autonomic division that supports rapid mobilization, cardiovascular adjustment, and adrenal-medulla activation in many contexts.

synapse

A junction where a neuron communicates with another neuron, muscle fibre, or gland cell.

target cell

A cell that responds to a signal because it has a compatible receptor and response machinery.

target tissue

A group of cells that responds to a neural or endocrine signal.

technology

A designed tool or process that applies scientific knowledge to a need and can create intended and unintended consequences.

thermoreceptor

A receptor that responds across a range of temperatures or to temperature change.

threshold

The membrane-potential level at which enough voltage-gated sodium channels open to initiate an action potential.

thyroid

An endocrine gland that produces thyroid hormones affecting metabolism, growth, and development.

thyroxine

A thyroid hormone that increases metabolic activity in many tissues and participates in hypothalamic-pituitary feedback.

tropic hormone

A hormone that stimulates another endocrine gland, such as TSH acting on the thyroid.

TSH

Thyroid-stimulating hormone; an anterior-pituitary hormone that stimulates thyroid-hormone synthesis and release.

tympanum

The tympanic membrane or eardrum, which vibrates when sound reaches it.

urinalysis

Analysis of urine characteristics or contents; classroom results can reveal patterns but are not diagnostic by themselves.

water-soluble hormone

A hormone that binds a membrane receptor and activates intracellular signalling because it does not readily cross the lipid bilayer.

A reliable data-reading routine

  1. Read the axes, units, and conditions. Identify what was measured and when.
  2. Describe before explaining. Name the baseline, direction, magnitude, timing, and any overlap.
  3. Connect a mechanism. Use a receptor, neural route, hormone, target, or feedback effect that predicts the pattern.
  4. Test an alternative. State another explanation and the evidence that would distinguish it.
  5. Bound the claim. Label synthetic data, design limits, and what the evidence cannot diagnose.
