Mutualism, commensalism and parasitism

How do we classify an interaction from its effects on both partners?

Learning goal

Use benefit, harm and no detected effect to classify close interspecific relationships.

Before you begin

Interactions can change survival and reproduction. The effect on each partner must be considered separately.

Vocabulary help

Key terms

Words for this lesson

Use these definitions when you need help with a term.

A close association between organisms of different species.
An interaction in which both partners benefit in the stated conditions.
An interaction in which one partner benefits and the other has no appreciable effect in the stated model.
An interaction in which a parasite benefits while harming its host.

Score both sides of the relationship

describes a close association between species. The association may help both partners, help one without an appreciable effect on the other, or help one while harming the other.

Use two signs to organize the effects. is +/+, is +/0, and is +/−. The signs describe benefits or costs in the context, not moral judgments about the organisms.

Connect the signs with a mechanism

A mutualistic pollination case can provide food to a pollinator and transfer pollen for a plant. An epiphyte can gain physical support on a tree without taking nourishment from the tree; when no appreciable host effect is established, the model is commensalism.

A parasite obtains resources from a host and imposes a cost. A tick feeding on a host is a familiar example. Parasites need not immediately kill the host, and the severity of harm varies.

Name the resource or service and explain the effect on each partner. A label alone does not show that you understand the interaction.

Classifications depend on the supplied evidence

A relationship can change with environmental conditions or with the level being measured. An organism described as harmless in one setting may impose a detectable cost in another.

“No effect” is especially difficult to establish absolutely. In a classroom case, use the stated absence of an appreciable effect rather than inventing an unreported benefit or harm. In research, the conclusion depends on the measurements and their sensitivity.

Keep competition, and the close associations considered here conceptually distinct. Several interactions can occur in the same community.

Worked example

Classify from measured effects

A hypothetical small plant gains support on a tree. The provided observations show no appreciable change in the tree’s growth or reproduction.

  1. The small plant benefits: +.
  2. No appreciable effect on the tree is supplied: 0.
  3. The case fits a commensalism model under those observations.
  4. Do not invent nutrient theft and relabel it parasitism without evidence.

Mutualism provides a benefit to both (+/+). Commensalism provides a benefit to one with no appreciable effect on the other in the stated setting (+/0). Parasitism benefits the parasite while imposing a cost on the host (+/−). Each label should be tied to a mechanism.
