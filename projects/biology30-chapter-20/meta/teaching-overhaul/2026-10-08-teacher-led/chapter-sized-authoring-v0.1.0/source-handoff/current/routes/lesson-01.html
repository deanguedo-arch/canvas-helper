Populations, density and distribution

What does a population count tell you, and what does it leave out?

Learning goal

Key terms

Words for this lesson

The number of individuals per unit area or volume.
Individuals occurring in groups rather than evenly across the area.
Individuals spaced relatively evenly across a defined area.
A spatial pattern without a strong regular or clustered arrangement at the scale considered.

Define the unit before measuring it

A population consists of individuals of the same species in a defined area and time. A community contains populations of different species living and interacting there. A common biological species definition uses interbreeding and fertile offspring, but not every organism or species boundary fits that simple definition.

Population size, N, is the number of individuals. Density compares that number with an area or volume: D = N/A or D = N/V. Choose the denominator that matches the environment and question.

Keep units attached to the calculation

If 500 rabbits occupy 25 km², density is 500/25 = 20 rabbits/km². If 120 fish occupy 60 m³ of water, density is 2 fish/m³. A correct number without the spatial unit is an incomplete density answer.

The relationship can be rearranged. If density is 2.3 bears/km² over 50 km², the estimated population is N = DA = 115 bears. This assumes the density estimate applies to the stated area.

Samples and estimates have limits. A count from a particularly crowded patch should not automatically represent an entire landscape with different habitats.

Describe the spatial pattern separately

Clumped populations occur in groups, often around resources or social groups. Uniform patterns show relatively even spacing, which can be associated with competition or territorial behaviour. Random patterns lack a strong grouping or regular spacing at the scale examined.

Two areas can have the same total population and average density but different distributions. A map is needed to distinguish their spatial patterns. Conversely, a clumped diagram does not by itself provide an exact density without the count and area.

Use the measured pattern first, then propose a mechanism. The pattern alone does not always identify one unique cause.

Worked example

Use density to estimate abundance

A survey estimates 4 individuals per hectare across 35 hectares of comparable habitat.

  1. Given density D = 4 individuals/ha and area A = 35 ha.
  2. Use N = DA.
  3. N = 4 × 35 = 140 individuals.
  4. The hectares cancel, leaving individuals.
  5. The estimate assumes the sampled density represents the whole stated habitat.
