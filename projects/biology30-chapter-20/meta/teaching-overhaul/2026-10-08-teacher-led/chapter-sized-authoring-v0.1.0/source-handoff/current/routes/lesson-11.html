The distribution of individuals among age groups in a population.
A model describing changes in birth and death patterns associated with changing social and economic conditions.
The quantity of resources used by a population under stated conditions.
The capacity to maintain ecological and human systems over time without undermining the conditions they depend on.

Human populations also have demographic structure

Births, deaths and migration change human population size. matters because a population with many individuals approaching reproductive ages can have different future births from one of equal size with a different age distribution.

Demographic-transition models describe changes in birth and death patterns as conditions change. They summarize patterns rather than providing a fixed timetable that every population must follow. A projection depends on assumptions about future fertility, and migration.

Historical textbook statistics describe the period in which they were collected. Do not treat an undated older figure as a current world-population measurement.

Combine numbers with per-person use

Total depends on both the number of people and the amount used per person, along with technology and the type of resource. A smaller population with high per-person use can have greater total demand for a particular resource than a larger population with lower use.

For example, a hypothetical group of 1000 people using 3 units each demands 3000 units, while 2000 people using 1 unit each demand 2000 units. This simple accounting does not describe every environmental impact, but it shows why head count alone is insufficient.

Resource extraction, waste and land-use change can affect habitat, species interactions and population connectivity. The mechanisms and measurements must be specified for the case.

Evaluate evidence and trade-offs without inventing certainty

An ecological evaluation identifies the proposed action, affected populations, measured outcomes and uncertainties. It can compare resource renewal with use, or habitat changes with survival and reproduction. Different measures may point to different consequences.

A claim needs a time frame. Short-term output does not by itself demonstrate that a resource or ecosystem can support the same use indefinitely. Human needs and ecological conditions should both be made explicit in a case analysis.

The course asks for biological reasoning and evidence interpretation, not a prescribed political choice. Use supplied hypothetical data and dated source records rather than inventing current demographic numbers.

Worked example

Compare total resource demand

Community A has 800 people using 4 units per person per day. Community B has 1600 people using 1.5 units each per day.

  1. A demand = 800 × 4 = 3200 units/day.
  2. B demand = 1600 × 1.5 = 2400 units/day.
  3. A is smaller but has greater demand for this specified resource.
  4. The comparison does not automatically measure every ecological impact or explain all differences.
