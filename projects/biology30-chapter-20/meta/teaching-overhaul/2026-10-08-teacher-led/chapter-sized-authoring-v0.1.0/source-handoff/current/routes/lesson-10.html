Primary and secondary succession

How do starting conditions influence community change after disturbance?

Learning goal

Distinguish primary and secondary succession and explain changing community composition without assuming one inevitable endpoint.

Before you begin

Communities contain interacting populations. Soil, resources and existing organisms influence which species can establish.

Key terms

Words for this lesson

Use these definitions when you need help with a term.

Change in community composition through time following establishment or disturbance.
Community development on a surface initially lacking developed soil and much biological legacy.
Community change after disturbance where soil and some biological legacy remain.
Early colonizers able to establish in the initial conditions.

Start with the substrate and biological legacy

Succession is change in community composition over time. begins where developed soil and much biological legacy are absent, such as a newly exposed rock surface. Early colonizers must tolerate those starting conditions.

occurs after disturbance when soil and some organisms, seeds or other biological material remain. Those resources can support faster establishment than on bare rock, although the actual rate depends on the site.

The name of a disturbance does not settle the classification. A fire that leaves soil differs from an event that removes it. Examine the starting conditions.

Organisms modify the conditions for later populations

Early colonizers can change a site by adding organic material, influencing soil conditions or altering light and moisture. Those changes can permit some later species and make conditions less suitable for others.

Competition, dispersal and interactions also influence which populations establish. Not every later species is simply “better” than an earlier one; it may be favoured by different conditions.

The community can become more structurally complex, but disturbance can interrupt or redirect the sequence. Local climate, available colonists and landform affect the pathway.

Treat climax as a model, not a permanent promise

A climax community is traditionally described as a relatively stable later-stage community under prevailing conditions. That term can help compare early and later stages, but it should not imply that the ecosystem stops changing forever.

Real landscapes contain patches at different stages. Weather, fire, disease and human activities can alter conditions repeatedly. Succession therefore need not follow one universal species list or end at one inevitable permanent state.

Use the supplied evidence to explain why a community changes, rather than memorizing a sequence of names without its environmental mechanism.

Worked example

Classify two disturbed sites

Site A is exposed rock after glacial retreat. Site B loses above-ground vegetation but retains soil and a seed bank.

  1. Site A lacks developed soil and begins a primary-succession case.
  2. Site B retains important biological legacy and fits secondary succession.
  3. The retained soil and seeds can support quicker establishment at B.
  4. Actual species and timing still depend on local conditions.
