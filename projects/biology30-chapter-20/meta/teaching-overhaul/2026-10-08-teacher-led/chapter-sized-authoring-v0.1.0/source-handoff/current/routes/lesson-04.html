Exponential and logistic growth

How do resource limits change the shape of population growth?

Before you begin

Population growth depends on reproductive contribution and losses. A graph shows a relationship over time rather than the entire mechanism.

Read the axes before naming the curve

On a population-size graph, the horizontal axis shows time and the vertical axis shows N. The slope describes how quickly N changes. A high point indicates many individuals, while a steep upward slope indicates rapid numerical increase.

Under an exponential model with a positive per-capita growth rate, more individuals produce more additions in each equal interval. The curve becomes increasingly steep, giving a J-like pattern. Constant proportional growth is different from adding the same fixed number each time.

Resource limits reduce per-capita growth

A logistic model includes limits associated with , K. When N is small relative to K, resources permit relatively rapid increase. As population size approaches K, competition and other constraints reduce net growth. The idealized curve is S-shaped.

At K in the simplest closed model, births and deaths balance on average. Births do not necessarily stop. Individuals continue to enter and leave the population even when the net count is stable.

Some teaching graphs include an initial lag while organisms establish and reproduce. A lag is a biological feature of that example, not a requirement of every mathematical exponential or logistic curve.

A model is not an unlimited forecast

cannot persist indefinitely in a finite environment. Resources, space, disease or other limits eventually matter. A population can overshoot a sustainable level and decline, but the timing and magnitude require evidence.

Real logistic-like populations can fluctuate around changing resource limits. K is not a fixed universal number attached to a species. Weather, habitat and interactions can alter the number supported.

Interpret the graph together with the stated conditions. Do not infer a particular limiting mechanism from curve shape alone.

Worked example

Read size and rate separately

An S-shaped graph is almost flat near its highest population level.

  1. The vertical position indicates a large N.
  2. The nearly flat slope indicates low net numerical growth.
  3. Under the ideal logistic model, additions and losses are approximately balanced near K.
  4. It is incorrect to call the highest N the point of fastest growth merely because it is highest.

Exponential growth assumes conditions allowing a constant positive per-capita rate, so numerical additions increase with N. Logistic growth includes resource limits that reduce per-capita net growth near K. Neither is a guarantee that a real population follows a smooth curve indefinitely.
