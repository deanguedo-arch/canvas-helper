Growth rate and per-capita growth

Why does the same numerical increase mean different things for different populations?

Learning goal

Calculate average growth per time and the course’s per-capita change over an interval.

Before you begin

Net change is final minus initial size. A rate or proportion requires an additional denominator.

Key terms

Words for this lesson

Use these definitions when you need help with a term.

Population change divided by elapsed time.
Population change divided by the initial population for a specified interval.
The population count at the start of the stated interval.
The elapsed time between the starting and ending observations.

Divide by time for average growth rate

compares net change with elapsed time: gr = ΔN/Δt. If a population grows from 170 to 245 over six years, ΔN = 75 and gr = 75/6 = 12.5 individuals per year.

This is an average across the interval. It does not imply that exactly 12.5 individuals were added in each individual year. The actual yearly pattern could vary.

Divide by the starting population for relative change

The course relationship cgr = ΔN/N₀ expresses over the stated interval. For the same example, cgr = 75/170 ≈ 0.441, or a 44.1% increase over six years.

Do not label 0.441 as an annual rate. If an average per-capita change per year is explicitly requested, the interval change also needs a time denominator. That average is still different from a continuously compounded or exponential growth parameter.

Using N₀ makes the change relative to the starting population. Replacing it with the final count answers a different question.

Compare populations using the appropriate measure

A gain of 20 individuals is a 20% increase for a starting population of 100 but only a 2% increase for a starting population of 1000. Equal numerical changes can therefore have different relative significance.

A complete calculation identifies the requested quantity, writes the relationship, substitutes values and includes the interval or units. Keep density, net change, average rate and per-capita change separate.

If the population declines, both ΔN and its rate or interval proportion are negative. A negative result is not automatically an arithmetic error.

Worked example

One dataset, three answers

A population increases from 205 to 253 over twenty years.

  1. Net change = 253 − 205 = 48 individuals.
  2. Average growth rate = 48/20 = 2.4 individuals/year.
  3. Per-capita change over the interval = 48/205 ≈ 0.234.
  4. This is about a 23.4% increase over twenty years, not 23.4% per year.
  5. Each answer describes a different quantity.
