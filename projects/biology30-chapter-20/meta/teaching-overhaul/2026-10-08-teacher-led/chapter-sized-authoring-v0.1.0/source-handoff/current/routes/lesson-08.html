Competition can occur without direct contact

Competition arises when organisms use a shared resource that limits their performance. Plants may compete for water without touching each other. Animals may compete for food, shelter or mating opportunities.

occurs within one species. occurs between species. Both can affect growth, survival or reproduction and therefore population size.

Simply using the same resource does not prove strong competition if that resource is abundant. Evidence that one user reduces another’s access or performance strengthens the claim.

Niche overlap helps explain the interaction

An includes the resources and conditions a species uses and its interactions. Habitat identifies where it lives; niche includes how it functions there.

Species with strong overlap in a limiting resource can compete. Under a simple competitive-exclusion model, complete overlap in a limiting niche can prevent indefinite stable coexistence under constant conditions. Real environments vary and often provide several resource dimensions.

Do not turn this principle into a claim that any two species in the same habitat cannot coexist.

Partitioning changes the overlap

occurs when species use different resources, locations or times. Two birds feeding in different parts of a tree may reduce overlap even though they share the same general habitat.

A study can compare performance with and without a competitor or measure resource use directly. A population increase after a competitor is removed is consistent with competition, but other changed conditions must also be considered.

Explain the limiting resource and the effect on the organisms rather than simply labelling every negative interaction “competition.” and parasitism involve different mechanisms.

Worked example

Classify the competitors

Two spruce seedlings compete for light, while a spruce and a cedar compete for soil water.

  1. The two spruce seedlings belong to the same species: intraspecific competition.
  2. Spruce and cedar are different species: interspecific competition.
  3. The proposed resources are light and water respectively.
  4. Evidence of limitation is needed to determine the strength of each interaction.

Competition within a species, such as two spruce seedlings sharing limited light, is intraspecific. Competition between species, such as spruce and cedar sharing limited water, is interspecific. Both involve a resource whose availability constrains performance.
