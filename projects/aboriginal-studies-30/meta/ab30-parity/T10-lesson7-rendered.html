Numbered Treaties: Purposes, Language, and Understandings

Why Ottawa wanted treaties and why each side heard something different. Questions 28-31 are answered in this lesson — your writing saves here.

The question this lesson investigates

Between 1871 and 1921, the Canadian government signed eleven numbered treaties with First Nations across the West and North. Both sides came to the councils with reasons, both sides spoke, and both sides signed. This lesson investigates a harder question than who signed what: when the signing was over, did both sides believe they had agreed to the same thing?

You will work with two kinds of evidence from your textbook band (Chapter 1, pages 24-28): the textbook's own summaries and explanations, and a published retrospective account from a former Dene Tha' chief about the Treaty 8 adhesion of 1899. Keep track of which kind you are reading at every step: a summary written a century later and a community account passed down through Elders can both be serious evidence, but they cannot show the same things.

Why Ottawa wanted treaties: five purposes

The textbook lists five things the federal government wanted when it negotiated the numbered treaties: farmland for settlers, land for a railway across the West, overland access to the Yukon gold discoveries, timber in northern Ontario, and treaties after oil was found at Fort William. Earlier notes for this course said four and left out the farming purpose; count five, because Question 28 asks what the government wanted and the textbook answers with all five.

Notice what connects them. The fur trade was declining, settlement and industry were rising, and every purpose on the list needs the same thing underneath: access to land already occupied by First Nations, Metis, and Inuit people. Ottawa's reasons explain why the government came to the table — and why it pressed hardest for a written surrender of title.

Sharing or surrendering? What each side thought signing meant

Here is the central split of the lesson. The textbook states it in one stark pair of sentences: the government saw the treaties as First Nations agreeing to surrender their claim to the land, while First Nations leaders saw them as agreements to share it. Surrender ends a claim; sharing continues a relationship. No later detail makes sense until you hold both meanings at once.

First Nations came with their own purposes. In exchange for letting settlers onto their land, they wanted help adapting to a new economy, a place to live, freedom to keep their ways of life as far as possible, and support while everything around them changed. Those wants are the direct answer to Question 30 — but read them as negotiating goals, not as a list of things the written treaties actually delivered.

Two ideas of what a treaty is

The gap ran deeper than competing wish lists. In the European tradition the textbook describes, nations signed treaties recognizing sovereignty, regulating trade, and promising peace — written instruments between states. First Nations made treaties for similar practical reasons, but sealed them as sacred commitments: ceremonies such as pipe smoking bound the parties, not signatures on paper.

That is why the textbook's contrast matters for Question 29: Europeans tended to read a treaty the way they would read a business contract, while First Nations tended to receive it the way they would receive a sacred oath. A contract asks what the words obligate; an oath asks what the relationship requires. The councils of the 1870s joined people operating under opposite answers.

Why language made agreement harder

Even where both sides negotiated in good faith, they negotiated across a language gap and a culture gap at the same time. Interpreters had to carry exact meanings and larger implications together — what 'cede' legally does, not just what it denotes — and some words had no ready equivalent at all.

Two further cautions come from the textbook itself. The interpreters were government employees, which gave them reason to present government terms favourably whether or not any individual interpreter bent the truth. And nearly a century later, sixteen chiefs from the Northwest Territories argued in court that their ancestors would never have signed had they understood Ottawa's intentions — evidence about how the treaties were received, not a transcript of the councils.

How to compare two accounts without overclaiming

To compare two accounts without overclaiming, work one shared issue at a time. First name each source: who speaks, when, and what kind of text it is. Then pull one detail from each that bears on the SAME issue — purposes with purposes, understandings with understandings. Then state what agrees, what differs, and what neither source lets you conclude.

The worked example below compares the textbook's general surrender-versus-sharing explanation with the Ahnassay account of one 1899 adhesion. Watch what it does not do: it never treats one adhesion as proof about all eleven treaties, and it never treats the textbook's summary as the words spoken at any council.

Worked: did both sides understand the 1899 adhesion the same way?

Follow each step. The question is bounded: one adhesion, one issue — what the signing transferred.

Evidence
  1. Textbook, p. 26 (general explanation): The government saw the treaties as surrender of the land claim; First Nations leaders saw them as agreements to share the land.
  2. Ahnassay account, p. 27 (Treaty 8 adhesion, 1899): Dene Tha' Elders did not know the written treaties had given most of their lands to newcomers, keeping only small reserves.
Reasoning
  1. Name the sources. The first is the textbook's general explanation of numbered-treaty understandings — a summary, not council minutes. The second is a former chief's retrospective account of his Nation's 1899 adhesion, reporting what Elders said.
  2. Pick one detail per source on the same issue. Issue: what did signing transfer? Detail one: Ottawa read signing as surrender of the claim. Detail two: the Dene Tha' learned afterward that the writing had transferred most of their lands.
  3. Compare. The details agree that a large transfer happened on paper and differ on whether it was understood at the council: the textbook's First Nations position expects sharing, while the Elders' reported surprise fits people who never knowingly surrendered.
  4. Limit the conclusion. One adhesion plus a general summary cannot prove every Nation was surprised at every treaty — but it can show that 'both sides signed' does not mean 'both sides understood the same transfer.'
Finished response

The two accounts describe the same signing pattern from opposite sides of understanding. Ottawa treated the 1899 adhesion the way the textbook says it treated the numbered treaties generally — as surrender of the land claim — while the Dene Tha', who signed to protect their traditional lands, reportedly did not know the written terms had given most of those lands away. The difference is not about whether a document was signed but about what each side believed the signature carried: for the government a transfer of title, for the Elders a sharing arrangement whose written terms surprised them. Because the evidence covers one adhesion and a general summary, it supports a limited conclusion: identical signatures are compatible with sharply different understandings of what was transferred.

Two jobs. First, three details are offered: (a) the business-contracts-versus-sacred-oaths contrast, (b) the list of treaty years 1871-1921, (c) Chief Chateh's week-long wait before signing. Choose the detail that best supports this explanation: 'The gap was about what a treaty IS, not only about what it SAID.' Second, repair this overgeneralization in one sentence: 'First Nations rejected everything the written treaties contained.'

Feedback

For the match: (a) is the load-bearing detail — it contrasts two ideas of a treaty itself, which is exactly what the explanation claims. (b) is a true but irrelevant fact here: dates sequence the treaties and cannot show what anyone believed a treaty to be; reaching for dates when asked about meanings is the classic drift. (c) is tempting and partly relevant — the wait shows care about protection — but it supports caution at one adhesion, not the general claim about what treaties are. For the repair: keep the true part (First Nations disputed key written terms, especially surrender language and omitted oral promises) and drop the 'everything': for example, 'First Nations leaders accepted the councils and sought protection through them, while disputing that the written terms captured the whole agreement.' Watch the confusion this repair avoids: the textbook's CLAIM that leaders saw sharing is our evidence; what any particular ancestor believed at any particular council is a further inference that needs its own source.
