How This Course Works

A repeatable learning process

  1. Read the brief and vocabulary. Identify the result you are working toward.
  2. Study the lessons and worked example. Revisit them when a decision becomes difficult.
  3. Try the practice decisions. Feedback explains the reasoning and you can retry.
  4. Create the required product in a document, presentation, drawing or recording. Use the response record to summarize decisions and identify your files.
  5. Check the rubric, revise, then choose Save to Portfolio. Print or download a backup when needed.

Plan your time

Each CTS code represents approximately 25 hours of access to instruction and practical learning. A suggested pattern is 6 hours of lessons and source investigation, 4 hours of examples and practice, 9 hours developing evidence, 4 hours testing and revising, and 2 hours evaluating and preparing submission. Adapt the pace with your teacher; there is no timer or automatic credit for hours.

Independent work and assessment

Use fictional customers and supplied data. You do not need to buy anything, travel, contact a business, recruit a partner or attend a live session. Written role responses and solo recordings let you practise applied skills. Some official outcomes require participation or performance evidence that a simulation alone may not demonstrate. Your teacher must validate those requirements before the affected module is released or awarded.

Project connections must refer to successfully completed eligible CTS courses, including one at the project’s level and in the same occupational area. Advanced projects connect to another intermediate or advanced course. Project and practicum codes cannot supply the connections. Teacher confirmation is separate from this page’s progress indicators.

Keep your work

Draft records save in this browser on this device. Different browsers and devices have separate storage. Download a JSON backup before moving devices or clearing browser data. A valid restore replaces the current course record; it does not merge drafts. Keep your original external files separately.

MAM1010 · Marketing & Management
MAM1010 · Learning module · Introductory


Northside Cycle is a fictional Alberta repair shop. It has reliable mechanics but irregular bookings. Develop a coherent marketing mix for one audience, explaining how the business will deliver its promises.
Learning focus: begin with a customer problem; make the four decisions agree; measure value and make an ethical decision.


1. Begin with a customer problem2. Make the four decisions agree3. Measure value and make an ethical decisionPracticeYour work


Words to know
Marketing
understanding needs and arranging exchanges that create value.
Target market
a defined group with a shared need or buying situation.
Marketing mix
connected product, price, place and promotion decisions.
Management
planning, organizing, leading and controlling resources.
1. Begin with a customer problem
Marketing begins before an advertisement is made. Identify the problem a customer wants solved and the alternatives available. A student might need a dependable bicycle for a short commute; a recreational rider might value a precise gear adjustment before a long ride. Both buy repairs, but their priorities differ. Describe the situation and evidence rather than assuming that everyone of the same age wants the same thing.


Segment a market using relevant needs, location, behaviour and circumstances. Then select a group the business can actually serve. A useful target description includes a need, a barrier and a reason to choose the offer. Avoid collecting real personal information for this course. The supplied fictional customer profiles give you sufficient evidence to practise.
Next lesson
2. Make the four decisions agree
Product includes the service, quality and support. Price includes the amount, payment terms and the customer's total cost. Place concerns where and how customers access the offer. Promotion explains the value through suitable communication. These decisions must reinforce one another: an affordable commuter service advertised to students should not require collection during school hours with no alternative.


Management turns the promise into an operation. Planning sets goals; organizing allocates tasks and resources; leading communicates expectations; controlling compares results with standards and adjusts. A promotion that attracts 40 bookings is unsuccessful if the shop can serve only 20 and disappoints the rest. Ask what staffing, scheduling and information the promise requires.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Measure value and make an ethical decision
Revenue is selling price multiplied by quantity, not profit. Costs and available capacity affect whether an offer can last. Compare enquiries, completed bookings, repeat visits and complaints rather than relying only on social-media attention. A measure is useful when it relates to the goal and can change a decision.


Marketing affects society through employment, access, consumption and the information people use. Technology can make booking easier but can exclude customers who cannot use an app. Offer an accessible alternative. Be clear about limitations and distinguish supported product claims from opinions. Professional judgement includes deciding what not to promise, even when a stronger claim might attract attention.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. The shop offers a fictional $35 basic tune-up with 20 appointments available each week. Its target is local student commuters who need predictable cost and after-school collection.
  2. Product: a clearly defined basic service; price: $35 with additional repairs quoted separately; place: online or phone booking and late collection; promotion: a readable school-area flyer. These choices address the same need.
  3. At 20 completed appointments, service revenue is 20 × $35 = $700 before costs. The shop tracks completed bookings and late collections. It limits advertising once capacity is reached rather than accepting work it cannot deliver.

MAM1020 · Quality Customer Service
MAM1020 · Learning module · Introductory

Words to know
Service standard
an observable expectation for how a service is delivered.
Active listening
checking understanding before deciding on a response.
Service recovery
action taken to address a service failure.
Escalation
referring a decision beyond your authority to the responsible person.
1. Understand the service encounter
Customer service includes what happens before, during and after a purchase. Reliability, timely information and respectful treatment all influence whether a person returns. A customer's frustration is information about a gap between an expectation and an experience; it is not permission to make assumptions about their character.


Listen for the requested outcome and the underlying need. Restate the concern in neutral language, then ask a focused question. Distinguish facts you can verify from claims you need to investigate. In a solo exercise, respond to the supplied customer statement aloud or in writing, then compare your response with the worked example.
Next lesson
2. Use policy without hiding behind it
A policy should support consistent decisions, but it does not answer every situation. Check what the policy covers, which remedies you can authorize and when to escalate. Explain the available choices and their consequences in language the customer can understand. Do not promise an exception you cannot deliver.


Fictional shop policy: obtain permission before additional work; notify customers of delays; refer refund disputes to the manager; provide booking by phone or online. A customer who cannot use the web form should receive the phone option without having to disclose a diagnosis. Collect only the information needed to arrange the service, and keep other customers' records private.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Close the loop and improve the system
A strong response has five parts: acknowledge, clarify, explain, offer a realistic next step, and confirm follow-up. An apology recognizes the experience without inventing facts or blaming a colleague. Record the issue accurately enough for the next person to act, using a fictional reference rather than unnecessary personal details.


Repeated complaints can reveal a process problem. If six customers misunderstand a quotation, examine the quotation wording instead of labelling all six customers difficult. Track the issue, change one element and compare results. Evaluate communication for clarity and usefulness rather than accent, personality or expensive presentation tools.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. Customer: 'You said my bike would be ready today. I rearranged my whole afternoon.' The parts delivery has been delayed until tomorrow.
  2. Response: 'You expected collection today and planned around it. I am sorry we did not notify you sooner. The replacement part is delayed. I can confirm the revised time tomorrow morning, or ask the manager about other available options. Which would be more useful?'
  3. The response recognizes the inconvenience, gives a verified explanation and offers choices within authority. A handover note records the promised update time so the next staff member can follow through.

  3. Add an internal handover note for each case and a simple service-improvement measure. Identify when a manager must decide.
  4. Review your language, privacy choices and follow-up promises. Revise the weakest response and explain what changed.

MAM2010 · Learning module · Intermediate

Learning focus: define quality before measuring it; diagnose before assigning blame; test an improvement and interpret the result.


1. Define quality before measuring it2. Diagnose before assigning blame3. Test an improvement and interpret the resultPracticeYour work


Words to know
Quality
how well an output meets an agreed need or standard.
Process
connected steps that turn inputs into an output.
Root cause
an underlying factor that contributes to a problem.
Continuous improvement
repeated measurement, change and evaluation.
1. Define quality before measuring it
Quality does not always mean the most expensive product. It means meeting an agreed requirement reliably. A repair can be mechanically sound yet fail a customer-service standard if it is ready two days late with no message. Write standards that another person could observe: notify a customer before the promised pickup time if work is delayed.


Separate inputs, process and outputs. Inputs include tools, parts, staff time and information. The process might be booking, inspection, approval, repair, quality check and collection. Outputs include a safe completed repair and an understandable invoice. A process map helps identify missing information and repeated work.
Next lesson
2. Diagnose before assigning blame
The fictional shop completed 40 repairs: 10 were late. Six late jobs waited for approval, three waited for parts, and one needed rework. These categories suggest where to investigate; they do not prove an individual's fault. The approval process may lack a clear contact step or decision deadline. Look for evidence at the point where work waits.


Managers coordinate through structure, leadership and communication. A supervisor can set a standard, assign responsibility and provide training, but needs feedback from the process. In this solo simulation, use the supplied roles and describe a handover. Recognize that written role descriptions are rehearsal evidence, not proof of collaborating with real colleagues.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Test an improvement and interpret the result
Use a plan–do–check–act cycle. Plan a limited change and predict its effect. Do a small test. Check results against the original measure. Act by keeping, adapting or rejecting the change. Change one important variable at a time where possible, and record other conditions that might influence the result.


After introducing an approval reminder, the next fictional batch has 4 late repairs out of 40. The late rate falls from 25% to 10%, a decrease of 15 percentage points. That is encouraging but does not prove the reminder caused all the change. Check workload, repair complexity and costs before expanding the process.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. Baseline: 10 late repairs ÷ 40 total repairs × 100 = 25%. The largest recorded delay category is waiting for customer approval.
  2. Test: the service desk sends an approval reminder at an agreed time and records whether a reply arrives. The repair team receives a clear approved/not-approved handover.
  3. Result: 4 ÷ 40 × 100 = 10% late. Recommend continuing the test for another comparable batch, checking that reminders do not create excessive workload or repeated unwanted messages.

Receive a fictional stock order, investigate a discrepancy, set a defensible price and prepare an operating record. Your calculations must be traceable enough for another person to check.
Learning focus: follow goods and information together; price with a clear denominator; write usable operating controls.


1. Follow goods and information together2. Price with a clear denominator3. Write usable operating controlsPracticeYour work


Words to know
Inventory
goods held for use or sale.
Stock discrepancy
a difference between recorded and counted goods.
Markup
difference between selling price and cost, expressed relative to cost when a percentage.
Gross margin
selling price minus cost, expressed relative to selling price when a percentage.
1. Follow goods and information together
Retail operations connect purchasing, receiving, storage, selling and record keeping. Compare the purchase order, delivery note, invoice and physical count rather than assuming they agree. Record damaged or missing items before approving an invoice. A clear discrepancy report lets the supplier investigate without guesswork.


Fictional order: 24 bicycle lights at $12 each. The delivery contains 22 lights; two of those have cracked cases. The count is therefore 22 received, 20 saleable, 2 damaged and 2 missing. Do not combine missing and damaged items into one number without explaining the categories. Keep stock secure and avoid putting damaged goods on sale.
Next lesson
2. Price with a clear denominator
At a $20 selling price and $12 unit cost, the difference is $8. Markup on cost is $8 ÷ $12 × 100, or about 66.67%. Gross margin is $8 ÷ $20 × 100, or 40%. These percentages describe the same transaction using different denominators. Label the method whenever you report a percentage.


Gross margin is not net profit: rent, wages and other operating costs still need to be covered. A discount can reduce the amount available for those costs. A 10% discount on $20 produces an $18 selling price and a $6 difference above the $12 cost. Consider demand, competition, product life cycle and stock age as well as arithmetic.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Write usable operating controls
A procedure describes an action, a responsible role and a record. For receiving stock, identify who counts items, where discrepancies are recorded and when approval is required. For returns, distinguish ordinary policy decisions from questions requiring current consumer-law guidance. Do not invent blanket statements about legal rights.


Inventory records help prevent both stockouts and excess stock. Reconcile opening stock, receipts, sales and closing stock. Investigate a difference before adjusting the record. Protect customer information, keep walkways clear and make handovers understandable. A useful control reduces error without making ordinary work unnecessarily difficult.

  3. The receiving record preserves the original order, counted quantity, condition and requested remedy. It does not quietly change the order to match the delivery.
