Begin with a pattern you can explain

In the last two lessons, we connected events in the with events in the uterus. Now we will change one part of that system and ask what should follow. Keep three questions separate: What was observed? What mechanism could explain it? What else would we need to know?

The cycle diagram below is our baseline, a simplified cycle without pregnancy. Read the ovarian row from a developing follicle through to the , the hormone-producing tissue left from the ovulated follicle. In the uterine row, follow the , the inner lining of the uterus. Growing follicles supply ; after ovulation the corpus luteum supplies and estrogen. These blood-borne signals help the lining grow and remain ready to support an early embryo.

The last column shows an active corpus luteum supporting the lining. Read the footer for the later event that returns this model to flow. Without pregnancy support, the corpus luteum regresses, meaning it shrinks and loses activity. Ovarian hormone output falls, and the functional part of the lining is shed. The first column shows menstruation overlapping new follicular activity; its downward arrow does not mean that a new follicle causes the shedding. The important cause is the preceding fall in hormone support. The picture is a sequence model, not a personal calendar or a picture of every altered cycle.

For example, a low progesterone result means only that the measured sample contained little progesterone. A single sample might have been collected before the usual post-ovulation rise. Even a missing recorded LH peak is not automatically proof that no peak occurred: the brief surge might fall between measurements. With a sufficiently complete pattern, absent surge and absent later progesterone rise can support a hypothesis of disrupted ovulation. They still do not identify one definite cause.

A weaker ovarian response can mean stronger pituitary stimulation

An ovarian follicle contains an immature oocyte and supporting cells. As the supply of functioning follicles declines with ovarian ageing, ovulation becomes less regular. Hormone levels can fluctuate during the transition; they do not simply fall by the same amount every month. names the end of menstrual cycling associated with the loss of normal ovarian follicular activity. After menopause, ovarian estrogen and progesterone output is much lower than in typical reproductive cycles.

Trace the feedback rather than assuming every hormone must move together. Normally, ovarian hormones contribute inhibitory signals to the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary. Negative feedback means that a response opposes the change that produced it. If ovarian output falls, some of that inhibition is removed. A functioning anterior pituitary can then release more and LH, the gonadotropins that stimulate the gonads. Reduced ovarian inhibin, another inhibitory signal especially important for FSH, also contributes.

So low ovarian output and high pituitary output can occur together. More stimulation does not create a fresh supply of responsive follicles. Picture a signal reaching a target that cannot respond as strongly: increasing the message does not guarantee restoration of the response.

The teacher notes also describe age-related changes, sometimes called andropause. Do not turn that comparison into an identical male menopause. Testosterone changes are more gradual and variable, and there is no universal sudden end to sperm production. Fatigue or a changed menstrual pattern alone cannot establish a hormone diagnosis. The older textbook treatment discussions are historical reading, not personal treatment guidance.

Early pregnancy keeps the corpus luteum working

Change the baseline at the corpus luteum. After fertilization and early development, the embryo reaches the uterus. Its outer trophoblast tissue participates in implantation, attachment and embedding in the endometrium, and contributes to the placenta. During early pregnancy, trophoblast tissue releases , abbreviated hCG.

hCG travels in the blood and supports the corpus luteum. The corpus luteum therefore continues releasing progesterone and estrogen instead of following its usual unsupported decline. Progesterone helps maintain the endometrium, so the ordinary menstrual shedding does not occur. The developing placenta later takes on a larger role in supplying these hormones; the detailed transition belongs to Chapter 15.

Follow the complete chain: trophoblast releases hCG; hCG supports the corpus luteum; the corpus luteum supplies progesterone and estrogen; those hormones support the uterine lining. hCG and progesterone are different signals with different sources and immediate targets. A pregnancy test can detect hCG, but neither a missed period nor a hormone sketch is a personal diagnosis.

External hormones change feedback rather than blocking a duct

Hormonal contraception uses hormones or hormone-like substances to alter reproductive processes. Combined methods supply an estrogen and a progestin, a substance with progesterone-like activity. These signals act through feedback on the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary, reducing the normal FSH/LH stimulation of the ovaries. In particular, suppression of the helps prevent ovulation. Reduced follicular development is another part of the mechanism.

Read this as supplied signal, changed pituitary output, then changed ovarian activity. It does not mean that all FSH and LH disappear or that every oocyte is destroyed. The teacher slide’s wording about “high doses” and being “without FSH and LH” is too absolute for this mechanism. Effects on cervical mucus also make sperm passage more difficult. Hormonal methods differ; this combined-method example does not describe every method in the same way.

Notice what has not been changed: altering hormone feedback does not create a physical barrier against infectious organisms. A combined contraceptive pill does not protect against sexually transmitted infections, or STIs. Preventing conception and reducing infection transmission are different biological functions. This is a comparison of mechanisms, not advice about choosing, dosing or changing a method.

The same feedback reasoning helps with the optional testosterone questions. An external testosterone-like signal can inhibit hypothalamic and pituitary stimulation, reducing LH support for the testes’ own testosterone production and disrupting support for sperm production. Effects at body tissues and effects on the control system can therefore differ. Human growth hormone, hGH, is a protein hormone, not an anabolic steroid; the example on teacher slide19 misclassifies it.

Worked example

Compare a source change with a target change

Two constructed models have functioning hypothalamic and pituitary feedback. In Model A, ovarian hormone output falls because follicles respond poorly. In Model B, ovarian tissue is responsive, but an external estrogen/progestin signal produces inhibitory feedback. Predict the direction of pituitary stimulation and explain why both models can disrupt ovulation. Assume no other changes.

  1. Locate the change in Model A: the ovary produces less inhibitory hormone signal. It is not a failure of the pituitary to send a message.
  2. Trace A upstream: weaker inhibition permits FSH/LH stimulation to rise. Trace back to the target: poor follicular responsiveness means more stimulation need not restore maturation or ovulation.
  3. Locate the change in Model B: an external inhibitory signal is supplied. Trace upstream output: normal FSH/LH stimulation and the LH surge can be suppressed. Responsive ovaries still need the appropriate stimulation to complete the normal sequence.
  4. Compare the results: disrupted ovulation can accompany increased stimulation in A or reduced stimulation in B. The mechanism, not just the final outcome, determines the hormone prediction.
  5. Check the limit: these deliberately specified models demonstrate feedback. A single measurement from a person would not establish which model, if either, applies.

Optional application

Optional model challenge: hCG is present in early pregnancy, but in a constructed model the corpus luteum cannot respond to it. No other tissue supplies enough progesterone or estrogen to replace that lost output. Would the presence of hCG alone guarantee maintenance of the endometrium? Trace the missing link.

Compare after your attempt

No. hCG is a supporting signal, not the progesterone itself. If the corpus luteum cannot respond, its hormone output is not maintained by that signal. Without sufficient replacement hormone support, the model does not guarantee maintenance of the endometrium. This identifies a target-response failure rather than a shortage of hCG; it does not predict an individual clinical outcome.
