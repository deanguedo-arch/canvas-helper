Locate production before tracing the journey

A sperm cell must be produced, mature and travel out of the body. Those jobs happen in connected structures. We will follow the connections rather than make a route out of every nearby label in an anatomy picture.

The two testes lie in the scrotum, a pouch outside the main body cavity. Sperm production works best a little below core body temperature. Moving the testes closer to the body in cold conditions helps retain heat; holding them farther away in warmer conditions helps lose heat. The scrotum helps provide suitable conditions for the organs inside it.

Within each testis, tightly coiled seminiferous tubules are the sperm-production sites. Sperm pass from them through small connecting ducts into the epididymis, a long coiled duct alongside the testis. In the epididymis, sperm mature, acquire the capacity for movement and can be stored. Production and maturation are connected stages at different locations.

Look at the lower part of the side-view diagram. The testis is the oval organ inside the scrotum. Follow the epididymis label to the curved structure along the testis, then find the ductus deferens leaving this region. The black lines connect names to structures; they are label lines, not arrows showing sperm movement. The picture shows a cut-away side view and does not display every small internal duct or the coiled seminiferous tubules.

Follow the connected ducts

During ejaculation, coordinated muscular contractions move sperm from the epididymis into the vas deferens. Ductus deferens is another name for this same duct. It carries sperm upward from the testis region and into the pelvis.

At the next junction, the vas deferens joins the duct of a seminal vesicle. The resulting ejaculatory duct carries the mixture through the prostate into the urethra. The urethra then runs through the penis to the outside. The penis is the organ containing that final passage, rather than an extra sperm-producing site. During intercourse, it can deposit semen in the female reproductive tract.

Return to the figure. Trace the labelled ductus deferens upward and around the bladder region, then locate the short ejaculatory duct near the seminal vesicle and prostate. Find the urethra continuing through the penis. The path passes near the urinary bladder; it does not go through the bladder's storage space.

The male urethra is shared by the urinary and reproductive systems, but urine and semen are normally released at different times. The ureter is a different tube: it brings urine to the bladder. Similar names and neighbouring positions do not make the ureter part of the sperm pathway. For the main reproductive route, keep the sequence seminiferous tubules, epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct and urethra.

Why the travelling mixture is called semen

Sperm are cells. Semen is the mixture containing sperm and fluid from accessory glands. Accessory means that the glands contribute to the reproductive system without being the gonads that produce the sperm. A gland releases a secretion; a transport duct carries material along a passage.

The seminal vesicles add fructose-containing fluid. Fructose is a sugar that sperm can use as an energy source. The prostate contributes fluid to semen through ducts entering the urethral region. The bulbourethral glands, also called Cowper's glands, release lubricating mucus into the urethra. The combined secretions support sperm activity and help provide a suitable fluid environment, including buffering acidity. A buffer helps resist changes in acidity.

Use the diagram to separate the gland bodies from the route. The seminal vesicle lies behind the bladder, the prostate lies below it around the beginning of the urethra, and Cowper's gland is shown lower down. Secretions reach the route through gland ducts. A sperm does not have to travel through the interior of every gland to receive the fluid it adds.

A fluid sample and a cell sample tell us different things. Seeing fluid leave a gland would establish secretion, not sperm production. Finding sperm in the travelling mixture would identify cells that originated in seminiferous tubules. The name semen describes the combined material and does not make every component come from one structure.

Keep the cell route and hormone route separate

The testes also contain interstitial cells, also called Leydig cells, between the seminiferous tubules. These cells release testosterone into the blood. This is an endocrine route: a hormone enters the circulation and reaches responsive tissues. Sperm instead travel through the reproductive duct system. The same organ contributes to both routes, but the routes are physically different.

A useful explanation follows the affected function. A change to a production site first concerns cell production. A change to an epididymis can affect maturation or storage. A change to a transport duct concerns passage along that route. A change in a gland's secretion concerns a fluid contribution. After identifying the first effect, trace which downstream parts depend on it and which still have their own functioning sources.

For an illustration with two testes and paired ducts, check whether a question describes one side or both before claiming that all sperm transport is interrupted. These classroom cases isolate a mechanism; they do not give enough information to diagnose a person's fertility.

We can now connect the journey to the starting question. Seminiferous tubules supply sperm, the epididymis supports maturation, ducts transport them, and accessory glands add fluid. Next, a closer view inside a testis will let us distinguish the cells that become sperm from the cells that support them.

Worked example: A changed gland secretion with an open sperm route

In a simplified model, sperm production and maturation continue normally and all transport ducts remain open. Only the seminal vesicles' fructose contribution is reduced. What changes directly, and what cannot be concluded from that change alone?

Locate the changed source. The seminal vesicles are accessory glands. They contribute fructose-containing fluid rather than producing the sperm cells.

Follow that contribution into the mixture. Less fructose from these glands reduces an energy-source contribution available to sperm in the seminal fluid. It may affect support for sperm activity.

Check the unchanged conditions. The problem explicitly keeps sperm production, maturation and duct transport functioning. It therefore does not establish that no sperm can enter the mixture or that the urethra is blocked.

Separate the other route as well. Testosterone enters blood from interstitial cells. A changed fructose contribution does not itself show that testosterone release stopped.

Conclude at the level the evidence supports: the gland contribution has changed. The model supplies no measurements that would let us calculate sperm speed, an exact sperm count or a probability of fertilization.

Optional application: In a second model, both testes still produce sperm but passage through both epididymides is completely interrupted. Accessory glands and their downstream ducts remain open. Explain which material loses its route and whether gland fluid must also disappear. Then identify one difference from the worked gland-secretion example.

Comparison: Newly produced sperm cannot pass through the interrupted epididymides to reach the downstream sperm ducts. Accessory-gland fluid still has its own downstream entry points, so it need not disappear. This prediction concerns new transport after the interruption; it does not determine whether sperm were already stored farther along before it occurred. Unlike the worked example, this model changes a cell-transport route rather than only the composition of a gland secretion.
