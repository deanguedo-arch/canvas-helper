### Required written explanation 1 — gonad, gamete, hormone

**First answer:** A gonad is an organ that produces reproductive cells and releases reproductive hormones. A gamete is one of those reproductive cells, with one chromosome set. A hormone is a chemical signal that travels in the blood to cells able to respond. A testis is a gonad: sperm develop in its small tubes, while other cells in the testis release testosterone into the blood. Sperm can travel through reproductive ducts and contribute chromosomes at fertilization. Testosterone travels by the blood route and signals responsive tissues. The organ, cell and signal are different things.

**Visible support for each consequential step:**
1. Organ/cell/signal distinction: the definitions in `ch14-l01-teaching-01` explicitly define all three.
2. One chromosome set and contribution at fertilization: that section's final paragraph states that each gamete normally has one set of 23 chromosomes, and each contributes a set at fertilization.
3. Testis produces sperm in tiny tubes and other cells release testosterone: that section's testis paragraph directly states both.
4. Different routes: the same paragraph explicitly contrasts sperm through reproductive ducts with testosterone in blood to responsive tissues.
5. Target response depends on receptors: the prerequisite and `ch14-l01-teaching-03` support the “able to respond” qualification.

**Missing support:** None for the testis example chosen. No more detailed cell names or meiosis mechanism are required or supplied in this answer.

### Required written explanation 2 — puberty does not determine category

**First answer:** Changing at puberty does not make a feature secondary, because the categories depend on what the feature is. An ovary can become more active during puberty, but it is still a reproductive organ and therefore a primary characteristic. Breast development is a secondary characteristic. Both can change at puberty, so the timing alone cannot tell them apart.

**Visible support:** `ch14-l01-teaching-02` directly supplies this two-example comparison: “The ovary remains a reproductive organ, so it remains a primary characteristic” and “Breast development belongs to the secondary category used here.” It explicitly says that “it changed at puberty” cannot settle classification. `ch14-l01-teaching-04` reinforces that primary structures can become more active without changing category.

**Missing support:** None. The question's two-example requirement is met with one primary and one secondary example, using the stated classroom categories.

### New optional application — uterus, egg, progesterone

**First answer:** The uterus is a reproductive organ, the egg is a gamete, and progesterone is a reproductive hormone. Not every reproductive organ or structure is a gonad. A gonad has the specific role of producing gametes and releasing reproductive hormones; testes and ovaries are the gonads named here. The lesson also includes reproductive ducts among primary structures even though they do not make gametes. Being part of reproduction is therefore broader than being a gonad.

**Visible support for each consequential step:**
1. Uterus is a reproductive organ/structure: `ch14-l01-teaching-02`, first paragraph, lists uterus immediately after defining that category.
2. Egg is a gamete and progesterone is a hormone: `ch14-l01-teaching-01`, definitions paragraph, explicitly supplies both.
3. Gonad's two defining functions: `ch14-l01-teaching-01` and the visible vocabulary definition.
4. The category is broader than gonads: `ch14-l01-teaching-02`, final paragraph, says a reproductive duct is primary “even though it does not make gametes” and that these structures include “not just the gonads.” This provides an explicit counterexample to the universal claim.

**Missing support:** None to answer the actual question. The packet does not teach a specific function for the uterus, so my first attempt does not invent one or use an untaught uterine function as the explanation.

## Lesson 2: Male reproductive structures and the sperm pathway

### Required selection 1 — route after epididymis

**First answer:** Vas deferens → ejaculatory duct → urethra.

**Visible support:** `ch14-l02-teaching-02`, “Follow the connected ducts,” first states that contractions move sperm from the epididymis into the vas deferens. The next paragraph says the vas joins the seminal-vesicle duct to form the ejaculatory duct, which carries the mixture into the urethra. The final paragraph gives the full sequence explicitly. It also excludes the bladder's storage space and the ureter from the sperm pathway.

**Missing support:** None.

### Required selection 2 — sperm versus semen

**First answer:** Semen contains sperm mixed with accessory-gland secretions.

**Visible support:** The prerequisite and `ch14-l02-teaching-03`, “Why the travelling mixture is called semen,” explicitly distinguish sperm cells from the mixture of sperm and gland fluid. `ch14-l02-teaching-04` says interstitial cells release testosterone into blood, excluding the alternative that they alone produce semen.

**Missing support:** None.

### Required written explanation 1 — production to outside, with maturation

**First answer:** Sperm are produced in the seminiferous tubules inside a testis. They pass through small connecting ducts to the epididymis, where they mature, gain the capacity for movement and can be stored. During ejaculation, sperm move into the vas deferens, also called the ductus deferens. This joins the duct of a seminal vesicle to form the ejaculatory duct. The mixture then goes through the ejaculatory duct into the urethra and along the urethra through the penis to the outside. The path runs near the bladder but does not pass through the bladder's storage space.

**Visible support for each consequential step:**
1. Production, connecting ducts, epididymis maturation/movement/storage: `ch14-l02-teaching-01`, third paragraph.
2. Epididymis → vas and vas/ductus synonym: `ch14-l02-teaching-02`, first paragraph.
3. Vas joins a seminal-vesicle duct; resulting ejaculatory duct enters urethra: the second paragraph of that section.
4. Urethra runs through penis to outside: same paragraph.
5. Bladder exclusion: third and fourth paragraphs of that section.
6. Image correspondence: the actual figure labels testis, epididymis, ductus deferens, ejaculatory duct, urethra, penis and urinary bladder. The teaching warns that it omits small internal ducts and seminiferous tubules; thus the production-site detail comes from the prose, not an imagined figure detail.

**Missing support:** None at the level requested. Exact names of the “small connecting ducts” are not taught or required.

### Required written explanation 2 — blocked vas deferens

**First answer:** A blockage in a vas deferens interrupts the sperm route from the epididymis on that side, so sperm from that testis cannot pass the blockage to reach the downstream mixture. Accessory glands add their own fluid through ducts joining the downstream route. A blockage of the sperm duct alone therefore does not mean those glands stop adding fluid. If both sperm routes are blocked, sperm from either testis cannot get through by those routes, while gland fluid can still contribute to the fluid released. If only one vas deferens is blocked, this information alone does not show that all sperm will be absent, because the lesson describes two testes and paired ducts.

**Visible support for each consequential step:**
1. Vas carries sperm from epididymis toward ejaculatory duct: visible vocabulary and `ch14-l02-teaching-02`.
2. Blockage affects passage, rather than automatically stopping production or secretion: `ch14-l02-teaching-04`, “A change to a transport duct concerns passage along that route,” followed by the instruction to trace dependencies and independently functioning sources.
3. Accessory fluid has distinct sources and joining ducts: `ch14-l02-teaching-03`, first three paragraphs; the seminal-vesicle junction is specified in `ch14-l02-teaching-02`.
4. One side versus both: `ch14-l02-teaching-04` explicitly says to check this “before claiming that all sperm transport is interrupted.”

**Missing support / ambiguity:** The prompt says “a blocked vas deferens” but asks about preventing “sperm from appearing in semen.” It does not say both vasa are blocked or limit the sperm claim to the affected testis. A first-time learner following the lesson's own caution should not infer total sperm absence from one blocked duct. The independent-fluid mechanism is well taught. Recommend either “Explain why blocking both vasa deferentia could prevent sperm from reaching semen while accessory glands continue releasing fluid” or, for a unilateral case, “Explain why sperm from the affected testis may not reach semen even while gland fluid is still added.” For an explicitly sperm-free immediate sample, the case would also need timing/downstream-residual conditions; my answer only claims interruption of sperm passage through the stated routes.

**Missing support:** None.

**First answer:** Providing an energy source for sperm.

**Visible support:** `ch14-l02-teaching-03`, second paragraph, explicitly says “Fructose is a sugar that sperm can use as an energy source.” The worked example follows this contribution into the seminal fluid and distinguishes it from sperm production and testosterone release.

**Missing support:** None. No metabolic mechanism or detailed meiosis knowledge is required to select the explicitly taught function.

### Stop and think — urinary bladder

**First answer:** No. The sperm route goes near the bladder but does not enter its storage space. Sperm go through the vas deferens and ejaculatory duct to the urethra, which runs through the penis. The ureter is a separate tube that brings urine to the bladder.

**Visible support:** `ch14-l02-teaching-02`, paragraphs 2–4, explicitly supplies every statement. The actual image helps locate the bladder and neighboring duct, but the label lines are not flow arrows; the prose provides the route direction and exclusions.

**Missing support:** None.

### New optional application — both epididymides interrupted

**First answer:** The sperm produced in both testes lose their route through the epididymides to the vas deferens and later ducts. Continued sperm production alone does not get those cells past the interruption. Gland fluid does not have to disappear: the accessory glands have their own fluid contributions, and the question says their downstream ducts remain open. In the worked example, the sperm route stayed open while the seminal-vesicle fructose contribution was reduced. In this new model, the sperm route is interrupted on both sides, while the problem preserves the glands' downstream route.

**Visible support for each consequential step:**
1. Sperm must pass from the production site through the epididymis before the vas: `ch14-l02-teaching-01`, third paragraph; `ch14-l02-teaching-02`, first and final paragraphs.
2. Both sides affected: the optional application's own “both testes” and “both epididymides” statements, read with `ch14-l02-teaching-04`'s paired-route caution.
3. Continuing production does not establish successful passage: `ch14-l02-teaching-04` distinguishes production-site effects from passage effects, and the prompt explicitly sets continued production plus interrupted passage.
4. Independent gland fluid and downstream connections: `ch14-l02-teaching-03`, paragraphs 1–3, plus the prompt's “Accessory glands and their downstream ducts remain open.”
5. Contrast with the worked example: its setup explicitly holds production, maturation and transport normal and reduces only the seminal-vesicle fructose contribution.

**Missing support / boundary:** None for the requested mechanism and comparison. The prompt does not establish a precise fluid volume, fertility result, or that every sperm already downstream is absent immediately. Those claims are not necessary and are not made.
