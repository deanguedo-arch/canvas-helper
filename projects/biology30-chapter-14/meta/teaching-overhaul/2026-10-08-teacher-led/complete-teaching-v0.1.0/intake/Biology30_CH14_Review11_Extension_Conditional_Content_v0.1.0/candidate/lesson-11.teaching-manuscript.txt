Chapter14 final review

Find the changed function before predicting the outcome

At this point, a list of structures is only the beginning of an explanation. Start a new case with the normal pathway. Then locate the changed step: producing a cell, supporting its development, moving it, adding fluid, releasing a hormone, or responding to that hormone. Two cases can have the same final outcome for different reasons.

Read the first comparison table one row at a time. The row tells you which function to compare; the two columns give examples from the male and female systems. Do not read the whole table as one continuous route. In both systems a gamete is a reproductive cell, not a hormone or a gland. The gonads, testes and ovaries, contain cells involved in gamete development and cells that release reproductive hormones.

Trace the sperm route before locating a blockage. Developing sperm form in seminiferous tubules of the testis with support from Sertoli cells. They then reach the epididymis, where they mature and are stored. From there the vas deferens carries them toward the ejaculatory duct and then the urethra. A block after the epididymis is a transport problem; it does not by itself prove that production in the testis has stopped.

Now separate sperm from semen. Semen contains sperm mixed with accessory-gland secretions. The seminal vesicles supply fluid containing fructose, a sugar that contributes energy for sperm activity. A reduced contribution of this fluid can therefore affect the conditions supporting sperm even when sperm are present and the transport ducts are open. It is not automatically evidence of failed chromosome reduction or absent testosterone.

In the female pathway, the ovary releases a secondary oocyte at ovulation. The fimbriae help guide it into an oviduct, where fertilization usually occurs. Early development then moves toward the uterus; the blastocyst, a later early-embryo stage, can implant in its endometrium. The endometrium is the uterine lining. Ovulation, fertilization, implantation and menstruation are different events, at different sites or times.

Use those locations in an infection case. Scarring can narrow or block an oviduct and interrupt the route needed for sperm and an oocyte to meet or for early development to move toward the uterus. Ovarian hormone production can continue despite that damage. A lack of visible symptoms cannot establish that no infection is present. Explain the stated mechanism without making a personal diagnosis.

Connect each hormone to its source and responding cells

Both reproductive control pathways begin with the hypothalamus releasing gonadotropin-releasing hormone, GnRH. GnRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release follicle-stimulating hormone, FSH, and luteinizing hormone, LH. These hormones travel in the blood to responsive cells. A hormone name alone is not the explanation: say which cells receive it and what they do.

In the testis, FSH acts on Sertoli cells, which support developing sperm within the seminiferous tubules. LH acts on interstitial cells, also called Leydig cells, stimulating testosterone production. Testosterone supports reproductive functions, including sperm development in the testis, and contributes inhibitory feedback to the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary. Sertoli-cell inhibin particularly restrains FSH release. Keep the two target-cell roles distinct: FSH does not make interstitial cells produce sperm.

Negative feedback opposes a change in a controlled system. If an outside testosterone signal increases inhibitory feedback, a functioning control system can reduce GnRH and pituitary stimulation. The testes may then produce less of their own testosterone, and support for sperm production can be disrupted. The external signal and the testes’ own production are not the same measurement.

In the ovary, FSH supports follicular development, with LH also contributing to ovarian hormone production. The growing follicle releases estrogen. Sustained high estrogen before ovulation temporarily promotes positive feedback: pituitary LH release rises sharply. This LH surge helps trigger release of the secondary oocyte and formation of the corpus luteum from the follicular tissue that remains in the ovary.

The corpus luteum releases progesterone and estrogen. Progesterone helps maintain the endometrium during the luteal phase. This explains why a typical hormone graph has a broad progesterone rise after the brief LH surge: a new ovarian hormone-producing structure has become active. LH is not converted into progesterone in the blood. Without pregnancy support, the corpus luteum regresses, its hormone output falls and the functional lining is shed. Early-pregnancy hCG can maintain corpus-luteum support, as explained in lesson 09.

A target cell also needs a functioning receptor, a molecule that recognizes the hormone and enables its usual effect. A normal hormone concentration establishes availability, not a guaranteed response. If Leydig cells cannot respond to LH, more LH need not restore testosterone output. If endometrial cells cannot respond to progesterone, a normal progesterone concentration need not produce the normal lining response. Distinguish a missing signal from a target that cannot use a present signal.

The second table applies this method to changed conditions. For each row, identify the normal step, locate the change and trace only the consequences supported by the case. Do not assume a duct blockage must stop all ovarian hormones, or that a later corpus-luteum failure proves earlier ovulation never happened. Likewise, a stated day in a model is evidence about that model, not a universal personal calendar.

Worked example: Normal input but reduced pituitary response

Two otherwise comparable constructed anterior-pituitary tissue models receive the same pattern of brief GnRH pulses. Model A has functioning GnRH receptors and releases FSH and LH in response. Model B has non-functioning GnRH receptors and shows much less FSH/LH release. Does this show that a hypothalamus supplied too little GnRH? What would the reduced output mean for otherwise responsive gonadal target cells?

Identify what is held equal: the GnRH signal supplied to the two tissues. Less GnRH was not supplied to B in this comparison. The experiment does not measure a real hypothalamus.

Locate the changed step: the receptor response in the anterior-pituitary tissue. A signal can be available while the receiving cells cannot respond normally. The stated observations place the immediate difference at this target-response step.

Trace the lower FSH/LH output onward. If those outputs reached otherwise responsive gonads, they would provide less normal stimulation of Sertoli cells and Leydig cells in the testis, or of follicular and other hormone-producing cells in the ovary.

Separate signalling from transport: this model describes hormone reception and release. It gives no evidence of a physically blocked vas deferens or oviduct. A different mechanism would be needed to explain a duct obstruction.

Check the conclusion: the tissue comparison supports a pituitary target-response explanation, not deficient GnRH production by a hypothalamus. It does not by itself predict every downstream hormone value or provide a personal diagnosis.
