What is critical thinking?

Most of the assignments in this course will ask you to engage in critical thinking. What is critical thinking? Students often say critical thinking is about criticizing (usually another's point of view). That is NOT critical thinking! Rather, a critical thinker uses the skills of logic and reason. A critical thinker weighs all the evidence and options and uses criteria to make reasoned judgements. Supporting evidence skills are explained in the Student Tools section; you'll be required to do this in all your written work so you should familiarize yourself with those skills too. This page will focus on the use of criteria to make reasoned judgements.

Criteria

Developing criteria on which to judge or assess something is the key to critical thinking. Furthermore, critical thinking is like any skill, you need to practice to get good at it. If you practice enough, it becomes a habit of mind - you do it automtically without even thinking about it.

For example, look at your purchasing decisions. Which is the better skateboard? Which is the better mobile device? If you don't use criteria, then your level of satisfaction with your purchase will depend on luck rather than a reasoned decision. Everyone may not agree on which criteria is most important. For one person the purchase price might be the most important. Another person might have weight or size as the most important criteria. A third person may consider a combination of criteria to be of equal importance.

Another example of critical thinking is when your teachers mark your assignments. The rubrics contain the criteria they use to make reasoned judgements about whether you met the objectives of the assignment. Similarly, the Website Evaluation section of this course went through six criteria to apply when you critically analyze websites to see if the information there is a good source.

Critical thinking involves considering background information and judging information based on criteria or guidelines. Background information and guidelines may be presented by your teacher to help you come to a conclusion, or you may be asked to find your own information and develop your own criteria, as in your next assignment!

Example of the use of criteria

What is the better fruit, an apple or an orange?

First decide what your criteria are for making this decision – what is important and why?  Amount of vitamin C and juiciness are my criteria.  I don’t eat enough fruit and veg so I need that extra vitamin C.  And my teeth are falling out (they aren’t but let’s pretend they are) so it’s also easier to make juice and to eat the raw fruit. So an orange is better because it has more vitamin C, and easier to juice and chew. 

Your criteria could be ease of consumption and portability.  In that case, an apple wins out because it is easier and less messy to eat and it is not as fragile.  You can throw it in the bottom of your backpack and not end up with a sticky mess by snack time.

How would bananas rank according to my or your criteria? Top, bottom or between apples and oranges?

You can use criteria to rank importance or relevance.

Don't forget to also support your opinions/decisions/arguments. See the Supporting Evidence page in the Student Tools section of content.

  • They consider a position or solution's accuracy, plausibility, fairness to all, and feasibility. (Will the plan work?)

Critical thinkers use thinking strategies that include

  • decision-making models and procedures
  • organization of information in logical ways
  • consideration of the perspectives of others

Critical thinkers have attitudes that include

  • being open-minded
  • being fair
  • being independent
  • questioning information and ideas

Big Idea: The relationship between nation, nationalism and identity.

Identity is shaped by many factors and expressed in different ways. We each have a unique identity as well as an identity within the groups and communities to which we belong. Your values and beliefs and behaviour are a result of who you are, where you belong and how others see you. You might have a personal identity as a son or daughter, a student, a brother, a cousin, a Tim Horton's cashier, a Wal-Mart employee, a hockey player, a bass player, and a good neighbour.

Social identity is the aspect of identity that pertains to membership in groups. You not only construct your identity but it is also influenced by families, friends, school, society and other social influences. You may have an identity as a member of your church, mosque, or synagogue. Many people have cultural, ethnic and national identities. You identify with groups because of similar beliefs, values or interests.

We have multiple identities because we have multiple personal experiences and belong to various groups. How many groups do you belong to? Are you a member of a family, of a school, perhaps a sports team or music club, religious group, your community, the province and/or nation?

Factors that shape identity

We are not born with an identity but rather our experiences shape our identities. This means that your identity changes over time. Your teacher may consider her/his career as a teacher to be part of her/his identity (and certainly others - such as yourself - typically see that person as "teacher" more than anything else). But your teacher was not always a teacher and therefore that was not always part of their identity. You too might have recently become interested in a particular music genre or activity that was not part of your identity 2 or 3 years ago. You might keep some aspects of your present identity but other aspects will disappear or change over time.

Is this the same as personality? Not really. Personality describes what you are like (eg. polite, intelligent, friendly). Identity defines who you are, how you define yourself and how others define you. Keep this in mind when you do the assignment below.

As you observed in the Who Am I assignment and discussion, you may or may not identify strongly with your nation. Or you may or may not identify strongly with your country. Now wait a minute, nation... country (or nation state). Aren't they all the same thing? Yes and no. Click on the words for definitions. So what's the difference between nation and country?

You might identify strongly with an ethnic or cultural background that comes from one or more of the many cultures around the world. A culture has shared characteristics. Add that the people of a culture recognize and identify with their common culture, and you now have a nation.

A nation can be either of the following:

  • A nation is a cultural group whose members have a strong sense of identity with their culture. The cultural group itself might not live within internationally recognized borders. This is a nation but NOT a country.
  • A nation is a cultural group whose members have a strong sense of identity with their culture. The cultural group itself may live within internationally recognized borders and have self-government. Therefore, a nation can be both a nation and a country at the same time given these conditions.

A country (also known as nation-state) must have both the following criteria (or conditions) to exist:

  • It is an independent (self-governing) nation. That is, it is a sovereign country; it can make its own decisions freely without outside influence.
  • It exists within clearly defined borders that are internationally recognized. This means that other countries recognize the nation as an independent nation-state (eg. not a colony or region of a larger country).

The citizens of a country or a nation-state also share values and beliefs (such as the type of government, for eg. democracy, the rule of law or freedom of speech; or a dominant religion or language).

Quebec, therefore, is a nation but not a nation-state. It is neither fully sovereign nor internationally recognized as a nation-state. But they do have shared values and a dominant language. Quebec seperatists seek to become an independent nation-state.

What is Nationalism?

Nationalism, like nation, can mean different things to different people. You will explore the meaning of nationalism and how it can be inspired by ethnicity, language, culture, religion, spirituality, poltics, geography and relationship to land.

Required Reading

Please read Chapter 1, pages 25-33 in your Exploring Nationalism textbook.

Nationalism is the feelings people have in identifying with their nation. It can be a shared sense of belonging, a sense of kinship or common identity. These feelings generally include loyalty for and pride in the nation's culture. Nationalism is also a collective feeling of separateness or uniqueness from other groups of people. This can be expressed in the form of "We are ..." or "We are NOT ...." - such as when Canadians distinquish themselves from Americans. You might find that you do this yourself.

Fill in the blank as many times as you can!

  • Canadians are not _______.
  • Canadians are _______.

These traits you mentioned in the blanks are your interpretation of the Canadian collective identity.

Nationalism can also be a political movement, either to increase self-government within a multi-nation state - such as the Inuit nation, mentioned on the previous page, which sought and attained self-government of the Nunavut territory - or to create a fully independent nation-state for a nation - such as members of the Parti Quebecois, which seeks a fully independent nation-state for Quebec.

In general terms, nation is the group; nationalism is the sense of belonging to a nation.

Begin by turning first to sports. Yes, sports! Think about the sports you like and especially the one team you are a big fan of. Maybe you like hockey or football and there's one team that you've always cheered for and stuck with through thick and thin. Even when your team loses, you believe that someday it will win the championship. No matter what sport you like, the feeling is the same. When your team wins, you're happy. When your team loses, you're upset.

You may not identify personally with a sports team in such a way (maybe you identify with another type of group) but you've probably seen others who do. You've seen the way the fans all like to get together to watch or talk about games. You've seen the way those fans identify with their team by wearing its colours and logo on their clothing or sometimes painted on their bodies! Those fans might even sing a team song on occasion. You may even have seen the fans of two opposing teams actually fight each other over a perceived insult!

At the core of all this is the basic human need to belong to a group—to be a part of something that gives people feelings of pride and loyalty in something greater than themselves. Not everyone feels it as strongly but that need persists. You may not identify as strongly with a sports team as others, but you may feel in a similar way for your ethnic or cultural group, or maybe even for your country.

Think back to the course page with Canadian inventions and achievements. If these stirred any feelings of pride in you, this was nationalism. Even if they did not stir any feelings, you have probably seen how such things do create a sense of pride for many people.

Ethnicity

Ethnicity Think about these questions when you examine the aspects of nation: Could the ethnic aspect stand out from the other aspects in the identity of a nation? Why...

  • Could the ethnic aspect stand out from the other aspects in the identity of a nation? Why?

Ethnic groups usually share similar racial and cultural characteristics. Often, a single ethnic group makes up a nation. For example, the German ethnic group makes up the nation of Germany or the Japanese ethnic group makes up the nation of Japan.

Ethnicity and culture are, of course, very closely related. However, not always does an ethnic group have one common culture. For example, Aboriginal peoples of North America have a common ancestry and, therefore, they are part of the same ethnic group. However, North American Aboriginal peoples do not share the same culture. West Coast Salish people do not have the same culture as the Iroquois, and both of these groups do not share a common culture with the Navaho.

Some believe that a single ethnic group should make up the collective identity of a nation. This is ethnic nationalism. However, others criticize this idea because it can create problems for those who are not of the same ethnic group. In extreme cases, it can lead to dangerous consequences. In Nazi Germany during the 1930s and 1940s, for example, many supported the idea that Germany should be a nation of only pure blood Germans. Other ethnic groups, especially German Jews, were to be exterminated so they would not "pollute" the German ethnic group.

Today, with the global movement of people, there are no pure ethnic countries, although there are many where one ethnicity is dominant. In Canada, ethnic and racial equality is guaranteed by Section 15 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. To discriminate against a person because of race or ethnic background is illegal under Canadian law. Indeed, some might say this ideal is part of our Canadian identity!

Hinduism and India's other religions have given the nation of India its unique sense of identity.

Geography and Relationship to the Land Think about these questions when you examine the aspects of nation: Could geography stand out from the other aspects in the iden...

Think about these questions when you examine the aspects of nation:

  • Could geography stand out from the other aspects in the identity of a nation? Why?

A nation's geography can affect its development economically and culturally, but also geography can affect a nation's relationship to the land as well.

Economically

Physical features such as mountains, oceans, and deserts can be important resources. For example, oceans can be a source of food and transportation while mountains and deserts can provide mineral resources. Development of such resources can help a nation provide for its people. Consider this website to see the role played by oil in Saudi Arabia: The Significance of Saudi Oil.

Resources from Canada's huge and varying geography have an important part in the development of the Canadian nation. Go to this Atlas of Canada website to explore some Canadian communities that rely economically on resources such as agriculture, energy, fishing, forestry, and mining.

Culturally

Physical features can also be obstacles. Mountains, oceans, and deserts can isolate people from one another. This isolation may cause groups to develop their own cultural characteristics that are different from other groups. Japan and Britain are good examples of island nations isolated by the ocean. Tibet is an example of a nation isolated on a high mountain plataeu.

Water is not always an obstacle. Canada was possible as a unified nation because of the vast network of natural waterways. From the St. Lawrence, the Ottawa and French Rivers, to the Great Lakes and beyond to Lake Winnipeg and the Red, Peace and Mackenzie Rivers, the network of waterways was used for thousands of years and they allowed for the European exploration and consolidation of Canada into a single nation.

A Nation's Relationship to the Land

Sometimes, a nation may have a relationship to the land that their ancestors have lived on for hundreds, even thousands, of years. This connection to the land is more than economic. A nation may have a spiritual relationship to the land, a relationship that has become rooted in that nation's identity.

David Aglukark, a co-chief negotiator for Inuit on the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement, stated:

"The relationship between the Inuit and the land was one, like a newborn baby to her mother.... Today, the connection between Inuit and the land has weakened, and Inuit struggle with their identity—the Inuit's latest challenge in a land that has always been challenging."

A collective is a group of people. For example, you belong to collectives such as your family, your school, and your community. You may belong to other collectives such as your circle of friends, a religious group, a language group, or an ethnic group. Whatever collectives you may belong to help form your identity.

Not all collectives form the identity of a nation, but to have a collective identity is important for a nation's citizens.   • cultural background
  • ethnic background
  • geographic identity (based on what region of Canada a person is from)
  • language

There seem to be other identities based perhaps on what Canadians think makes them special, such as

  • Canadians are a polite people.
  • Canadians are a peaceful people.
  • Canadians are a hockey nation.

Many others could be added to these lists, which confirms just how difficult it is to determine the Canadian collective identity!

Civic

Civic Think about these questions when you examine the aspects of nation: Could the civic aspect stand out from the other aspects in the identity of a nation? Why? Wou...

Think about these questions when you examine the aspects of nation:

  • Could the civic aspect stand out from the other aspects in the identity of a nation? Why?
  • Would the civic aspect and/or other aspects stand out for you regarding the identity of your nation? Why?

The key element of a nation is the people who make up that nation, of course. Although we refer to a nation as a nation state or country, the people are the citizens of that nation. Along with being a citizen of a nation, especially in free societies such as Canada, comes civic responsibility. For a nation's citizens to exist peacefully together, a large part of that civic responsibility means that the citizens must agree to

  • accept that all the nation's citizens are equal
  • share a common set of values and beliefs
  • abide by the laws of the nation based on those shared values and beliefs

The civic responsibility that citizens have toward their nation refers to the civic nation and their loyalty to the civic ideals of their nation over all other aspects is civic nationalism. Although citizens have rights and freedoms, citizens also have responsibility to fulfill active roles voluntarily in the life of their nation.

That does not mean that a citizen should follow blindly whatever the nation does. When something occurs in the nation that may be wrong, a citizen's is responsible to criticize and perhaps act to make it right. Free societies usually have peaceful ways to allow their citizens to demonstrate actively their disapproval, such as peaceful rallies and demonstrations or letter writing and petitions to the media and government officials.

Canadians have no common religion, culture, language, or ethnic group, but they seem to have agreement of a civic nature. Canada tends toward being a civic nation*. In other words, Canadian citizens seem to have some common agreement on equality and on common values and beliefs such as individual freedoms and democracy. In Canada, citizen equality and legal rights (based on common values and beliefs) are protected in the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

*Even as a civic nation, Canada does have some issues with common values and beliefs as expressed by groups such as the Quebecois and some First Nations. More on these issues is explored later in this unit as well as later in this course.

Political Think about these questions when you examine the aspects of nation: Could the political aspect stand out from the other aspects in the identity of a nation?...

Think about these questions when you examine the aspects of nation:

  • Could the political aspect stand out from the other aspects in the identity of a nation? Why?

The political aspect of nation includes both government and the political borders of the country.

Government

The political aspect of a nation involves the concept of being a sovereign nation. This means that the nation has the political authority to control its own affairs. In some nations, democracy allows citizens the right to have a say in their government and the affairs of their nation. In other nations, various forms of government restrict their citizen's right to have a say in their government and the affairs of their nation.

Political Borders

The borders of a nation recognized internationally by other countries make it a political entity.

Canada is a sovereign nation because it has the political authority to control its own affairs. The Canadian Parliament is recognized as the government of a sovereign Canada. Canadian citizens have the right to elect democratically their representatives to the Canadian government. Democratic rights are protected under Section 3 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. Canada has had self-government since 1867 when it ceased being a British colony. However, some aspects of British colonialism stuck with Canada for a long time:

  • Britain controlled Canada's foreign affairs until 1931.
  • Britain had some control over the Canadian Constitution until 1982.

The traditional role of the British monarch (a British king or queen) still exists in Canada. The British monarch is Canada's Head of State, which means that the Canadian Parliament in Ottawa makes laws for Canada on the monarch's behalf. The British monarch's role as Head of State is, in reality, only symbolic. The Canadian Prime Minister with his Cabinet and the members of the House of Commons and Senate actually run Canada's federal government. The Governor General of Canada is the British monarch's representative in Canada and must sign all federal government bills into law on the British monarch's behalf. Because the Governor General always signs laws that have been passed through the House of Commons and the Senate, Canada is said to have responsible government; that is, the monarch operates within the constitution of the country.

Canada's borders started out small with only smaller versions of the provinces of Ontario and Quebec, and the present Nova Scotia and New Brunswick. Canada's present borders were determined 1949 with the addition of the province of Newfoundland.

We live today in a system of nation states, and as you learned in previous lessons, many people identify themselves with their nation. But the world was not always organized in this way! Indeed, the system of nation states and the ideology of nationalism are just over 200 years old - about 3 lifetimes!

If not nations, then what?

In the ancient world, people were organized into city states; not nation-states. The city controlled and provided security for an agricultural region, like the first known city in ancient Sumeria (Uruk, in present day Iraq) and Greece (Athens, Sparta, Corinth, etc.) and Egypt (Memphis). City states traded and made war with each other, like nation-states do now. Sometimes city states became empires, controlling other city states. Greeks shared ethnicity and language and certainly would have seen themselves as "Hellenic" (Greek), but they organized around a city state and fought regularly with other Hellenic city-states. While Athenians had a shared belief in democracy, most city states were led by hereditary princes or kings.

Before the 1800s, Europe was completely organized around empires and kingdoms (monarchies) rather than language, culture, or ethnicity. Although peasants may have had a strong sense of identlty, they did not exhibit nationalism in the modern sense of the term; Europe was not yet a continent comprised of nation-states with boundaries based on the ethnic origin of the people. Instead, the continent was controlled by absolute monarchs whose territories overlapped ethnic and linguistic groups and changed with war, treaties and marriages of convenience. Because of this, peasants did not have a sense of belonging to a nation-state. Their allegiance was to their family, their village and, probably, to their church (think back to you and your classmates' identities expressed in the Who Am I assignments).  The world over the next hill was often a mystery to them (like Burma or Ecuador may be to you). Most people spent their entire lives living within a fifty kilometre radius. This was the case in earlier times and other parts of the world as well (Assyrian, Babylonian, Persian, Macedonian, Roman, Chinese, Mongol, Inca, British and Soviet empires all contained different ethnic and linquistic groups).

Louis XIV (14th), King of France from 1643 to 1715, said, "I am the state." In other words, as the absolute monarch, Louis was France. Without the king, there was no country of France. His wish was law, and the people of France existed only as his subjects. France belonged to him and his descendants. The French people owed their loyalty to the king and had no say in government decision-making. The identity of the French people and the country of France came from the king. Other European countries had similar experiences.

All that changed during a relatively brief period in human history marked by the French Revolution and the Napoleonic Era. These periods and events are considered by scholars as the key to understanding the rise of the nation-state, national identity and nationalism.

The French Revolution

By 1789, a revolutionary change in French society was occurring. The newly elected National Assembly of France issued The Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen. This document made revolutionary statements such as these:

  • "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights." (All French citizens are equal to each other and have the same rights as everyone else, including the king.)
  • "The sources of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation." (A country's people and not the king control the nation.)

If the French Revolution helped to establish the concepts of national identity, nation state, and nationalism in France, the Napoleonic Era helped spread these concepts to the remainder of Europe. The Napoleonic Era had its roots in the French Revolution. The French general, Napoleon Bonaparte, took France on a war of conquest throughout Europe spreading the idea of nationalism to Europeans everywhere—all to the dismay of European monarchs who felt their power was threatened by such concepts.

The Industrial Revolution

Meanwhile, the Industrial Revolution was happening at the same time as both the French Revolution and the Napoleonic Era and continued into the early 20th century. People began to move to crowded cities to work in factories. The destruction of old customs, traditions and identities set individuals adrift. The rapid change created alienation among the public. In search of an identity, people turned to nationalism.

We will explore these events in more detail in the following pages.

Multiple Perspectives - Did nationalism start with the American Revolution?

Many historians argue that nationalism began with the American Revolution in 1776, which occurred before the French Revolution. At the time, the United States of America (USA) did not yet exist as a nation-state but instead it was a collection of British colonies. The colonists were upset by their lack of rights and freedoms. They felt that Britain had too much control over their lives. Eventually, the colonists banded together to create their own nation-state where their rights would be recognized. An army was formed, by common people, who believed that they were fighting for the right to establish their own nation. The British did not recognize these new nationalists and considered them rebels and traitors. Seven years of war followed until the British government finally withdrew from the 13 colonies, allowing the Americans to govern themselves.

We will concentrate, however, on the French Revolution because in the context of the times, the American Revolution was an isolated event in the far-off colonies. These were colonies rising up against the empire. France, on the other hand, was a leading world power. France was the centre of an empire, not a rebellious colony. While French revolutionaries were encouraged by the success of the American revolution, it was the French Revolution, and its aftermath, that allowed the Enlightenment ideas and nationalism to spread through Europe. Note also that the leaders of both the American and French revolutions were influenced by the ideas of the European Enlightment thinkers. Pre-revolutionary France became the centre of Enlightenment thinking well before the American Revolution.

What are the origins of Nationalism?

The French Revolution

Despite the noise and confusion, something remarkable was going on in the minds of French people at this time: they were becoming nationalistic. That they had taken a hand in their own destiny and forced a new nation state (a democratic state) had much to do with it. Another reason was that on the very day that the National Convention first met, the French army, to the surprise of everyone, turned back the Prussian army at Valmy (September 20, 1792). This boosted the morale of the people of France tremendously. They saw themselves then as the victorious champions of liberty, successfully resisting the forces of autocracy.

National Symbols of France

The storming of the Bastille on July 14, 1789, immediately became a symbol of French nationalism. It was proof that power no longer resided in the King but in the people, based on the theories developed by Enlightenment philosophers. July 14 is still a national holiday in France, celebrated as Bastille Day. Can you think of a similar national celebration in Canada?

On July 27, 1789, Louis accepted (not willingly) the tricolour cockade in official recognition of the Revolution. The colours red and blue were historic colours representing the city of Paris. White represented the monarchy. Wearing the cockade soon became compulsory for revolutionaries.

The cockade was usually worn on a hat and the hat of choice for French revolutionaries was the Phrygian cap (made of red felt or wool). This cap had its roots in ancient Greece. Roman slaves who had won their freedom wore the cap. During the Enlightenment, the cap became a symbol of liberty and the French took that meaning to heart. Soldiers in the revolutionary army often wore the cap as part of their uniforms. Because of its red colour, the Phrygian cap in France came to be known as the bonnet rouge (or "red cap"), but it was also referred to as bonnet de la liberté (Liberty cap). During The Terror, many wore the cap to show their support for the revolution. (Avoiding suspicion and perhaps the guillotine may have been incentives.)

The Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen (click the link), adopted by the National Assembly in the early days of the French Revolution, was meant as a pledge of what the Assembly intended to accomplish for the people of France. It was full of grand ideas of freedom, equality, and nationalism.

During the war with Austria and Prussia, French nationalism really grew. That is, the French people were taking the first steps in forming the nation state of France with loyalty to the nation of French people and not just the king. When the combined Prussian and Austrian armies were advancing on the French capital of Paris in July 1792, the French people rallied to the cry of Liberté, Egalité, Fraternité (Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity) in the defence of France:

  • Liberty meant freedom for all French people.
  • Equality meant that all French people were equal to each other—no three estates where nobles or clergy had special privileges.
  • Fraternity meant "brotherhood" or that the French people had a common heritage as a French nation. Notice that the idea of being loyal to the king was never mentioned; it was loyalty to the French nation that was evolving.

Summary of the French Revolution

Because you have covered a tremendous amount of ground in studying the French Revolution, likely you will appreciate the following summary!

  • The monarchy was destroyed.
  • The old social, political, and economic structure from the Ancien Régime was destroyed.
  • Based on Enlightenment ideas, the French developed the notion that the nation state and the government belong to the people and not a king. A republic was established where leaders were elected.
  • The more radical views of the Jacobins led to the Reign of Terror in an attempt to secure the Revolution.
  • Based on Enlightenment ideas, the French developed the notion that all the citizens of a nation state are equal—egalitarianism.
  • Nationalism in France was developed out of love for the nation state and belief in control for and by the people.
  • Ideas about egalitarianism, the nation belonging to the people, and nationalism spread to the people of other European kingdoms.

The most important point in this course is the one about nationalism. No longer were the French loyal to the their king or the church or a local nobleman. Now, they were loyal to the French nation and the ideas of liberty and equality. This new kind of loyalty was destined to spread throughout Europe and eventually the world.

Napoleon and Nationalism

What are the origins of nationalism?

The Napoleonic Era

Big Idea: The concept of nation is shaped by historical, geographical, political, economic, and social aspects. Nationalism develops from the concepts of identity and nation.

Required Reading

Please read Chapter 2, pages 53 - 56 in the textbook.

 

By 1797 the French Revolution had foundered.  Various factions came to power and lost power; each seemed more interested in fighting each other than in building a stronger country.  A French general, Napoleon Bonaparte, seized power in 1799 and proceeded to build a strong central government.

Napoleon's Rise to Power

By the age of 27, Napoleon had worked his way up through the military ranks and secured the command of the French army in Italy where he had won a great victory over the Austrians in 1796. Next, in 1798, Napoleon was sent to Egypt. His orders were to take Egypt to block Britain's supply lines to India. Although Napoleon quickly achieved victory over the Egyptians on land, the British destroyed the French fleet in the Battle of the Nile. The British had cut off Napoleon's supply line to France and stranded his army in Egypt. Napoleon returned to Paris without his army, where the people, who were unaware of France's losses in Egypt, gave him a hero's welcome. (News travelled slowly in 1799!)

Back in France, Napoleon found the Directory (the revolutionary government from 1795-1799), in a state of disarray with inefficiency and corruption everywhere. France was desperate for a strongman to take charge, and he felt himself to be that man.

On November 9, 1799, Napoleon and two members of the Directory overthrew the government with the help of loyal soldiers. They established yet another type of government in France—the Consulate, with Napoleon as First Consul, essentially the dictator of France. Napoleon had the final say in government, but he kept many of the reforms made by the Revolution such as egalitarianism and liberalism. As a result, most of the French people accepted his dictatorship. Napoleon was very popular in France and he had the loyalty of the French army.

Thus the French Revolution ended with Napoleon's coup d'etat in 1799. Napoleon argued that his take-over was an attempt to save the revolution and he took steps to entrench French nationalism. He set about trying to solve France's domestic problems by creating a national bank, reforming the tax, legal and education systems and modernizing the state bureaucracy by producing the first generation of professional civil servants. All of these activities consolidated steps towards the creation of the modern nation-state that had begun during the French Revolution.

Napoleon and the Spread of Nationalism to Europe

Despite his contributions to the organization of the modern nation-state, Napoleon is best known for his foreign military adventures. His attempt to conquer Europe was designed to eliminate Britain as France's main competitor. While Napoleon was a military genius, his lasting importance rests with his re-organization of Europe. His creation of the Confederation of the Rhine and domination of most of the continent allowed the ideas of the French Revolution to spread throughout Europe. For the first time, Spaniards, Germans, and Italians were exposed to ideas of the modern state.

Another factor that aroused nationalistic feelings was that Napoleon had changed the very nature of warfare. Before Napoleon, wars had been fought by kings to increase their personal wealth. Their nobles fought with them, and, of course, some commoners were forced into service. Most people paid little attention, hoping only to avoid the fighting.

Napoleon's wars, however, were total wars, fought not for kings but for countries. Whole nations got caught up in such wars, and everyone contributed to the war effort. Officers were promoted on merit, not because a noble could afford to raise a regiment or wealthy person could buy an officer's rank, as in Britain in this period.

Feelings of patriotism and nationalism began to run high in all countries exposed to this kind of warfare, and the higher they ran, the shakier Napoleon's hold on Europe became.

Napoleon's Reforms in France

Napoleon was a great military commander, but he was also an able governor and administrator. He inherited all that was done during the French Revolution—both the positive changes and the resulting chaos. Napoleon's task was to consolidate the Revolution's positive accomplishments, to work out the details of proposed reforms, and, in some cases, to reject unworkable ideas. In other words, he had to make the new France work.

Napoleon's most noteworthy domestic achievements in France with respect to aspects of the nation-state are as follows:

  • Economic: Napoleon introduced many economic reforms in France. Taxes were collected fairly and efficiently, roads were built, commerce was stimulated, and attempts were made to balance the budget. Napoleon also founded the Bank of France to control French finances.
  • Religion: The French Revolutionwas hard on the Catholic Church. It alienated many French people (devout Catholics). Napoleon arranged an agreement (the Concordat) with the Catholic Pope to ease most of the tensions between the church and the French State. Confiscated church lands were kept by those who had bought them, but church buildings were returned. The French state paid the clergy and chose bishops, but the Pope consecrated and installed them. However, when the Pope challenged his authority later, Napoleon had the Pope taken prisoner and held in France.
  • Political: Separate government departments set up in the early years of the Revolution did not work efficiently; they were too independent. Napoleon centralized all political power. Although this was not as democratic, it was far more efficient.
  • The Code Napoleon (or Code Civil) was a standard system of laws created for all France in which all male citizens were considered equal before the law (women were still second class citizens). The Code was put into use in all the lands that Napoleon conquered. Today, this code remains the legal system of France (with some amendments) and elements of the Civil Code can be found in other countries, including the Canadian province of Quebec.
  • Education: an extensive system of public schools (the lycées) was established. Although very strict and open mostly to the wealthy, the system was a first step to universal public education.

Although Napoleon clearly kept many of the ideals of the Revolution alive through these achievements, along with promoting egalitarianism and liberalism, he also did a great deal for himself. Because he was, in effect, dictator of France, he decided that the title of First Consul was not enough; in 1804 he decided to become Emperor. The people of France, as he knew they would, voted overwhelmingly in support of this move. After all, Napoleon not only brought France out of the chaos of revolution, but through war and conquest, he also made France the most powerful nation in Europe. This made Napoleon very popular with the French people and swelled their pride, their nationalism, for France.

The Fall of Napoleon

During both the French Revolution and Napoleon's reign, France was almost always at war with shifting coalitions of European countries. Allies came and went on both sides. Napoleon enjoyed great military successes throughout this period, but the Russia campaign in 1812 proved disastrous. The Russian scorched earth policy, combined with the harsh winter, spelled defeat for Napoleon's troops. The Sixth Coalition, the combined forces of Prussia, Britain, Russia, Austria, and Sweden, seized the opportunity and finally defeated Napoleon at Leipzig in 1813.

After a short exile on the French island of Elba, Napoleon escaped and returned to France in 1815 and raised another army, only to be defeated at Waterloo and exiled again, this time to the British-held island of St. Helena - in the middle of the South Atlantic Ocean - where he eventually died from stomach cancer in 1821.

The Rise of the Nation-State

Napoleon's domestic reforms promoted changes introduced by the French Revolution. He made the structures of the state more like a modern nation-state by establishing a permanent bureaucracy, a codified system of laws, and a modern education system. Economic reforms such as the Bank of France and tax reform ensured professional management of the economy. The Concordat separated church and state and continues to serve as the basis of modern liberal-democratic states, like Canada. The Napoleonic Code swept away the maze of medieval laws based on divergent regional customs. The introduction of the lycees introduced mass public education and allowed advancement based on merit rather than the luck of birth. All of these elements are clearly recognizable in modern Canadian society.

Spread of Nationalism

Napoleon became the rallying point of French nationalism. He actively cultivated this sentiment by creating the Legion of Honour and nationalist symbols through public works such as the Arc de Triomphe monument to French soldiers. The French were now so supportive of their nation that they were willing to die for it. This was demonstrated by their participation in the series of wars led by Napoleon. For many French people, the Napoleonic era is considered the high point in the history of their country.

Countries that were occuppied by Napoloeon have a less favourable view of Napoleon, yet his march across Europe spread the concept of nationalism beyond the borders of France. In many ways this transfer was unintentional. French soldiers, while occupying neighbouring territory, talked of being "citizens" of the nation of France rather than "subjects" of some monarch. The French people had become a nation and an example of what was possible to the people of Europe.

While French troops spread the concept of nationalism, their occupation created resentment among the local populations who were now ruled by a foreign power. This emergence of a national identity among European peoples became more evident over the next half century.

Case Study Tibet: A Nation within a Nation-State

Case Study Tibet: A Nation within a Nation-State Big Idea: The relationship between nation, nationalism and identity. History, language, religion/spirituality, culture...

Big Idea: The relationship between nation, nationalism and identity. History, language, religion/spirituality, culture and geography impact nationalism.

The example of Tibet demonstrates how history, language, religion/spirituality, culture or geography impacts nationalism. Review this page before completing the upcoming assignments.

History of Tibet


Tibet was first united in the 7th century of the Common Era.  Over the centuries Tibet has been entangled in the numerous conquests and power struggles of many different ruling regimes including the Mongol, Manchu and Qing empires.  Although frequently subject to foreign rule, Tibet was always a cultural entity distinct from the dynasties and empires of which it was a part.  Much of the conflict between modern day China and Tibet is a result of the fact that the People’s Republic of China claims that Tibet has long been under Chinese control.  However, many Tibetans feel that Tibet has historically been a separate nation, only under direct Chinese authority since Chinese forces, under communist leader Mao Zedong, invaded in 1950. Scholars continue to debate this issue. 

The historical separateness of Tibet has deeply affected Tibetan nationalism.  This separateness is evident in the fact that there are mythical creation stories unique to Tibet alone.  The fact that Tibetans have withstood, to some degree, near continuous outside influence throughout the centuries is a source of nationalistic pride to Tibetans, particularly in modern times, where Chinese rule is seen as unjust occupation.

Language of Tibet

Currently, Tibet’s official language is Chinese.  However, the Tibetan people speak a group of languages, unique to the area, consisting of many different dialects collectively referred to as Tibetan.  In Tibet, primary education is generally taught in Tibetan, and secondary education is taught in Chinese. The Tibetan linguistic uniqueness is one element that brings Tibetans together allowing not only for ease of communication within the group, but a language for religion, literature and other forms of culture.

Religion/Spirituality

Tibetan Buddhism is a form of Buddhism that considers the Dalai Lama its spiritual leader.  The word “lama,” roughly translated, means teacher and the Dalai Lama is the highest of all lamas, an incarnation of a Buddhist master.  Tibetan Buddhism is a highly ritualized form of Buddhism and is known for its elaborate, colourful ceremonies, and distinctive sounds and music, most notably, the chanting of the Tibetan Buddhist monks.

Caption: Tibetan Monks in Song Zan Lin Si temple (photo credit: Chris Webster)

There is also a small but notable Islamic community in Tibet and traditions from the pre-Buddhist indigenous shamanistic religion known as Bön, still influence religious practices today.

The common religion of the Tibetan people is a large part of Tibetan nationalism.  This is because the spiritual leader of Tibetan Buddhism, the Dalai Lama, is also the political leader of the Tibetan people.  Forced to flee to India in 1951, the Dalai Lama is the head of the Tibetan Government in Exile situated in Dharamsala, India.  A popular and charismatic figure, the Dalai Lama is the voice of Tibetan nationalism, bringing attention to the plight of Tibetans.

Culture of Tibet

Many aspects of Tibetan culture are unique to the nation.  Rice, a common staple food in surrounding areas, does not grow in Tibet due to the high altitude.  Instead, the staple food eaten by Tibetans is barley, known locally as Tsampa.  The national drink is salted butter tea.

Tibetan people dress modestly and conservatively, wearing long sleeves even during warm summers.  One item of clothing with ritual significance is the Khata, a white silk scarf, given at various occasions as a symbol of good will.

Many other aspects of Tibetan culture are unique, such as music, architecture, poetry, art, festivals and holidays.

Caption: Tibetan woman in Shangri-La (photo credit: Chris Webster)

Many in Tibet fear that Tibetan culture is rapidly disappearing due to the influx of Han Chinese immigrants. In fact, the Tibetan government in exile reports that Tibetans are now a minority in their homeland and also make the claim that Chinese immigration into the region is a deliberate strategy being undertaken by the Chinese government to dilute Tibetan culture.

Geography of Tibet

Tibet is a high altitude, mountainous region found in the centre of Asia.  Much of the Himalayan Mountain Range, including the world’s highest mountain, Mount Everest, is located in Tibet.  The Tibet Autonomous Region, the area that the Chinese government considers to be Tibet, is only a small part of the territory that Tibetans consider to be Tibet.

Caption: Mount Everest from the Tibet side. (photo credit: Paal A. Martinussen)

Geography strongly affects Tibetan nationalism.  Many Tibetans do not accept the division of allegedly Tibetan territory.  In addition, geography affects Tibetan nationalism because of the effect that Tibet’s location in the world has on the Tibetan way of life.  Living at such a high altitude, in relatively harsh conditions and physical isolation of the plateau from the surrounding countries has resulted in a unique mountain culture that binds Tibetans together.

It should also be noted that many Tibetans are scattered around the world, and many can be found in India.  Despite their scattered locations, the Tibetan nation is bound by many other nationalistic factors.

Define nation in a way that fits Social 20-1.

Reveal unit answer

A nation is a group of people who feel connected by shared identity, history, culture, language, religion, geography, politics, or collective memory.

For diploma-style writing, the important move is to explain whether that shared identity should guide personal choices, political action, or loyalty to a state.
