Virtual Experiments

Computer simulations have made possible experiments that could not otherwise be ethically conducted.  In addition to experimentation, virtual reality programs have been used to treat psychological disorders.  The basis of virtual treatment is the notion that an effective way to reduce pain, whether emotional or physical, is to distract the person with something else.  The "something else" in this case is a computer-generated virtual experience.

Virtual Therapy Example
In one study, (summarized from Scientific American, August 2004), a woman had acquired several obsessive-compulsive behaviours linked to her fear of spiders.  She regularly used pesticides in her vehicle and at home to eradicate any spiders that lived there.  She sealed her bedroom windows with duct-tape to prevent any spiders from entering her house.  She sealed freshly laundered clothing in plastic bags to prevent "contamination" from spiders.  Her condition worsened, so much so, that she was hesitant to leave her house.  After 20 years of coping with her phobia, she eventually sought help.      

 

Her treatment involved gradually introducing her to a virtual spider.  Progressively, the fear decreased and the woman became more comfortable.  In the first few sessions of virtual therapy, the woman saw a virtual spider in a virtual kitchen.  She approached as close as possible (using a handheld joystick to navigate through the three-dimensional scene), with a goal to come within a metre of the virtual spider.  Her goal was to reach for the spider while wearing a special glove that tracked the position of her hand.  The spider was then to make a brief noise and flee a few inches away from the "cyberhand". 

The woman was then instructed to pick up a virtual vase with her cyberhand.  When she let go of the vase, the vase remained in midair but an animated spider with wiggling legs came out.  The spider then drifted to the floor of the virtual kitchen, accompanied by a brief sound effect from the classic horror movie Psycho.  The patient repeated similar tasks until she reported little anxiety surrounding the virtual spiders.


Our patient then moved on to the next challenge.  Her final therapy sessions added tactile feedback to her virtual experience - specifically a toy spider with an electromagnetic position sensor.  The toy was suspended in front of the patient, which allowed her to feel the furry object with one hand while touching the virtual spider with her cyberhand.  In the end, the patient had a significantly reduced fear of spiders and her obsessive-compulsive behaviours also disappeared.  Her specific success was unusual - after treatment she was able to hold a live tarantula for several minutes with little anxiety.  It is hoped that the future of virtual therapy will expand to help people cope with more disorders, both physical and psychological.
