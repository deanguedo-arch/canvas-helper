Graphic Display

Researchers have many options when visually displaying data.  No matter which display format a researcher uses, less is often more.  Strunk and White reiterate this notion when they say:

"A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences for the same reason that a drawing should contain no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts."

In general, the less "ink" used in a display, the better the display.  Graphs should be no more complex than the data they portray.  Unnecessary "parts" are collectively known as "chart junk" and include irrelevant decoration, too much colour, and 3-D effects.  Many spreadsheet programs offer options for graphing that add chart junk to data displays.  Researchers should avoid the lure of such options.

Types of Graphs

Graphs are of several types, including line graphs, histograms, and box and whisker plots.  Three common display formats used by researchers include line graphs, pie charts, and bar graphs.  The best use of each type is described in the table below.

Type of Graph Application
Line Graphs Line graphs are used to track changes over time.  They can also be used to compare changes among two or more groups.  When the changes are small, line graphs are often preferable to bar graphs. 
Bar Graphs Bar graphs are used when comparing two or more groups or when tracking changes over time.  When changes are large, bar graphs are often preferable to line graphs.
Pie Charts Pie charts are best used when comparing the parts of a whole.  Pie charts do not show changes over time and, generally speaking, should be used infrequently.
