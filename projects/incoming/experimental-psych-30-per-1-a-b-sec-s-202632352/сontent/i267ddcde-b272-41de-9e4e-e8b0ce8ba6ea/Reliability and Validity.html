Reliability and Validity

Researchers in all sciences, not just psychology, want their experiments to be reliable so that others may repeat their experiments and obtain similar results.  When experiments are reliable and valid, conclusions reached can be transferred to similar situations in other parts of the world.

 Reliability can be divided into three categories: test-retest, internal, and inter-rater. 

The first category, test-retest reliability, requires that the psychologist obtain similar results with the same test or measurement at two different points in time. 

With internal reliability, various parts of a test or measurement must produce similar results. 

In the last category, inter-rater reliability, two or more psychologists (or "raters"), must come to similar conclusions regarding a measurement, test, or experiment.




 

Experiments, tests, and measurements must also be valid.  Validity can be divided into five categories. 

As the category implies, face validity refers to a test or technique appearing to measure what it is supposed to measure. 

With content validity, the test or technique used should actually measure the content or areas that are most important.  The test or technique used may not measure everything, and it does not have to -- as long as it measures the major areas of concern.

The third category, concurrent validity requires that similar tests or experiments yield the same results.  For example, two different I.Q. tests taken by the same individual should yield similar results. 

As the name suggests, predictive validity refers to how well behaviour can be predicted.  For example, different patients with the same diagnosis are predicted to respond the same way to the same treatment. 

The last category of validity, construct validity, requires that a test measures what it is designed to measure.  For example, if a test is designed to measure musical ability, but instead it measures verbal reasoning, it cannot be considered a valid test.
