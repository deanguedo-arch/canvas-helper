Core vocabularyInflation, purchasing power, nominal amount, fee

Inflation: a rise in the general price level over time. Purchasing power: what an amount of money can buy at the relevant prices. Nominal amount: the stated number of dollars before a price adjustment. Fee: a charge whose base and timing must be known to compare it.

Learn the method

Ask two different money questions

  1. What is the new price? Multiply an old target by 1 plus its percentage change. Here, $100 × 1.03 = $103.
  2. How much is in the account? Read the nominal balance: $101. Nothing was taken out.
  3. What can that balance buy in the old price scale? Divide by the price factor: $101 ÷ 1.03 ≈ $98.06 in start-of-year dollars. This is a comparison measure, not a withdrawal.

A fee is another cost, but its effect depends on what amount it is charged on and when. Later, you will compare two model runs while holding growth and inflation fixed and changing only the fee. For a real decision, use the actual goal price if known. Do not assume a riskier return will arrive by a deadline.

Why a future balance can be much larger than deposits

This FINLIT question fits here because it separates money contributed from growth produced by a model. It is not needed to complete Riley’s required task. Continue with the required lesson at any time.

The original Magic of Compounding Assignment 7.1, page 1, question 3 begins with $500, then compares $40 and $60 deposited each month for 40 years at an assumed 5% return. Watch the clip or read its transcript before running the two versions here.

Read the compounding transcript

Visuals: a rising line illustrates growth on earlier growth. The line is an illustration, not a verified forecast.

But what is it exactly? Basically, it's the snowball effect that happens with money and earning interest, or how interest makes money grow exponentially over time. If you put money into a savings account or investment that earns interest, in the beginning, interest is calculated on the initial amount you started with. But as that money starts to earn interest, you continue to earn interest not only that initial amount, but you begin to earn additional interest on the interest you've already made. And as the total amount of interest earned grows, the more interest you continue to earn. Hence, the snowball effect.

Read the purchasing-power transcript

Visuals: a presenter speaks to the camera. The basket and balance above provide the figures for this lesson.

You need to protect your purchasing power of your savings against the effects of inflation. You see, as prices rise, each dollar is able to buy less. Makes sense right? If the dollars you have saved for the future don't increase in value by at least as much as inflation, the purchasing power of those savings will fall, which pretty much defeats the purpose of saving. Remember inflation? Inflation is the increase in the average level of prices in the economy.

What happened to the $100 basket learner’s purchasing power?

Follow a complete example

Explain $101 in the new price scale

  1. Price change: $103 − $100 = $3, a 3% increase for this fixed basket.
  2. Dollar balance: $101 − $100 = $1 growth in the account; nothing has been withdrawn.
  3. Can it buy the basket? No. $103 − $101 = $2 short.
  4. Old-price comparison: $101 ÷ 1.03 ≈ $98.06 in start-of-year dollars. This is a measure of purchasing power, not a bank charge.
“The nominal balance increased from $100 to $101, but the unchanged basket now costs $103. It is $2 short. Dividing $101 by 1.03 gives about $98.06 in the old price scale, so purchasing power fell even though the dollar balance rose.”

Do not say “the bank took $2.94.” That would confuse a price adjustment with a transaction. The account still contains $101.

Apply independently · New situation

Update Riley’s materials plan

Riley is a fictional learner saving for required course materials. The original all-in quotation was $240. A new confirmed quotation increases that price by 5% and is valid until payment in two weeks. Riley has $248 saved now.

Riley will have a confirmed $10 of unallocated money in one week that can go to this target without affecting other obligations. Or Riley can buy a suitable used set for $226 all-in until the same deadline. Both sets meet the course requirement; the used set has cosmetic wear. No interest or market gain is assumed.

Your task

Calculate the new quoted price and the gap against $248. Choose either the new set with the confirmed $10 addition or the used set. In 3–4 sentences, explain timing, remaining money and one trade-off. Use the confirmed price for this goal; do not describe it as Canada’s current inflation rate.

After writing, compare with a model

The new quotation is $240 × 1.05 = $252, which is $4 more than Riley’s current $248. One valid plan is to wait one week for the confirmed $10, bringing the amount to $258, buy the new set before the two-week deadline and keep $6. A second valid plan is to buy the suitable used set for $226 and keep $22, accepting cosmetic wear. Neither plan depends on an assumed investment gain. Your response should explain the choice you actually selected.
