Learn the basics

A career title sounds interesting. What would the work involve?

You might like a title such as graphic designer and still be unsure what the person does during a working day. A career page can help, but it may contain more information than you need at first. How do you find the parts that answer your question, and avoid using one sentence as a promise about every job? That is the reading task for this lesson.

Choose the document that answers your question

A job is a particular position with an employer. An occupation groups work with similar duties. A career is a longer path that may include several jobs, learning and other life roles. An describes a kind of work across workplaces. A job posting advertises one opening. A broad description and a particular opening can both be useful, as long as we keep their different purposes visible.

Canada’s National Occupational Classification, or , organizes occupations into groups with identifying codes. A code helps you check whether two records cover the same group. It does not rank someone’s ability or make every job identical. We will keep that group information with the source when comparing occupations later in the lesson.

Read for work, conditions and preparation

A answers “What would I do?” A answers “When, where or under what demands?” Preparation describes the education, experience or other evidence used to enter the work. For example, “prepare notices” names a duty, “evening shifts” a condition and “provide examples of your writing” requested preparation. An appealing task does not answer the question about evening hours.

The strength of the wording matters. “Some employers prefer experience” does not say every applicant must have it. “Required” identifies a stated condition. We will read the words around a fact before turning it into a claim. We will also keep the place, section date and retrieval date attached: when a page was read and when its content was revised are different facts.

Use the method with the case

Read the useful sentence before making the claim

Jordan wants to know about visual problem solving. The profile’s duties include discussing project needs, developing visual solutions and preparing work for approval. Those tasks give the interest a connection to an occupation. They do not establish that Jordan has already done them in a workplace. We are deciding whether the direction deserves investigation.

A career cluster can group related possibilities for exploration, such as visual-communication work. A group has a more formal information-organizing purpose. Keeping the code and group label prevents us from combining numbers about different occupations simply because both titles involve design. It also reminds us that the Graphic Designer group includes illustrators and related work.

Ask a separate question about the schedule

For predictable finishing times, the duties field is not enough. I move to Working conditions. It says weekday work is usual and deadlines can bring overtime or weekend work. That gives a pattern and an exception. Removing the exception would create a much stronger claim than the record supports.

Now compare the fictional notice: usual weekday hours are stated, together with a possible Saturday shift before the festival. The profile and notice can both be accurate. They describe different levels of detail. We should ask how often busy periods change the hours of a particular opening, rather than average the statements or choose the more convenient one.

Separate a preparation summary from an entry decision

A credential is a formal record of qualification. A portfolio is a selected collection of work showing what someone can do. The profile says design education is common and a strong portfolio important; it does not decide admission to a program. The employer notice asks for three layout examples and describes paid experience as helpful. That wording lets us distinguish requested evidence from an experience preference.

A person may have relevant school examples without a paid studio job. A truthful portfolio explains the task, the person’s contribution and the work produced. It should not change a school activity into professional employment. Whether the examples meet this opening’s expectations still depends on the kinds of layout and presentation requested.

Keep the source and its limits with the answer

The three-pass reading method is useful here: locate the field that answers the question, explain its fact in your own words, then state what it supports and leaves open. For example, the overtime statement supports asking about scheduling; it does not support “every design job always ends late.”

The record’s March 22, 2023 section date and September 28, 2026 retrieval date also need different labels. Retrieval tells us when the page was read. It does not make every field newly updated. The supplied record is enough for this exercise; a real future decision would need current information relevant to its particular opening.

What is a worked example? An answer that is already completed, with the decisions or calculations explained. As you read, connect the answer to the records; you do not need to copy it.

A research note explains what a record supports and what still needs checking. An occupation profile covers a kind of work; one posting describes one opening. Their different scopes matter when you compare them.

Let’s answer Jordan’s question without deciding more than the evidence allows. I begin with the duties because the question mentions an interest in visual problem solving. Developing visual solutions relates to that interest. This is my reason to keep graphic design under consideration, not proof that Jordan is qualified for every design task.

Next I check the other part of the question: predictable finishing times. Both records mention possible work beyond the usual weekday pattern. Prairie Print Studio makes the condition more specific by naming a possible Saturday shift. I therefore keep the schedule concern in the conclusion even though the duties look promising.

I attach the profile’s place and dates so a reader can find the basis of the claim. Finally, I turn the remaining gap into a precise question about hours and portfolio expectations. The completed note below joins an encouraging connection to those unresolved points. Notice how “worth investigating” follows from the evidence without becoming a job offer.

Completed research note: “Graphic design is worth investigating because its visual-solution work relates to Jordan's interest in arranging information. The Graphic Designer profile is an Alberta profile, with relevant sections updated March 22, 2023 and retrieved September 28, 2026. It also indicates that deadlines can affect hours. Prairie Print Studio's fictional posting includes a possible Saturday shift, so Jordan should not assume every job has a predictable weekday-only schedule. The next useful question is how often a particular opening changes its hours. Jordan also needs to investigate portfolio expectations before applying.”

If we wrote “this job definitely fits,” we would skip the hours and preparation. If we wrote “no designer has regular hours,” we would turn a possibility into a universal statement. The example avoids both by naming what interests Jordan and what still needs checking.

You will now investigate the preparation statement yourself. Keep the same documents and person. Focus on the difference between the three layout examples requested and the previous paid experience described as helpful.
