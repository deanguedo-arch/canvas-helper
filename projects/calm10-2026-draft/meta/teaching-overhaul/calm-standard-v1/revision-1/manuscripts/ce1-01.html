Learn the basics

How do you know what you might be good at?

A friend sees something you have made and says, “You should do that for a living.” The suggestion might be encouraging, but what makes it useful? Enjoying a task, doing it well and wanting the conditions that come with it are three different things. In this lesson, we will learn how to turn an experience into a reason to investigate a kind of work.

Look for three different clues

An is something you enjoy or want to explore. A is an ability you can demonstrate through an action. A is something that matters to you about working, such as having variety or knowing when the day will finish. These clues belong together in a career decision, but each answers a different question. “I like drawing” does not show how well I explain an idea with a drawing.

A career goal describes a direction for work or learning. A life goal describes what you want your wider life to support. Someone might want to investigate creative work and also keep regular time for activities outside work. A promising direction should be examined against both goals. We are looking for a next step that makes sense, rather than asking a short school experience to decide an entire future.

Start with something that actually happened

Suppose a learner rewrites confusing club directions and two readers can then follow them without help. The rewritten directions are an action; the readers managing without help are a result. Together, they provide for some in explaining information. Saying “an excellent communicator” gives a label without showing a reader why it fits. Naming the action and result makes the claim open to examination.

The size of the claim matters too. One set of successful directions does not establish that the learner can explain every subject to every audience. That limit leaves room for learning while preserving the useful . Our method will be to describe an action and result, identify a possible , explain the limit, then ask a question about work that could use the skill.

Use the method with the case

Read what Jordan did, then read what Jordan wants

We now have more than a friend’s opinion. In the poster row, two classmates could not find essential information. Jordan changed the layout and asked them to check it. That sequence lets us connect a specific action to a reported result. The reflection adds that Jordan enjoyed deciding which information came first and improving the draft after feedback. Enjoyment gives us a reason to keep exploring; the action gives us to examine.

The science display adds a complication worth keeping. Jordan made a checklist and arranged a check-in, but found repeated follow-up tiring. A person can be able to organize a task and still dislike part of the work. If we record only the successful group result, we lose a useful question about how often a future role would require chasing incomplete work.

The schedule note answers a third question. Jordan wants reasonably predictable hours and regular time outside work. This is a connected to a life goal. It is not a claim that Jordan has already organized a workplace or that graphic design provides predictable hours. We will use it to ask about conditions in work that might use Jordan’s strengths.

Give the reader enough detail to judge the claim

A claim is a statement you want someone to accept. Before writing “Jordan has some in organizing visual information,” ask what the reader could check. The problem, layout change and classmates’ response give that statement a basis. The result need not be a prize or a grade: readers finding the information is useful of what changed.

Ownership of the action matters. Jordan organized the display check-in; the group found and completed the missing caption. “Jordan completed everyone’s work” would add a contribution the notebook never reports. Describe Jordan’s part in the team result. This keeps a truthful claim useful without making the person responsible for everything the team did.

Use an inventory as a starting suggestion

An or strengths inventory organizes a person’s answers to questions about themselves. If it suggests creative work for Jordan, it tells us something about the reported answers. It does not independently establish professional . We can test the suggestion against the poster and ask whether the working conditions would fit Jordan’s goals. A disagreement between an inventory and experience becomes a question to investigate. No inventory account is needed here.

Carry a into a new question

A transferable is one that may be useful in another setting. Organizing a poster could provide a starting point for organizing instructions or explaining information to a customer. It does not establish knowledge of professional software, client budgets or workplace deadlines. The next question should connect the action to work without skipping the learning still needed.

For Jordan, “How often does visual-communication work involve urgent revisions?” is useful because the answer could affect the schedule preference. “Is design good?” would give us much less direction. A provisional goal is a direction that can change as develops. Jordan can investigate roles using visual explanations while keeping the possibility of changing the plan.

Financial goals can become part of that investigation: for example, keeping later training costs within a budget. This lesson does not supply a training price or wage, so we cannot decide affordability yet. We are establishing a direction and the questions that later would need to answer. Connect the video to this task. The video explains influences on values. This lesson adds a separate distinction: Jordan’s poster action supports a skill claim, while the predictable-schedule preference helps frame a question about work.

  1. Start with the recorded action and result. Two classmates could not find the time and location. Jordan moved those details into a larger heading, reduced decoration and asked for another check. Both classmates then found the details without help. This gives us something observable to use.
  2. Explain the possible skill. These actions suggest some skill in organizing visual information and responding to feedback. The claim explains what the action shows; it does more than call Jordan “brilliant.”
  3. Keep the claim within the evidence. The notebook describes one school task. It does not show that Jordan can manage a professional client’s project, use professional software or meet every workplace deadline. A useful skill claim can still have a limit.
  4. Connect a question to Jordan’s goals. Jordan wants visual problem solving and clear explanations, with regular time outside work. Asking about revisions and unpredictable deadlines could help Jordan test whether a possible role fits both goals.

Let’s build the poster response together. Begin with the reader’s problem: the time and location were hard to find. That context explains why enlarging and moving information mattered. If I began with “Jordan is creative,” the reader would have to guess what Jordan had done.

Next I connect the action to the result. Jordan changed the layout, reduced decorative text and asked the same classmates to check it. Both then found the information without help. I can therefore describe some in organizing visual information and responding to feedback. I keep “some skill” because one school task gives a narrower basis than repeated professional work.

The final step connects that to Jordan’s goals. Visual communication is a direction worth investigating. The preference for regular time outside work gives us a question about revisions and deadlines. Notice that the shapes the question; it is not added to the claim as if it were an ability. Read how those parts fit together in the completed response below.

“During a school poster task, Jordan enlarged the time and location and reduced decorative text after two classmates had trouble finding the details. Both readers could then locate the information without help. This suggests some skill in organizing visual information and responding to feedback. It is evidence from one school task, so it does not establish professional design expertise. Jordan could investigate visual communication work and ask how often the work involves revisions and unpredictable deadlines. This would test the career goal against Jordan's wish for regular time outside work.”

The completed response gives a reader a path from an observed change to a possible direction. It also says what has not been established: professional design expertise. That boundary does not cancel the useful . It identifies the difference between a reason to explore and a qualification to claim.
