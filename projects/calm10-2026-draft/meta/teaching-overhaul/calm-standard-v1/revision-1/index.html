A

A career idea becomes more useful when you can explain what led you to it. You will learn to distinguish what someone enjoys, what they have actually done and what they want work to support before reading the experience notebook.

B

A friend sees something you have made and says, “You should do that for a living.” The suggestion might be encouraging, but what makes it useful? Enjoying a task, doing it well and wanting the conditions that come with it are three different things. In this lesson, we will learn how to turn an experience into a reason to investigate a kind of work.

Career websites contain several kinds of information. Before reading the profile and employer notice, learn which question each kind can answer. This will help you compare work with a person’s interests and preferred conditions.

B

You might like a title such as graphic designer and still be unsure what the person does during a working day. A career page can help, but it may contain more information than you need at first. How do you find the parts that answer your question, and avoid using one sentence as a promise about every job? That is the reading task for this lesson.

A

When you explore a career, you may read that an occupation is growing, pays well or has few openings nearby. Each statement is a labour-market claim: something a person or publication says about work and workers. You need to understand what the statement describes before using it to make a plan.

B

A report describes promising work across Alberta, but a search near home finds only a few advertisements. It is tempting to think one source must be wrong. Before deciding, ask what each one is measuring. A forecast, a count of people already employed and a list of advertised openings can tell us different things about the same broad area of work.

A

A high-school route connects what a learner has completed with what a future program requires. A useful plan shows the order of preparation, the dates it must fit and the time and money available.

B

A student can list all the courses that seem useful for a career and still have a plan that cannot be followed. A later course may require an earlier one, a school may offer it only in a particular term, or a portfolio may be due before the course that helps produce it finishes. How do you turn a list of useful preparation into a route that fits the actual time available?

A

An interest can lead toward employment or a small venture. Before comparing the offers, learn who arranges the work, which costs each person carries and what the stated money amounts represent.

B

Someone enjoys a practical task and sees money coming in from it. Does that make running a venture the best way to work? The sales figure is only part of the story. Someone also supplies materials, arranges the work, keeps records and accepts the uncertainty of finding the next customer. We will compare ways of working by following both the money and those responsibilities.

A

A change in time or responsibilities can make an old plan stop fitting. Revising means checking what changed and finding a workable next step toward the goal.

B

You make a plan for something you want to learn, then another commitment changes the week. The original trip no longer fits. It can feel as though the whole plan has failed, but the trip and the purpose are not the same thing. How can you keep a worthwhile goal while changing the route honestly? We will work through that question using supplied weekly plans.
