Work-related experiences · Lesson 1

Show what you can do at work

Learning goal

Use a job advertisement and an applicant’s past experiences to write a truthful example of a skill.

What you will produce

A short application response for Rowan: one job duty and one or two sentences showing a matching experience.

Teacher review copy · Your responses and completion save in this browser, separately from the course’s saved work. View the current lesson.

How to complete this lessonRead, follow an example, practise, then write
  1. Meet Rowan. Read the situation so you know whose application you are preparing.
  2. Read the two documents. Learn what a job posting tells you, then read Rowan’s experience notes. Answer the short question about the posting.
  3. Follow the demonstration. See why an action can show a skill, then follow one complete application example.
  4. Try the same method. In guided practice, check your evidence match, plan and write a response, then compare and revise.
  5. Check your understanding. Answer the six practice questions and read their explanations.
  6. Use a new case. In the festival application, use the new posting and notes to write your own response as Rowan.

Core vocabularySix words used in this lesson
Job posting
An employer’s advertisement for a job, describing the work and the person they need.
Duty
A task the person would be expected to do in that job.
Skill
An ability used to do something, such as explaining instructions or checking a list.
Evidence
Specific information that supports a claim. Here, it is an example of what Rowan did.
Context
The setting and purpose of an experience: where it happened and what needed doing.
Result
What happened after the action: what was completed, changed or improved.

Start with the situation

Rowan is applying for a first job

Prairie Library Hub runs Saturday activities for families. It needs someone at the welcome desk to greet visitors, explain how to sign in and help keep its records accurate. Rowan would like this job but has never been paid to work in a library.

An application is a request to be considered for a job. In this lesson, the employer asks applicants for a short written example of relevant experience.

Rowan has helped at school and community events. Those experiences may show skills the library needs. The challenge is to choose a useful example and explain it honestly, instead of simply writing “I would be good at this job.”

Your role: help Rowan prepare a short application response. You will read the employer’s advertisement, find a matching experience in Rowan’s notes and write as Rowan using “I.” You do not need to supply your own work history.

First, find out what the employer needs. Then look at what Rowan has already done.

Read the employer’s advertisement

What is a job posting?

A job posting is an advertisement an employer uses to find someone for a job. It usually tells you the job title, the work involved, qualifications or skills, practical details such as hours, and how to apply. Its duties are the tasks the successful applicant would do.

At this hub, signing in means recording that you have arrived. Reservation records list the people who have booked a place in an activity.

Read this practice notice in two passes. First, use the title and hours to understand the role. Then read “What you will do.” Those duties tell Rowan which experiences would be useful in an application.

Prairie Library Hub · Community programs

We’re hiring a Welcome Desk Helper

Help families get a clear, friendly start to our Saturday activities.

When
Four-hour Saturday events
Where
Community program welcome desk
Getting started
Training provided

About the role

You will be one of the first people visitors meet. You will help them understand the sign-in process and ask a supervisor when a request is unfamiliar.

What you will do

  • Welcome visitors and explain the sign-in process.
  • Keep reservation records accurate.
  • Ask for help with unfamiliar requests.
  • Protect visitor information.

What we are looking for

Clear communication, careful organization, sound judgement and respect for privacy.

Tell us about your experience

Give a short example of something you have done that matches one of these duties. Your example may come from school, volunteering or a community activity.

Find the work Rowan would do

Which detail tells Rowan what the helper would actually do?

The posting tells us what the library needs. It does not tell us whether Rowan can do it. For that, we need an example from Rowan’s past experience.

Read the applicant’s preparation notes

What has Rowan already done?

Before applying, Rowan made notes about three school and community activities. These are experience notes: a way to remember what happened and choose examples for an application. They are not an employer’s form or a list of job requirements.

For each activity, notice the situation, what Rowan did and what happened afterwards. You will use these details to support a claim about a skill. A paid job is not the only place someone can practise a skill.

Rowan’s application notebook

Experiences I can use in an application

School welcome evening

What needed doing?
Visitors needed to sign in. Two student helpers needed to know their roles at the table.
What did I do?
I created a three-step sign-in order and explained each role to the two helpers.
What happened?
The helpers followed the order and the table opened on time.

Club attendance list

What needed doing?
The club attendance list needed to match the permission forms.
What did I do?
I checked the names against the forms, found two missing forms and asked the staff adviser before changing the list.
What happened?
The adviser obtained the missing forms. I then updated the list with verified names.

Community event

What needed doing?
Families arriving at the event needed to find their activity rooms.
What did I do?
I greeted families, gave directions and checked that each person knew where to go.
What happened?
The families could identify where their activities were taking place.

Rowan has not worked at a library or managed confidential library records. Do not add either claim.

You now have both sides of the application: the employer’s duties and Rowan’s experiences. Next, learn how to connect them in a sentence the employer can understand.

Learn how to choose evidence

Show the action behind a skill

A skill is an ability someone uses to do a task. For example, explaining instructions is a communication skill. An employer cannot tell how Rowan communicates from the sentence “I am a good communicator.” That sentence makes a claim but gives no example.

A claim on its own

“I am organized.”

This names a quality. It does not explain what Rowan did or why an employer should believe the claim.

A claim supported by an action

“I created a three-step sign-in order and explained the roles to two helpers.”

The employer can picture the action and ask Rowan how it worked.

That specific example is evidence: information supporting a claim. It must also fit the duty. Explaining sign-in roles is relevant to explaining the library’s sign-in process. Checking an attendance list is more relevant to keeping reservation records accurate.

Use three parts to make the example clear

ContextExplain where you were and what needed doing. The employer was not there, so give enough background.
ActionSay what you personally did. Use verbs such as checked, explained, organized or asked.
ResultExplain what happened afterwards. Use an outcome supplied in the notes; do not invent a success.

A result does not have to be a number or an award. “The table opened on time” is a useful result because it tells the employer the preparation was completed when needed.

Describe the original setting accurately. A school example can show a skill that could be used at work, but it does not become library experience. If an example involves other people’s information, describe the task without including their names or private records.

The demonstration below uses the welcome-evening notes. Watch how the writer chooses one duty, selects the matching action and turns it into a short application response.

Follow the writer’s decisions

Write Rowan’s first application example

We will answer the job notice’s request: “Give a short example of something you have done that matches one of these duties.” We are writing as Rowan, so the finished response uses “I.”

1. Choose one duty to answer

“Welcome visitors and explain the sign-in process.”

From the Prairie Library Hub notice

The useful action here is explain. The library needs someone who can make a process clear to other people. We need an example of Rowan explaining steps, not just an example of Rowan being at an event.

2. Find a matching action in the notes

“I created a three-step sign-in order and explained each role to the two helpers.”

From Rowan’s school welcome evening notes

This is a close match: both the job duty and Rowan’s action involve explaining a sign-in process. We also know the result—the helpers followed the order and the table opened on time. We can use those facts without guessing.

3. Build the response from the supplied facts

Context

At a school welcome evening…

Action

…I created a three-step sign-in order and explained each role to two helpers…

Result

…which helped the table open on time.

Finished application response

Duty: Explain the sign-in process.

“At a school welcome evening, I created a three-step sign-in order and explained each role to two helpers, which helped the table open on time.”

The context tells the employer where this happened. The action shows what Rowan contributed. The result explains why that contribution mattered. Together, the three parts give more useful information than “I am organized.”

4. Check that the claim stays truthful

Compare the finished response with “I am an expert at managing confidential library records.” The notes do not show expert status, a library job or responsibility for confidential records. That version adds facts Rowan cannot support.

The finished response names the school welcome evening and describes only the action and outcome recorded in Rowan’s notes. The employer can decide how that experience relates to the job.

You have seen one duty matched to one experience. Now use the same method for a different duty in the same library notice. The relevant details will be beside the task so you do not have to remember them.

Practise with support

Help Rowan show careful record keeping

This time, you will support “Keep reservation records accurate.” Accurate means correct: the names on a list should agree with the information used to check them. You will choose an experience, plan the three parts and write one or two sentences as Rowan.

1. Choose the experience that fits

Which past action most clearly shows Rowan checking information before updating it? Read the choices, select one, then check your match.

2. Plan with the club attendance notes

The club attendance example is the strongest match because Rowan checked information before changing a record. Use the excerpt below to plan your response. Short notes are enough in these three fields.

Duty from the notice

Keep reservation records accurate.

Facts from Rowan’s notes

Rowan checked the club list against permission forms, found two forms missing and asked the adviser before changing the list. The adviser obtained the forms; Rowan then updated the list with verified names.

3. Write as Rowan

Turn your notes into one or two sentences. Use “I” for Rowan’s actions. Include enough context for an employer who has never heard about the club. Do not claim that the club task was a library job.

4. Compare, then revise

Open the model whenever you need help. Your wording can be different. Compare the facts and explanation rather than trying to copy every word. Your writing is not automatically graded.

One supported response

“For a club attendance list, I checked names against permission forms and asked the adviser about two missing forms. After the forms were obtained, I updated the list with verified names.”

Context“For a club attendance list” identifies the original task. It does not claim library experience.
ActionChecking names and asking the adviser show care with records. The writer states what Rowan did, rather than only saying “I am careful.”
ResultThe list was updated with verified names after the forms were obtained. Asking the adviser is an action; the updated list is the outcome.

Use these questions to revise

  • Can a reader tell which list Rowan was checking?
  • Have I explained the check and the decision to ask before changing the list?
  • Have I included the verified update as the result?
  • Have I removed claims about paid library work or expertise?

Make any changes in your application response above. A different sentence is acceptable when it uses the same supplied facts and shows their connection to accurate records.
