h3  :: Understand a labour-market claim before using it

p ce1-04-foundation-purpose :: When you explore a career, you may read that an occupation is growing, pays well or has few openings nearby. Each statement is a labour-market claim: something a person or publication says about work and workers. You need to understand what the statement describes before using it to make a plan.

p  :: A labour market concerns work and workers: occupations, employment, vacancies and conditions affecting them. A forecast is an estimate about a specified period. A vacancy is an unfilled position; an advertisement is one way it may be publicized. An employment count is the number reported as employed in a defined group, not the number of openings.

p ce1-04-foundation-example :: For example, “many people already work in this occupation” describes employment. “Two jobs were advertised in this town today” describes the results of a particular search. “Employment is expected to grow” describes a forecast. These statements can all be true at the same time. They measure different things; none tells you on its own whether you will get a job.

p ce1-04-foundation-goal :: In this lesson, you will learn to identify what a claim measures, which workers and place it covers, and which time period it describes. You will also name the publisher and explain what the information leaves unanswered. These details help you decide whether a claim is useful for the decision you are making.

p ce1-04-foundation-task :: First, read Learn the method. Then meet Noor and read the two supplied records. Follow the worked comparison, practise with Noor's evidence and answer the short checks. Finally, use a new case for Ellis to write an annotated comparison: brief notes explaining each record, followed by a conclusion about what to investigate next. All required information is provided in the lesson; no live job search, account or outside contact is needed. Practice notes and final responses save separately.

h3  :: How to read a labour-market claim

p ce1-04-method-orientation :: Read this explanation before the case documents. You do not need to write an answer here. Use the distinctions and steps below when you reach Noor's records.

h3  :: First identify the kind of information

p ce1-04-method-measures :: An employment count describes people already working in a stated group. A vacancy count describes unfilled positions. An advertisement count describes the job notices a search found; it may miss vacancies that were not advertised in the places checked. A forecast describes a projected pattern for a stated period. A favourable forecast is a reason to investigate an occupation, not a personal job offer.

h3  :: Attach a label to every number

p  :: NOC means National Occupational Classification. Its number identifies an occupational group used in Canadian statistics. A group can contain several related jobs. Keep the group name beside its number so you know which workers a figure describes.

p ce1-04-method-scope :: The scope is what the information covers: the occupation or group, place and time. An Alberta-wide figure can help you understand a provincial pattern while leaving questions about one town unanswered. Always keep a number's measure and scope together.

p  :: A number without its measure can mislead. “2,470” might mean employed people, advertised vacancies or dollars; those would answer very different questions. Before using a number, identify what, which occupation/group, where and when. Also identify who published it and what the source says it does not cover.

p  :: For example, label an employment figure as “reported employment count; Database analysts and data administrators, NOC 21223; Alberta; displayed in an alis profile read September 28, 2026.” A separate forecast period belongs to the forecast. Do not assume that it also tells you when the employment count was measured. If a record does not give that detail, say it is not stated.

h3  :: Keep three clocks separate

p  :: The source period is the span of time described by the data or forecast. The update date records a revision to a page or section. The retrieval date is when you read it. A forecast labelled 2024–2026 does not become a forecast for 2026–2028 when someone opens it in 2026.

p  :: Different dates on a page are a reason to label information carefully. Do not silently choose the newest date and attach it to every item. For a future real decision, the original source would need checking again. For this lesson, analyze the dated record as supplied.

h3  :: Compare questions before comparing answers

table  :: Record Question it can help answer Question it cannot settle Provincial forecast What pattern was projected for this occupational group and period? Will one person be hired in a particular town? Employment count How many people are reported as employed in the group? How many vacancies are open today? Local advertisement search What advertisements did this search capture on its date? What is every employer planning or hiring privately?

p  :: Both a favourable forecast and a small local search can be accurate. They cover different geography, time and measures. A low advertisement count can reflect a small local market, search terms, sources checked or the search date. These are possible explanations to investigate, not facts the log has already established.

h3  :: Include the cost of living when interpreting wages

p  :: Cost of living means the expense of meeting everyday needs in a place and household situation: for example, housing, food, utilities and transportation. Childcare or other needs may also matter. A higher advertised wage does not automatically leave a person with more money after those costs. Hours, the length of a job and payroll deductions also affect the income available.

p  :: Dated source record — checked September 28, 2026: ALIS advises interpreting salary information alongside location and whether work is temporary. Its moving guide identifies housing availability and costs, transportation and childcare as questions to investigate. Optional salary-context source and optional community-planning source.

p  :: A provincial opportunity label does not tell you whether local work would cover someone's living costs. To compare that, you would need a wage or income figure and local expense information on the same time basis. The case records you will read next do not provide those details. Recognizing that gap is part of interpreting the claim; no extra calculation or outside search is required.

h3  :: Turn uncertainty into a next step

p  :: A conditional conclusion says what the evidence supports now and which condition might change it. “Investigate further within the travel limit” is different from “there will definitely be a job.” It is also different from dismissing the occupation after one search.

p  :: Labour evidence is only part of a career decision. A person's preparation, interest, transport and costs still matter. A source may suggest an opportunity while leaving those personal constraints unanswered. The goal is to organize evidence so a plan can be revised when new information arrives.

p  :: Optional first-step hint: Ask whether a number describes people already working or positions being advertised. If you cannot answer, reread the field heading before calculating anything.
