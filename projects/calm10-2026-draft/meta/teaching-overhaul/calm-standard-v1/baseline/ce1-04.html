Start here

Understand a labour-market claim before using it

When you explore a career, you may read that an occupation is growing, pays well or has few openings nearby. Each statement is a labour-market claim: something a person or publication says about work and workers. You need to understand what the statement describes before using it to make a plan.

A concerns work and workers: occupations, employment, vacancies and conditions affecting them. A is an estimate about a specified period. A is an unfilled position; an advertisement is one way it may be publicized. An employment count is the number reported as employed in a defined group, not the number of openings.

For example, “many people already work in this occupation” describes employment. “Two jobs were advertised in this town today” describes the results of a particular search. “Employment is expected to grow” describes a forecast. These statements can all be true at the same time. They measure different things; none tells you on its own whether you will get a job.

In this lesson, you will learn to identify what a claim measures, which workers and place it covers, and which time period it describes. You will also name the publisher and explain what the information leaves unanswered. These details help you decide whether a claim is useful for the decision you are making.

Learn the method

How to read a labour-market claim

Read this explanation before the case documents. You do not need to write an answer here. Use the distinctions and steps below when you reach Noor's records.

First identify the kind of information

An employment count describes people already working in a stated group. A vacancy count describes unfilled positions. An advertisement count describes the job notices a search found; it may miss vacancies that were not advertised in the places checked. A forecast describes a projected pattern for a stated period. A favourable forecast is a reason to investigate an occupation, not a personal job offer.

Attach a label to every number

NOC means National Occupational Classification. Its number identifies an occupational group used in Canadian statistics. A group can contain several related jobs. Keep the group name beside its number so you know which workers a figure describes.

The is what the information covers: the occupation or group, place and time. An Alberta-wide figure can help you understand a provincial pattern while leaving questions about one town unanswered. Always keep a number's measure and scope together.

A number without its measure can mislead. “2,470” might mean employed people, advertised vacancies or dollars; those would answer very different questions. Before using a number, identify what, which occupation/group, where and when. Also identify who published it and what the source says it does not cover.

For example, label an employment figure as “reported employment count; Database analysts and data administrators, NOC 21223; Alberta; displayed in an alis profile read September 28, 2026.” A separate forecast period belongs to the forecast. Do not assume that it also tells you when the employment count was measured. If a record does not give that detail, say it is not stated.

Keep three clocks separate

The source period is the span of time described by the data or forecast. The update date records a revision to a page or section. The retrieval date is when you read it. A forecast labelled 2024–2026 does not become a forecast for 2026–2028 when someone opens it in 2026.

Different dates on a page are a reason to label information carefully. Do not silently choose the newest date and attach it to every item. For a future real decision, the original source would need checking again. For this lesson, analyze the dated record as supplied.

Compare questions before comparing answers

Record Question it can help answer Question it cannot settle
Provincial forecast What pattern was projected for this occupational group and period? Will one person be hired in a particular town?
Employment count How many people are reported as employed in the group? How many vacancies are open today?
Local advertisement search What advertisements did this search capture on its date? What is every employer planning or hiring privately?

Both a favourable forecast and a small local search can be accurate. They cover different geography, time and measures. A low advertisement count can reflect a small local market, search terms, sources checked or the search date. These are possible explanations to investigate, not facts the log has already established.

Include the cost of living when interpreting wages

Cost of living means the expense of meeting everyday needs in a place and household situation: for example, housing, food, utilities and transportation. Childcare or other needs may also matter. A higher advertised wage does not automatically leave a person with more money after those costs. Hours, the length of a job and payroll deductions also affect the income available.

Dated source record — checked September 28, 2026: ALIS advises interpreting salary information alongside location and whether work is temporary. Its moving guide identifies housing availability and costs, transportation and childcare as questions to investigate. Optional salary-context source and optional community-planning source.

A provincial opportunity label does not tell you whether local work would cover someone's living costs. To compare that, you would need a wage or income figure and local expense information on the same time basis. The case records you will read next do not provide those details. Recognizing that gap is part of interpreting the claim; no extra calculation or outside search is required.

Turn uncertainty into a next step

A conditional conclusion says what the evidence supports now and which condition might change it. “Investigate further within the travel limit” is different from “there will definitely be a job.” It is also different from dismissing the occupation after one search.

Labour evidence is only part of a career decision. A person's preparation, interest, transport and costs still matter. A source may suggest an opportunity while leaving those personal constraints unanswered. The goal is to organize evidence so a plan can be revised when new information arrives.

What does a wage headline mean?

This illustrated video uses Sam and a wage headline as a separate example. Follow how date, region and the meaning of a wage figure change a conclusion. Use the Noor and Ellis records in this lesson for your written responses.

Read the lesson’s written equivalent

A headline is a clue, not a job offer. Start with the question you are actually trying to answer. Sam wants an entry-level job within travelling distance of home.

Document A reports a provincial median of twenty-eight dollars an hour. Median means the middle observation in an ordered set. It does not mean every worker earns that amount. The data include different experience levels, so Sam cannot treat the median as a starting wage.

Document B lists a local assistant job at nineteen dollars an hour. That is useful for this particular opening, but it does not describe the entire occupation. It may also be a different role from the occupation in Document A.

Now compare the dates. The report was published in 2026, but its wage observations are from 2024. A recent publication date does not automatically make every number current.

A careful conclusion is: I have evidence worth investigating, but not a guaranteed job or starting salary. Sam's next step is to check the role, entry requirements and travel, then compare another relevant source.

What is a worked example? An answer that is already completed, with the decisions or calculations explained. As you read, connect the answer to the records; you do not need to copy it.

A forecast, an employment count and a count of advertisements answer different questions. A search log records how and when a search was done. Its results do not describe every employer or explain why an advertisement disappeared.

I first identify the Database Analyst record as Alberta group information. Its Hot label belongs to the stated three-year forecast period. The 2,470 figure is an employment count. Neither tells me how many entry opportunities are inside Noor's travel area.

Noor's Meadowcross search log answers a narrower question: what two employer boards displayed using two search terms on September 24. It found two advertisements. I can preserve that fact without claiming it is the entire local market.

Completed interpretation: “Noor's records do not necessarily conflict. The Database Analyst record is an Alberta forecast/group record for 2024–2026; it is not a count of Meadowcross vacancies or a prediction that Noor will be hired. Noor's Meadowcross search log found two advertisements within the stated search limits on September 24. That small snapshot matters because Noor currently has a 20-kilometre travel limit, but it cannot establish every employer's future plans. I would continue investigating related entry roles and preparation using the supplied record limits. A later, broader search could change the picture. I would not recommend moving or committing to training from these two records alone.”

“There are 2,470 jobs waiting for Noor” changes employment into vacancies. “Two advertisements prove the occupation has no future” generalizes a small snapshot to every place and year. Both fail because their conclusions exceed their records.
