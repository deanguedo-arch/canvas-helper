h3 ce1-01-foundations-title :: What counts as evidence of a strength?

p ce1-01-foundations-purpose :: A career idea becomes more useful when you can explain what led you to it. You will learn to distinguish what someone enjoys, what they have actually done and what they want work to support before reading the experience notebook.

h3 ce1-01-foundation-heading-1 :: Separate interest, skill and value

p ce1-01-foundation-copy-2 :: An interest is something that attracts your attention or that you enjoy. A skill is an ability used to carry out a task. A work value is something important to you about work, such as variety, helping people or predictable hours. Enjoying an activity does not establish how well you can do it; completing it successfully does not establish that you want to do it every day.

p ce1-01-foundation-copy-3 :: A career goal describes a direction for work or learning. A life goal describes something you want your wider life to support. Both matter when exploring a role. A role can use a skill while conflicting with a schedule or other priority.

h3 ce1-01-foundation-heading-4 :: Build the claim from the action

p ce1-01-foundation-copy-5 :: A claim is a statement you want a reader to accept. Evidence is the information supporting it. Look for the situation, a specific action and an observed result. In a simple example, a learner rewrites confusing club directions and two readers then follow them without help. This supports some skill in explaining information. It does not establish that the learner can teach every subject.

p ce1-01-foundation-copy-6 :: Use the sequence recorded action → possible skill → limit → question about work. Keep the person’s contribution separate from a team’s result. A transferable skill may be useful in another setting, while technical training may still be needed.

p ce1-01-foundations-reading :: When you read the case: When you reach the notebook, first find something the person actually did. Keep their reflection about enjoyment and preferred hours separate from the action evidence. You will practise turning those different clues into a cautious direction to investigate.

p  :: Use the method with the case

h3  :: Separate three kinds of clue

p  :: An interest is something that attracts your attention or that you enjoy doing. A skill is an ability used to carry out a task. A work value is something you want work to support, such as a predictable schedule, variety, helping people or time to improve your work. “I enjoy drawing” is an interest. “I rearranged information so two readers could find it” gives evidence of a skill. “I want regular hours” describes a value. None of these clues chooses an occupation on its own.

p  :: A person can enjoy an activity while needing practice. They can also perform a task well and dislike doing it repeatedly. Jordan's successful checklist suggests organization, while the reflection says repeated follow-up was tiring. Record both. Hiding a difficulty would make the career question less useful.

p  :: An interest or strengths inventory organizes a person's answers to a set of questions about themselves. Its results describe what the person reported; they do not independently prove a skill or assign a career. Imagine an inventory suggests “creative work” for Jordan. The poster notebook adds actual evidence, while the schedule preference adds a condition to investigate. If a suggested direction conflicts with experience, keep the conflict as a question rather than accepting the label. No inventory account or survey score is needed here.

h3  :: Make the action visible

p  :: “Jordan is creative” is a claim: a statement you would like a reader to accept. Evidence is the information that supports it. Useful evidence names the setting, the person's action and an observable result. The result need not be an award. Two readers finding the information is a result because it shows what changed after the revision.

p  :: Be precise about whose action it was. Jordan arranged the check-in; the group finished its display. Writing “Jordan did every part” would give Jordan credit for actions the notebook does not report. You can explain a person's contribution to a team result without pretending they worked alone.

h3  :: Build a cautious pathway idea

p  :: A transferable skill can be useful in more than one setting. Organizing information might matter in a poster, a set of instructions or a customer explanation. Transfer does not mean that someone already knows all the technical work in a new role. A school example provides a reason to investigate; it is not proof of professional expertise.

p  :: Use this sequence:

p  :: Recorded action → possible skill → limit of the evidence → question about work.

p  :: For example, “revised a poster after feedback” can support “some skill in making information clearer.” Its limit is one school task. A useful question is whether Jordan enjoys repeated revisions for other people's purposes. That question could change the plan; “Is design good?” is too broad to guide a comparison.

h3  :: Connect the idea to a goal

p  :: A provisional goal is a goal you can revise as evidence develops. It might be “Compare two roles that use visual explanations” rather than “Choose my lifelong occupation today.” Keep a career goal and a life goal visible when deciding what to investigate. A role might use a skill but conflict with a strongly held work value.

p  :: Financial goals can shape the route too. Wanting to keep training costs within a stated budget is different from wanting the highest advertised wage. Later lessons will connect goals to complete cost records. Here you are learning to state the direction and values before assuming a particular occupation, income or training expense will fit.
