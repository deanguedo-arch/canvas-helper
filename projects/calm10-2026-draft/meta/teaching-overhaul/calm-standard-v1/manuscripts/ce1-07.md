**Capacity** — The time and resources actually available after existing commitments.

**Buffer** — Time or money left available for variation or unexpected needs.

**Priority** — Something given preference when resources cannot cover every option.

**Revision** — A specific change to a plan in response to evidence or a changed condition.

Learn the basics

### Learning to revise a plan when circumstances change

A plan for learning or work has to fit alongside the rest of life. When transport, family commitments or available time changes, the original route may stop working. In this lesson, you will learn to revise a plan while checking whether its underlying goal can still be kept.

We will work with Maya, who wants to try design activities while preserving family commitments and rest. Maya originally planned two club sessions with travel. A new commitment overlaps one of those trips. We need to understand the goal and the full time required before choosing an alternative.

First, I will explain the difference between a goal, a route and its timing. We will read Maya’s schedule and the supplied alternatives, then work through a revised plan together. You will try a second revision when transport changes, using the time blocks, feedback and comparison model to check your reasoning.

Your final response uses Arun’s new schedule and a task that takes 75 minutes. You will place the full task in an available interval, explain what changed and identify a review date and trigger. All access and timing information is supplied; no meeting or outside response is needed. Your practice and final notes save separately. Let’s learn how to find the part of a plan that needs to change.

### Identify which part has changed

A goal is what you want to accomplish. A route is the way you will work toward it. Timing places that route into a day or week. Wanting to try a design task is a goal; attending a club is one route; Tuesday afternoon is its scheduled time. A new commitment may change the time or route without telling us that the interest itself has vanished.

A constraint is a limit the plan must respect. Capacity is the time or resources actually available. In our cases, fixed commitments cannot move and only the listed flexible blocks can be reassigned. This makes the planning question specific: which supplied option fits? We cannot repair the chart by inventing spare time elsewhere or counting the same interval twice.

### Count the full activity, not just its title

A one-hour session can need two hours if it includes travel in both directions. At home, an activity may still need reading, drafting and transition time. Those parts need a continuous block large enough for the stated work. Adding scattered spare minutes is not enough when the task or journey needs an uninterrupted period.

A buffer is time left unassigned to allow for variation and transition. Recovery and existing commitments also need to stay visible. A chart that fits only by removing every break or assuming every step takes exactly its shortest possible time creates another planning problem. We will use the protected rest blocks stated in the cases.

A revision note records the old assumption, the new evidence, the revised route or timing and when it will be reviewed. A checkpoint is a planned review moment; a trigger is a change that makes an earlier review useful. Naming both helps a plan respond to evidence rather than leaving “review later” as an empty intention.

These ideas will help us examine Maya’s actual intervals. We will preserve the commitments that cannot move, count the whole activity and test only alternatives the supplied notices confirm.

Use the method with the case

### Trace the conflict before choosing a replacement

The old plan assumes Maya can leave home at 15:30 and return at 17:30 on Tuesday. The family commitment occupies that same interval. The problem is therefore the availability of the route, including travel, rather than the one-hour session considered alone. Naming the changed assumption tells us what a replacement must solve.

Maya’s design-learning goal remains in the record. A changed circumstance does not automatically cancel it, although later experience could lead Maya to revise the goal itself. For now, we should test a supplied route that keeps the goal possible without pretending two simultaneous commitments can both be completed.

### Find a block that holds all the work

The written package needs 40 minutes for the example and 20 for a draft: 60 minutes altogether. Its notice confirms that it can be used at home and that Maya has the access needed. Those are important facts. We cannot assume an online alternative exists merely because it would be convenient.

The available Saturday hour is continuous and at home. The following 30 minutes are unassigned transition or buffer time, while the afternoon rest is protected. Checking each interval ensures the proposed work fits the actual week. It does not mean every spare minute needs to become another task.

### Choose a digital habit that supports the task

A calendar or reminder can support the plan, but the scheduled hour still needs to contain the work. Repeatedly checking messages could leave less time for the example or draft. A manageable habit might be keeping the task document visible, closing nonessential messages or setting a reminder at the change from one part to the next.

A habit supports available capacity; it does not create time that the schedule lacks. If the task really takes longer, the route or scope needs to be reviewed. Fictional labels are sufficient for this course’s planning notes, so there is no need to publish family details or put personal routines in a public calendar.

### Use overload as information for revision

If the plan fits only by consuming protected rest, sleep or fixed commitments, the apparent solution hides another conflict. A reasonable response is to change conflicting demands, use a confirmed alternative or reduce the scope where allowed. The case’s written package is a specific support, not a requirement for Maya to solve every barrier through effort alone.

Support can also be clear information or an accessible format. Here it comes from the notices, worked timing and comparison model. You may identify a question for future planning, but a real reply is not needed to complete the supplied task. Keep an uncertainty as a question rather than write an imagined permission into the plan.

### Say when another look would be useful

A review date gives the plan a place on the calendar. A trigger gives a reason to revisit it sooner or again: changed transport, missing materials or evidence that the work takes longer than expected. The revision note should name what would change after the review, not simply promise to try harder.

We will now combine these checks in Maya’s Tuesday replacement. As you read, look for the preserved goal, the changed route, the full work block and a review that can test the plan against what happens. That is how a revision becomes a useful response to evidence.
