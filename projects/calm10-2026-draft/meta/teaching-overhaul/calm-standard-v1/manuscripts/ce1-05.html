Learn the basics

Learning to build an Alberta high-school plan that keeps options open

Course planning connects what you do in high school with choices you may want after graduation. A plan needs to do two jobs: meet Alberta’s graduation requirements and prepare you for the particular next step you are considering. In this lesson, you will learn how to check those jobs separately and arrange preparation in a realistic order.

We will plan with Tariq, who is considering visual design while keeping an information-services direction open. Tariq has already completed some Grade 10 courses, has limited space for electives and wants to protect other commitments. The question is how to keep both directions possible within the time and money actually available.

First, we will establish the Alberta graduation and course-planning basics. Then we will read Tariq’s school and program notices. I will explain a first plan, showing why each course belongs in its term. You will revise that plan when one offering is cancelled, with checks, a hint and a comparison model for support.

Your final task introduces Nessa, whose proposed plan has a different elective conflict. You will write a Grade 10–12 route, explain its goals and costs, and give two checkpoints with useful verification questions. All the case information is supplied. You are practising planning; you do not need to contact a school or change your own course registration to finish. Let’s begin with what an Alberta course plan must account for.

Separate graduation from preparation for a particular program

Credits are the units used to count completed high-school learning. For the Alberta High School Diploma in the English-language program, plan for at least 100 credits and the course categories. The standard requirements include English Language Arts 30-1 or 30-2, Social Studies 30-1 or 30-2, an eligible mathematics course at the 20 level and an eligible science course at the 20 level. Physical Education 10 and CALM each require at least three credits. There are also ten credits in eligible complementary courses and ten credits in eligible 30-level courses beyond the required English and Social Studies. These category checks matter: a total of 100 by itself is not enough.

Now consider a program after high school. It may name a particular English or mathematics course, a minimum mark or a portfolio even when your diploma requirements are met. For example, English Language Arts 30-1 and 30-2 can both satisfy the diploma’s English requirement; a particular program may accept one or both. Read that program’s stated conditions before choosing. Completing the preparation permits consideration; it does not guarantee selection.

Read the course names and count the actual credits

In Alberta course names, 10, 20 and 30 usually identify successive high-school course levels. The -1 and -2 labels identify different sequences, rather than the number of credits. Tariq’s practice plan uses English Language Arts 10-1 → 20-1 → 30-1. We use that sequence because it matches the supplied program condition, not because every learner or every program needs the -1 sequence.

Art 10 → Art 20 → Art 30 gives us a real example of a . The Art courses used here carry five credits each. Career and Technology Studies, or CTS, courses are different: an individual module such as INF1050: Database 1 carries one credit. Schools can group several CTS modules into a larger offering. A one-credit module does not become five credits just because it appears in one timetable slot.

Start with the conditions at the destination

A prerequisite is a step that must be completed before another course or entry point. preparation is a stated condition; preparation may help without being compulsory. If a program requires two pieces of work, a particular school course may be one way to create them. We need to distinguish the evidence the program asks for from the route used to produce it.

A portfolio is a selected collection of work with explanations of what it demonstrates. Some programs set a submission deadline for that evidence as well as a later date for completing a school course. Those are different points on the timeline. Applying before final school results are available can fit the supplied conditions if the record explicitly allows completion later. It still does not guarantee admission.

Join the deadline to the starting point

Working backwards identifies what must be ready by the deadline and which earlier steps it needs. Working forwards checks what the learner has completed and when the next course is actually offered. Imagine a course requires an introductory course first: putting both names in the same term would not make the prerequisite complete. The two directions of reading need to meet in one workable sequence.

Capacity means the space and resources available for the plan. A course can be offered and useful but still conflict with another elective or exceed the additional study time available. Money matters as well. We will count the listed material charges and state what they cover, rather than assume a school exercise supplies every future tuition or living cost.

A is a planned moment to review progress. A trigger is a change that makes revision necessary, such as a cancelled course. Leaving some room in the plan can make a later revision easier. A plan is therefore more than a timetable: it explains why the steps fit and when we will check whether those assumptions still hold.

Now we can use the program conditions and school offerings together. The case focuses on selected pathway choices inside an otherwise reserved timetable; it is not a complete graduation-credit audit. We will keep that boundary visible while learning the planning method.

Use the method with the case

Find the latest point at which evidence must be ready

Program A’s portfolio is due April 15 of Grade 12. That means two original pieces need to exist by then. Art 30 is a supplied way to develop them, finishing by March 30 of its year. I compare those dates first because a course finishing after the submission date would not support this route, however relevant its content might be.

Now follow Art 30’s prerequisite backwards. It needs Art 20, which needs the Art 10 already completed. The school offers Art 20 in fall and Art 30 in winter, so the sequence can move forward from Tariq’s existing preparation. We have connected a destination deadline to an available chain rather than simply selecting appealing course names.

Keep the shared preparation visible

The communication chain runs from the completed English Language Arts 10-1 to English Language Arts 20-1 in Grade 11 core and English Language Arts 30-1 in Grade 12 core. These are already reserved if the prerequisites are completed. They do not compete for the elective slot. English Language Arts 20-1 contributes to Program B’s preparation, while English Language Arts 30-1 meets the later condition named by Program A.

Program B also requires INF1050: Database 1, which has an available place in the school table. It is a real condition within this fictional case, not a vague backup label. To keep B possible, the plan needs to show where that course will be completed and retain the May 1 application deadline.

Check whether each term has enough capacity

Every additional elective needs its own available slot. Art 30 takes three extra weekly hours; INF1050: Database 1 takes two. Putting them together would use two slots and five hours, beyond both limits. A workable sequence separates those demands even when both courses are useful.

The listed materials are $40 for Art 20, $35 for Art 30 and $20 for Database 1. Adding them once each gives $95. Tariq has a $100 material limit, leaving $5 in that category. This is an elective-material calculation. It does not establish the cost of post-secondary study or provide money for an expense omitted from the record.

Decide what a should check

A good has an object: the completed pieces, a prerequisite result, continued interest or a deadline. “Review at the end of Grade 11” becomes useful when the plan says what will be reviewed then and why the result could change the next step. A trigger, such as cancellation, may require a review before the scheduled date.

Support questions should identify an uncertainty that matters. Asking which formats are accepted for the two portfolio pieces is more useful than asking whether everything is fine.
