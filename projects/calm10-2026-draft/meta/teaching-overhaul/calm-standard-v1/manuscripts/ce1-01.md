Learn the basics

### Learning to recognize a strength you can explain

Choosing a career direction can start with ordinary things you have done at school or in your community. The difficult part is knowing what those experiences actually tell you. In this lesson, you will learn to explain a possible strength using an action and its result, then connect that strength to what a person wants from work.

We will begin with Jordan, who enjoys making information clear. A friend has suggested graphic design after seeing Jordan’s school poster. Jordan wants to know whether that suggestion fits both the work already done and the wish for regular time outside work. You do not have to decide Jordan’s future. We are learning how to find a sensible direction to investigate.

First, I will explain the difference between an interest, a demonstrated skill and a work value. Then we will read Jordan’s notes together. I will build a short evidence card from the poster experience; you will try the same reasoning with a science display, using checks and a comparison model to improve your answer.

Finally, you will write a strengths-and-goals response for Amina, a new person whose experiences are supplied later. Your three fields will explain two experiences, connect them to possible directions and give questions worth investigating. Your practice saves separately from that final response. You can pause, return to the notes and revise before the four completion checks. Let’s learn what each kind of clue can tell us.

### Look for three different clues

An interest is something you enjoy or want to explore. A skill is an ability you can demonstrate through an action. A work value is something that matters to you about working, such as having variety or knowing when the day will finish. These clues belong together in a career decision, but each answers a different question. “I like drawing” does not show how well I explain an idea with a drawing.

A career goal describes a direction for work or learning. A life goal describes what you want your wider life to support. Someone might want to investigate creative work and also keep regular time for activities outside work. A promising direction should be examined against both goals. We are looking for a next step that makes sense, rather than asking a short school experience to decide an entire future.

### Start with something that actually happened

Suppose a learner rewrites confusing club directions and two readers can then follow them without help. The rewritten directions are an action; the readers managing without help are a result. Together, they provide evidence for some skill in explaining information. Saying “an excellent communicator” gives a label without showing a reader why it fits. Naming the action and result makes the claim open to examination.

The size of the claim matters too. One set of successful directions does not establish that the learner can explain every subject to every audience. That limit leaves room for learning while preserving the useful evidence. Our method will be to describe an action and result, identify a possible skill, explain the limit, then ask a question about work that could use the skill.

Keep these distinctions beside you as we meet Jordan. The notebook provides actions and results; the reflection provides preferences and goals. We will read each for the information it can actually supply.

Use the method with the case

### Read what Jordan did, then read what Jordan wants

We now have more than a friend’s opinion. In the poster row, two classmates could not find essential information. Jordan changed the layout and asked them to check it. That sequence lets us connect a specific action to a reported result. The reflection adds that Jordan enjoyed deciding which information came first and improving the draft after feedback. Enjoyment gives us a reason to keep exploring; the action gives us evidence to examine.

The science display adds a complication worth keeping. Jordan made a checklist and arranged a check-in, but found repeated follow-up tiring. A person can be able to organize a task and still dislike part of the work. If we record only the successful group result, we lose a useful question about how often a future role would require chasing incomplete work.

The schedule note answers a third question. Jordan wants reasonably predictable hours and regular time outside work. This is a work value connected to a life goal. It is not a claim that Jordan has already organized a workplace or that graphic design provides predictable hours. We will use it to ask about conditions in work that might use Jordan’s strengths.

### Give the reader enough detail to judge the claim

A claim is a statement you want someone to accept. Before writing “Jordan has some skill in organizing visual information,” ask what the reader could check. The problem, layout change and classmates’ response give that statement a basis. The result need not be a prize or a grade: readers finding the information is useful evidence of what changed.

Ownership of the action matters. Jordan organized the display check-in; the group found and completed the missing caption. “Jordan completed everyone’s work” would add a contribution the notebook never reports. Describe Jordan’s part in the team result. This keeps a truthful claim useful without making the person responsible for everything the team did.

### Use an inventory as a starting suggestion

An interest or strengths inventory organizes a person’s answers to questions about themselves. If it suggests creative work for Jordan, it tells us something about the reported answers. It does not independently establish professional skill. We can test the suggestion against the poster evidence and ask whether the working conditions would fit Jordan’s goals. A disagreement between an inventory and experience becomes a question to investigate. No inventory account is needed here.

### Carry a skill into a new question

A transferable skill is one that may be useful in another setting. Organizing a poster could provide a starting point for organizing instructions or explaining information to a customer. It does not establish knowledge of professional software, client budgets or workplace deadlines. The next question should connect the action to work without skipping the learning still needed.

For Jordan, “How often does visual-communication work involve urgent revisions?” is useful because the answer could affect the schedule preference. “Is design good?” would give us much less direction. A provisional goal is a direction that can change as evidence develops. Jordan can investigate roles using visual explanations while keeping the possibility of changing the plan.

Financial goals can become part of that investigation: for example, keeping later training costs within a budget. This lesson does not supply a training price or wage, so we cannot decide affordability yet. We are establishing a direction and the questions that later evidence would need to answer. Next, use the inventory check to test that distinction, then watch or read how values can be influenced.
