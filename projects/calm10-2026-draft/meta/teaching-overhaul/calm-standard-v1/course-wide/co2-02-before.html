Learn the basics

Recognize a hazard before choosing a response

A workplace request does not supply the knowledge needed to carry it out safely. Before reading the case, learn to separate an observed condition, a possible harm and the information needed to respond.

Describe a hazard without creating exposure

A hazard is something with the potential to cause harm. concerns the possibility and seriousness of that harm in the circumstances. A is a measure used to prevent or reduce exposure. An unreadable product label is an observable information gap; it does not identify the substance.

Observation does not require touching, testing or deliberately smelling something unsafe. “The label cannot be read” reports a fact. “Handling it without instructions could cause harm” states the concern. A claim about its exact chemical identity would need evidence the observer may not have.

Choose the immediate safe action first

Use identify → pause and avoid guessing → report and request information or → reassess using the responsible assessment and required controls. The first response can stop exposure while a competent person decides the final control. Do not select protective equipment for an unknown hazard by guessing.

The right to know connects to hazard information and instruction; the right to participate connects to reporting and safety assessment. Dangerous-work refusal has an applicable threshold and process; the dated Alberta record in this lesson explains them. A concern should be communicated through that process rather than treated as permission to resume on appearance alone.

For a simple example, unfamiliar equipment with a missing instruction is a reason to stay clear, report the gap and ask for approved training—not to experiment to prove whether it is dangerous.

When you read the case: Read the observation, training information and official-process record before deciding what the worker can do. The first check asks which sentence reports the evidence accurately. The four-step image and the later teaching will connect that observation to a response.

On a first shift, you may want to show that you can handle a task without slowing anyone down. But a request does not supply the information, equipment or needed to do it safely. Knowing when to pause is a useful workplace skill.

Vocabulary. A hazard is a source or condition that could cause harm. concerns how harm could occur and how serious or likely it might be. A is a measure that removes or reduces exposure to a hazard. A procedure is an agreed way to do a task safely. A concern report states what was observed and what action is needed. Reporting does not require proving a chemical's identity or a legal violation.

Meet the situation and read the documents

Devin is a new employee at fictional Brookside Community Supplies in Alberta. At 4:10 p.m. on October 6, 2026, supervisor Jo asks Devin to clean a work surface beside a storage area. Devin has not started. The site is provincially regulated.

Document A — Observation and training record

What the record establishes What it does not establish
Jo pointed to a container of liquid with no readable label. Its identity, concentration or hazard classification.
The room feels stuffy; Devin does not know whether the ventilation is operating adequately for this product. A measured exposure level or proof that the air is toxic.
Devin has not received the product information, chemical-use training or procedure for this cleaning task. Which gloves, ventilation or other controls would be suitable.
Jo is helping another worker in the front area. Devin can reach Jo from the clear doorway without handling the container. Permission to improvise while Jo is busy.
Devin has not opened, carried, mixed, smelled deliberately or used the liquid. No injury or visible spill is reported in this written case. Any reason to perform spill cleanup.

Document B — Supplied worksite instructions

If an assigned task lacks safety information or training, pause it and tell supervisor Jo. Stay out of the affected area when its safety is uncertain. From a safe place, warn another person who may enter; do not take an untrained action to block, move or clean a hazard. Jo will arrange a competent person's assessment and controls. If immediate serious danger develops, move to safety and follow the site's emergency procedure. Devin's orientation identifies the clear front exit and the outside assembly point. This case involves no current injury or emergency alarm.

These are fictional site instructions for the scenario. They do not provide a method for handling any real chemical.

Document C — In-course rights and responsibilities record

Government of Alberta, “Young worker rights and responsibilities,” sections “Workplace safety,” “Job training,” and “Emergency training and equipment”; “Employer responsibilities,” “Overview” and “Employing youth.” Checked September 28, 2026.

Workers have rights to hazard information, participation in safety and refusal of dangerous work. Workers should report unsafe conditions, follow safe procedures and ask for training they lack. Employers must provide the skills, information, competent supervision and safety arrangements needed for the work. Learners still developing the necessary skills require direct supervision by someone who has them. Chemical work requires special training; equipment alone does not replace instruction.

Government of Alberta, “Refuse dangerous work,” “How it works” and “Worker obligations.” Checked September 28, 2026.

At provincially regulated worksites, the refusal right concerns a reasonable belief in an undue hazard: a serious, immediate threat actually observed or experienced. A worker should stop the dangerous work and promptly report what they refuse and why. The employer investigates and addresses it. Unless fixed immediately, an investigation report is provided. Safe alternative work the person can reasonably do may be assigned. Unresolved danger can be taken to Occupational Health and Safety (OHS). Exercising the legal right is protected from reprisal.

This record explains a process; it does not decide that every uncertainty automatically meets the legal threshold. A worker can ask for missing information and training before reaching a legal conclusion.

Use the method with the case

Describe what you know without testing the hazard

Observation is useful when it makes the concern concrete: a label cannot be read, training has not occurred, or insulation is visibly damaged. You do not need to get closer, touch an object or deliberately smell a substance to make your report more convincing. Information gathering must not create the exposure you are trying to avoid.

Separate the observation from the possible harm. “The product is unidentified” is established. “Using an unidentified product without instructions could expose someone to harm” explains the concern. “This is definitely a poisonous gas” makes a claim the documents cannot support. Cautious wording can be specific and urgent without exaggeration.

Use rights as practical actions

The right to know connects to requesting the identity, hazard information, training and procedure. The right to participate connects to reporting the condition and contributing information during its assessment. The right to refuse dangerous work has an Alberta process and threshold, described in Document C. It is more than walking away silently: the employer needs to know which work is being refused and why.

Worker and employer responsibilities fit together. Devin must report and avoid unsafe improvisation. Jo and the employer must arrange appropriate information, assessment, supervision and controls. Devin does not become the chemical expert merely because Devin noticed a problem.

Choose the immediate action before the final fix

An immediate action keeps exposure from increasing while a competent person works out the fix. For Devin, that means not handling the liquid, staying clear and reaching Jo from the doorway. The final control depends on information Devin does not have. It might involve a verified product, a different task or another trained worker; this lesson cannot select gloves or chemical procedures for an unknown substance.

Use the four-step image as a reminder, with this complete text equivalent:

  1. Identify the potential hazard: name the observable condition and the information gap.
  2. Pause and avoid guessing: do not handle or test the hazard; stay clear when needed.
  3. Ask the responsible person: report promptly and request information, training and controls.
  4. Reassess the remaining risk: verify the responsible assessment and safe procedure; report unresolved danger and use the applicable refusal process where warranted.

“Reassess” does not mean giving yourself permission because the room looks normal. Work can resume only when the hazard has been addressed and the worker has the competence and controls required for the assigned work. Replacing Devin with another equally untrained person merely transfers the concern.

Close the loop

A useful handoff identifies who will act, what must be checked and how the worker will learn the result. “Someone will sort it out” does not tell the next shift what is unresolved. In a formal refusal, read the employer's investigation report and raise remaining concerns through the process. OHS is the external safety route; Employment Standards deals with matters such as wages and employment hours.

Read the lesson’s written equivalent

Slide 1: The first shift

A request is not the same as safe information. UNLABELLED LIQUID; POOR VENTILATION; SUPERVISOR BUSY. What is unknown?

A first shift is a time to ask, not guess. Devin is asked to use a cleaning liquid beside a storage area with poor ventilation. There is no readable label, and the supervisor is helping another worker. A coworker says, “It is probably the usual cleaner.” That description is not enough to identify the substance or its safe use. Pause the task. We will separate what Devin observed from what still needs confirmation.

Slide 2: Name the hazard and the gap

Describe evidence before deciding what the substance is. OBSERVED: container has no readable label; CONDITION: air feels stale; ventilation uncertain; UNKNOWN: contents, exposure risk, required controls. Observed fact ≠ confirmed identity

A hazard is a source or condition that could cause harm. Here, the unlabeled container creates uncertainty; the ventilation condition may affect exposure. Devin should not infer the chemical from its colour, smell, or a coworker’s guess. The missing information includes the product identity, its instructions, possible exposure routes, and the site procedure. This is a case about a missing safety control, not about diagnosing a chemical or deciding whether a law was broken.

Slide 3: Use the three worker rights

Know • Participate • Refuse dangerous work KNOW: ask for hazard information and training; PARTICIPATE: report the concern and suggest a control; REFUSE: follow the applicable process if danger remains. Rights guide a safe process

Canadian workplace safety guidance describes three worker rights: to know about hazards, to participate in health and safety decisions, and to refuse work that may endanger someone. Devin can request the product label, the relevant safety information, instruction, and supervision. Devin can report the concern and take part in choosing a safer method. If the hazard remains, the worker follows the applicable refusal process; it is not a dare or an invitation to confront someone alone. Details belong to the current Alberta process and workplace.

Slide 4: Choose an immediate control

Reduce exposure before collecting more detail. 1 STOP the task; 2 KEEP CLEAR if safe; 3 TELL the supervisor; 4 WAIT for trained direction. Never smell • mix • pour • test

The safe immediate move is to stop before handling the container. If it is safe, Devin should keep other people away from the area and alert the supervisor or designated trained person. Devin should not smell the liquid, mix it with another cleaner, pour it down a drain, or carry it to find a label. Those actions can create exposure or a reaction. If there is immediate serious danger, move to safety and follow the site emergency procedure.

Slide 5: Pause: make the decision

Name the hazard, missing evidence, and next safe action. What is observed?; What must be confirmed?; What should Devin do now?. Pause the video and write a 1–2 sentence response.

Before Devin acts: name the hazard, the missing information, and the safest next step. Pause here and decide.

Slide 6: Worked response and follow-up

State facts, ask for controls, and close the loop. DO NOT handle the unknown liquid; Ask for identity, information, training, and procedure; Record the concern through the site process. A good response is specific—not dramatic.

A strong response says: the container has no readable label, ventilation is uncertain, and Devin has not received the safe procedure. Devin pauses, keeps clear if safe, alerts the supervisor, and asks for the identity, hazard information, training, and approved control before using it. The misconception is that a familiar-looking product or coworker’s confidence makes handling safe. It does not. After the hazard is addressed, Devin records the outcome and follows up. For an actual workplace, check current Alberta guidance and site instructions; this example does not decide a legal case. Before closing, Devin checks who will identify the liquid and what training is needed before anyone resumes the task. If no trained person is available, the area remains controlled and the concern is escalated through the site process. The follow-up is not “clean it up later”; it is to confirm that a responsible person has identified the product, selected controls, and explained how to work safely. This makes the handoff concrete and prevents the unknown hazard from becoming the next shift’s problem.

What is a worked example? An answer that is already completed, with the decisions or calculations explained. As you read, connect the answer to the records; you do not need to copy it.

An observation is something the record directly reports. A possible harm explains why it matters without pretending an unknown substance is identified. A control reduces the hazard or exposure; a co-worker’s guess does not verify it.

I begin with Devin's actual position: the work has not started, and Jo is reachable from a clear doorway. That makes it possible to pause and report without handling the container. I do not invent a spill because a general safety illustration happens to show one.

My observation is the unreadable label and missing training. My information request follows from those gaps: product identity, hazard information, approved procedure and appropriate instruction. “Give me any gloves” would jump to equipment before anyone has identified what the equipment needs to protect against.

Here is Devin's complete report and follow-up plan:

Jo, at 4:10 p.m. I found that the cleaning container beside the storage area has no readable label. I have not been given the product information or training for this task, and I cannot confirm that the ventilation is suitable. I have not handled the liquid. I am pausing the cleaning and staying at the clear doorway. Please arrange a competent person's assessment and explain the identified product, safe procedure, controls and training before assigning this task. I can warn someone approaching from this safe position and do another safe task that I am trained to do. I will record who takes responsibility and ask for the assessment outcome. If serious danger remains, I will promptly state the dangerous work I am refusing and follow the Alberta process, including OHS help if unresolved.

This uses the right to know and participate immediately. It also identifies the refusal route without pretending a classroom paragraph has already made a legal finding. “Use a small amount to see whether it works” is an unsafe alternative because the proposed test itself could cause exposure.

A new hazard: the damaged vacuum cord


Imani is a new employee in fictional Alder Display Shop, an Alberta provincially regulated workplace. Create a safe-response handoff using the following complete records. Do not perform a real inspection, repair or test.


Document D — Observation, October 9, 2026, 5:20 p.m. Imani is asked to vacuum a dry sales-floor area. The vacuum is switched off and already unplugged. Before touching it, Imani sees a split in the cord insulation with metal visible. No one has been shocked; there is no fire, smoke, water or immediate injury. Imani stands in a clear area and has not moved, plugged in or tested the machine. Supervisor Ren is at the nearby service desk and can be reached safely.


Document E — Training and instructions. Imani has ordinary vacuum-use instruction, but no electrical repair training or authority to repair equipment. The shop's written procedure says: “Do not use damaged electrical equipment. Report the equipment and location to Ren; Ren arranges removal from service and assessment by a competent person. Workers must not improvise repairs. A replacement may be used only after its condition and safe use are confirmed.” A coworker says, “Wrap it in tape and finish quickly.” No replacement has yet been supplied.




Success means a specific report, no testing or improvised repair, appropriate responsibility for controls, and a follow-up that does not silently pass damaged equipment to another worker. You may describe a safe replacement or another safe task; do not assume either has already been supplied.
