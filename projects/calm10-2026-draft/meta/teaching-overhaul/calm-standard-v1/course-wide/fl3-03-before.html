Learn the basics

Separate the account from the investment inside it

An and a holding describe different parts of an investment decision. Learn both layers before reading the options.

Read the two layers

An account arrangement supplies rules around eligible holdings. A holding is where the money is placed. A TFSA is an account arrangement with tax rules; a GIC is a holding with stated repayment and interest conditions. “A GIC held in a TFSA” names both layers.

An account name does not supply a holding’s rate, guarantee its value or make locked money accessible. The supplied case states the account eligibility and room needed here; no personal tax information is required.

Compare the holding with the goal

Liquidity describes how readily value becomes available money. Risk includes possible loss or failure to meet the goal. Return includes gains, losses and income, after relevant costs. A fee reduces what is kept. Read how and when money can be withdrawn or sold.

A holding saleable on a deadline may still have an uncertain sale value. A holding with a stated repayment date may not be accessible earlier. First identify when money is needed and what happens if it is worth less or unavailable.

Use goal and date → holding’s access, risk, return and fees → account rules → choice and remaining uncertainty. A willingness to take risk is different from having enough resources to bear a loss.

When you read the case: Identify which record names an account arrangement and which names a holding before the first check. Later you will use the goal’s deadline and the supplied access terms to evaluate a combination of the two.

Arrangement Purpose and tax distinction
TFSA Contributions are not deductible; eligible growth and withdrawals are generally tax-free, subject to the rules and available room.
Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) Retirement-oriented saving; deductible contributions within limits may reduce taxable income; ordinary withdrawals are generally taxable.

Use the method with the case

Read the two layers

Think of the account as a set of rules around eligible holdings. The holding is what the money is actually placed in. “A GIC held in a TFSA” names both layers. “A TFSA earns 5%” is incomplete unless a specific holding or offer provides that rate. The account label does not guarantee a return or prevent a market loss.

Choose using the goal's deadline and the consequences of loss first. Then examine the account rules. A tax advantage cannot make locked money accessible before its contractual date. Nor does a familiar product name establish that all versions have identical terms.

Compare access, risk, return, fees and knowledge

Liquidity describes how readily a holding can become available money, considering time and cost. Noor's GIC cannot be redeemed at six months under its supplied contract. The fund can be sold, but that does not guarantee its value at sale or instant access. A saleable asset and a stable balance are different features.

Risk includes the possibility of loss or of failing to meet the goal. Return includes gains or losses and income, after relevant costs. Fees reduce what the investor keeps. The matters, but a long horizon is not a guarantee of profit. Risk tolerance concerns willingness to accept uncertainty; the ability to bear a loss also matters. Noor cannot make the fee payment if this $500 is lost, even if Noor says market swings feel exciting.

Before using a product, a person should be able to explain what it holds, how money is accessed, what can cause loss and what fees apply. If that knowledge is missing, pausing to investigate is an informed action.

Recognise common holdings

A deposit records money held with an institution under its account terms. A GIC commits money under specified repayment and interest conditions. A bond represents lending to an issuer; issuer failure and changes in resale price can create risk. A share represents an ownership interest in a company. A fund pools investments; its actual holdings determine how diversified and risky it is. spreads exposure, but does not eliminate losses.

Crypto assets are digital assets with potentially substantial price, platform and access risks. A token's popularity or a platform's registration does not guarantee its value. The Alberta Securities Commission's CheckFirst material identifies potential loss and liquidity risks. No crypto purchase is required here, and no advertised return is treated as evidence of suitability.

What is a worked example? An answer that is already completed, with the decisions or calculations explained. As you read, connect the answer to the records; you do not need to copy it.

An account arrangement sets rules for holding money or assets. A holding is the deposit, fund or other asset inside it. Access, fees and uncertain value belong to the actual product’s terms; an account name alone does not settle them.

Noor needs the money after six months. S permits access then. G's higher full-year rate is irrelevant to that access problem: its contract does not allow early withdrawal. F could be worth less at the deadline, which Noor cannot absorb for this goal.

For S, the illustrative six-month interest is $500 × 0.02 × 6/12 = $5, giving $505 before any tax applicable to the chosen arrangement. Its terms specify this simple calculation. Do not substitute G's full-year $25 interest into a six-month comparison.

Complete comparison: “I would use S for the course-fee money because its supplied terms preserve access and avoid market-price loss before the six-month deadline. If held in the case TFSA, the $500 contribution fits Noor's confirmed $1,000 room; S is the holding and TFSA is the arrangement. G locks the money too long, while F exposes the required fee money to uncertain value. The 2% rate and account room are supplied case facts, not promises for another person.”

This conclusion uses both product and account evidence. Simply writing “TFSA is safest” would fail to identify what is held or what could happen to it.
