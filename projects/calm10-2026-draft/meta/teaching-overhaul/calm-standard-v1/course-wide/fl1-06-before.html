Learn the basics

Separate starting cash from ongoing cost

A move or purchase needs more than an advertised price. This lesson teaches how to identify money due at the start, costs that repeat, money potentially returned and amounts not yet known.

Give each payment a category

An upfront cost is due at the beginning. A recurring cost repeats over the use period. A refundable deposit is paid now and may be returned under stated conditions. It reduces cash available now even when a return is expected later.

For a simple example, $300 first rent and a $300 deposit require $600 initially. If the deposit is later fully returned, that return changes the lasting cost; it does not reduce the $600 needed on the first date. Count the first rent once when calculating a period that already includes it.

Keep known and unknown costs visible

An or use cost helps keep an arrangement usable: for example, maintenance, utilities, storage or insurance where relevant. An needs a basis and time period. A missing amount is unknown, not zero.

First calculate the known payments due by the start date and compare them with accessible money. Then calculate repeated commitments over the stated period, keeping deposit-return assumptions and irregular costs visible. A known minimum exceeding the budget already establishes a gap; a minimum that fits does not establish that every unknown will fit.

Use start-date cash → money remaining → recurring commitments → irregular or unknown costs → feasible change or specific question.

When you read the case: Read the deposit condition before categorizing it. The first check asks how it affects move-in money. The later comparison uses both starting cash and the stated ownership or ongoing costs without inventing missing figures.

Use the method with the case

Sort costs by what happens to the money

An upfront cost is due at the beginning. A recurring cost repeats, such as a monthly transit pass. A refundable deposit ties up money now with a possible later return under specified conditions. These categories can overlap: the first monthly payment is both an upfront cash requirement and part of an ongoing cost. Count it once in the period being analysed.

An ownership or use cost is something needed to keep using the purchase or arrangement: maintenance, storage, utilities, insurance or interest, depending on the situation. A vehicle's advertised price, for example, does not include every future insurance, maintenance and operating expense. The relevant amounts require quotes and a time period; guessing that they are zero hides rather than resolves the .

Use two views. Cash needed by the start date includes every payment due then, including a deposit. Cost over the use period separates money expected back from money used up, and states return assumptions. A deposit can affect affordability even when it is not a permanent expense.

Give an unknown its own line

An unknown is a missing figure, not a zero. A known minimum is the total of amounts already established. If the known minimum exceeds available money, you can identify a problem without knowing every extra cost. If it fits, you still cannot call the plan fully affordable until material unknowns are resolved or a justified allowance is supplied.

A quote should state what it includes, how long it applies and when payment is due. “Utilities around something small” is not a quote. “$65 for this month's supplied utility package, due September 1” is usable in a dated exercise.

Change the plan deliberately

A shortfall can be addressed by reducing an optional cost, choosing a different arrangement, delaying the start or adding confirmed resources. Each change has consequences. A cheaper setup might be suitable; skipping necessary food is not an equivalent saving. Promised extra hours that have not been scheduled cannot be counted as confirmed money.

Cost map: start-date payments → accessible money remaining → repeated monthly commitments → irregular/unknown costs → decision and condition. This keeps “Can I start?” separate from “Can I keep doing it?”

Optional Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) note, read September 28, 2026: distinguish an unexpected expense from an occasional expense that can be planned. A known monthly storage bill belongs in the plan before an emergency occurs. “Setting up an emergency fund”.
