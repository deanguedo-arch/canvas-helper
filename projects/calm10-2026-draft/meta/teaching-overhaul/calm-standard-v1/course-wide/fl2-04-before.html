Learn the basics

Understand minimum payments before choosing a strategy

A repayment strategy decides how to direct money among debts. It must start with what each account requires and how much repayment money is actually available.

Protect required payments first

A is the least required by the account’s current terms. Extra money is what remains after all required minimums. Choosing a priority for does not remove the other account’s minimum.

For a small invented example, $70 is available and two minimums are $20 and $30. The minimums use $50, leaving $20 extra. If only $40 were available, there would instead be a $10 shortfall; no extra-payment strategy creates missing money.

Compare the priority and its consequences

Highest-rate-first directs extra money to the debt with the higher rate while maintaining other minimums. Smallest-balance-first directs it to the smaller debt, which may help motivation by clearing an account sooner. Compare the supplied schedules for total interest and finish date rather than assuming either always changes both.

A sustainable plan can be repeated while protecting necessary commitments. An emergency reserve has a separate purpose; do not count it as both reserved and spent on debt.

Use balances and rates → required minimums → available extra → stated strategy → schedule cost and timing → ability to continue. Hoped-for gambling winnings or uncertain investment gains are not confirmed repayment money.

When you read the case: Read the statements for both minimums and the budget for the combined available amount. The first check asks how much is already committed to minimums. Only then compare where the remaining money goes.

Use the method with the case

Protect the minimums, then choose a priority

A is the least required under that account's current terms. Choosing a priority for extra money does not give permission to skip another required payment. Jo first sets aside $70, leaving $50 to direct deliberately.

With highest-rate-first repayment, extra money goes to the debt with the higher interest rate while the other minimum continues. Each dollar of that balance removed avoids a relatively larger future charge under these fixed-rate assumptions. With smallest-balance-first repayment, extra money goes to the smaller debt. Clearing an account sooner may help someone stick to a plan, even if this costs more interest. Compare the actual schedules rather than assuming one strategy always changes the payoff month.

For Jo, both finish in 11 months, but highest-rate-first costs $12.30 less interest. This is a mathematical comparison under the supplied terms, not a judgement about Jo's character. Motivation, irregular income and past-due accounts can change practical priorities. A plan should be both affordable and repeatable.

A shortfall needs a different decision

If available money is below required payments, choosing where to put “extra” money cannot fix the shortfall. Identify the gap. Review the budget and, in a real situation, seek the creditor's current options before assuming an extension or changed terms. Do not invent income or promise to miss essential costs. A lump-sum payment can reduce debt if it is genuinely available; spending the same emergency reserve twice cannot.

An emergency fund is accessible money set aside for unexpected costs. Its purpose is to reduce the need for new borrowing after a shock. An affordable contribution and a debt plan may coexist. The amount depends on circumstances; this lesson supplies Jo's available repayment money after that allocation.

Test the promise of a shortcut

means borrowing to buy an investment. The investment may rise or fall; the debt remains payable under its agreement. If someone borrows $500 and must repay $540, an investment worth $600 at sale leaves $60 after repayment. If it is worth $450, selling it leaves a $90 shortfall. These are two invented outcomes, not forecasts. Borrowing increases exposure and adds a fixed obligation to an uncertain return.

Gambling, lotteries and sports betting also cannot supply dependable repayment income. The possible prize is not money already available. For example, four fictional $5 losing tickets cost $20 and produce no repayment money. A winning story does not establish how often winning happens or cover losses. Do not insert hoped-for winnings into a budget as though they were confirmed earnings.

What is a worked example? An answer that is already completed, with the decisions or calculations explained. As you read, connect the answer to the records; you do not need to copy it.

A minimum payment is a required amount. Extra repayment is money left after all minimums. A payoff schedule belongs to its stated inputs; a new budget requires a new calculation rather than the old promised date.

The required payments are A $25 and B $45. The remaining $50 goes to B under highest-rate-first, making A $25 and B $95. The payments still sum to $120. Choosing smallest-balance-first instead makes A $75 and B $45. Both satisfy the minimums; they use the extra money differently.

Completed recommendation: “I would start with A $25 and B $95 each month, subject to the final-balance adjustment. This covers both minimums and directs $50 extra to B's 19% balance. The supplied schedule costs $96.35 interest, $12.30 less than smallest-balance-first, while both finish in 11 months. I would review the budget if income changes rather than treating $120 as guaranteed forever.”

“Put all $120 on B” leaves A unpaid and does not follow the model. “Borrow to invest and use the profit” replaces a known repayment budget with an uncertain future outcome. Neither is the same plan with a faster payoff.

Dara's repayment decision


Dara, a fictional adult, has two current debts. Account C owes $200 at 10% annually, minimum $20 monthly. Account D owes $600 at 18% annually, minimum $30 monthly. A budget supplies $100 monthly for these debts, after essentials and planned emergency saving. No fees or extra-payment penalties apply, and no account is past due.


A separate proposal says Dara could borrow $600, buy an investment and repay $648 in full after one year. There are no other charges in that proposal. A supplied stress scenario says the investment could be worth $540 at that time. That is a scenario, not a forecast or guaranteed sale price. Dara's priority is to reduce existing debt cost without adding an obligation that depends on an investment gain.
