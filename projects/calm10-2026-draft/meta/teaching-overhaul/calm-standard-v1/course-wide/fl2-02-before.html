Learn the basics

Use the same time unit as the interest rate

An interest rate needs an amount and a period before it gives a charge. This lesson uses a simple-interest model with an unchanged principal and the stated months-divided-by-12 rule.

Read the formula’s quantities

Principal, P, is the amount on which this model charges interest. The , r, must be a decimal: 6% means 6 ÷ 100 = 0.06. Time, t, must be in years when r is annual. is I = P × r × t, without adding earlier interest to principal in this model.

Two months is 2 ÷ 12 year; six months is 0.5 year. Multiplying an annual rate by a number of months without conversion treats those months as years.

Calculate the charge before the repayment

In a separate invented example, $200 at 6% annually for two months gives $200 × 0.06 × 2/12 = $2 interest. With no fees, is $202. The $2 charge and $202 repayment are different amounts.

Use principal → annual rate as a decimal → time in years → interest → principal plus interest and any separately stated fee. Label the result with dollars and its period. Round using the lesson’s stated convention.

This formula is for the specified unchanged-principal model. Reducing-balance payments and interest added to a balance require different calculations. The next loan-schedule lesson shows that distinction; do not assume every loan follows this formula for its full length.

Meet the situation and read the documents

Mina's simple-interest agreement

Mina is a fictional adult borrowing $600 for exactly three months. The agreement expressly uses 12% per year, with months counted as twelfths of a year. No interim payment is made; the full principal stays outstanding until the end. Interest is not added to principal during the period. Principal and interest are paid together at the end of month three. There is no setup fee or other charge in this example.

This is a mathematical teaching agreement, not an offer from a lender. Real products can calculate interest daily, add fees or use changing balances. Use this model only with the stated assumptions.

Terms on the agreement

  • Principal, amount borrowed: $600.
  • Annual rate: 12%, or 0.12 as a decimal.
  • Duration: three months, or 3/12 of a year.
  • Interest rule: principal × annual decimal rate × years.
  • Repayment at the end: principal + interest.

Use the method with the case

Match the rate to the time

Simple interest is calculated on the stated principal without adding earned or charged interest to that principal during the model period. Let P mean principal, r mean annual rate written as a decimal, and t mean time in years. Then I = P × r × t. I is the interest charge, not the whole repayment.

A percent means “per hundred.” Twelve percent becomes 12/100=0.12. Multiplying $600 by 12 rather than 0.12 would charge twelve times the principal for one year instead of twelve hundredths. Check whether the size of the answer makes sense: at 12% annually, one full year's simple interest on $600 is $72. Three months is only a quarter of that year, so the charge should be a quarter of $72.

The rate and time must use the same unit. With an annual rate, divide the number of months by 12. Three months is 0.25 year; six is 0.5 year. Do not apply the whole annual amount to every month. A useful alternative representation is annual interest ÷12 × number of months, which gives the same result under this agreement's month rule.

Keep three amounts distinct

Principal is the amount borrowed. Interest is the charge calculated by the formula. Principal plus interest is the repayment before any additional fee. With a separate fee, add that fee once to the total paid. Do not call the whole repayment “interest,” and do not assume a missing fee is zero.

Write units beside each step. “600×0.12×3/12=18” becomes more meaningful as “$18 interest over three months.” A sentence about what the number means is part of the answer, not decoration around it.

Change one assumption at a time

For a fixed principal and simple annual rate, doubling the time doubles interest. Doubling the rate or principal also doubles interest when the other two stay constant. These relationships help catch errors, but they do not establish the behaviour of every loan.

If payments reduce the principal, later interest may apply to a smaller balance. If interest is added to a balance and later interest is charged on that enlarged amount, the process involves compounding. The following lesson uses a reducing-balance repayment schedule. It would be wrong to apply this unchanged-principal formula to that whole schedule without accounting for payments.

What is a worked example? An answer that is already completed, with the decisions or calculations explained. As you read, connect the answer to the records; you do not need to copy it.

In simple interest, the annual rate needs time expressed in years. Months divided by twelve gives that fraction. Interest is the added cost; repayment includes principal as well. Keep rate and duration changes separate.

The agreement supplies P=$600, r=0.12 and t=3/12. First, $600×0.12=$72 for a full year at the stated simple rate. Three months is one-quarter of a year, so $72×0.25=$18 interest. Repayment is $600+$18=$618.

Completed calculation note: “The $600 remains outstanding for three months. At 12% simple annual interest, I=$600×0.12×3/12=$18. With no fees, Mina repays $618 at the end of the three months. The $18 is the borrowing charge; $600 is the return of principal.”

An answer of $216 comes from $600×0.12×3. It treats three months as three years. An answer of $72 uses a whole year. Neither matches the agreement's duration, even though the multiplication itself was carried out correctly.
