Learn the basics

A budget must fit both the total and the calendar

A monthly total can look affordable while a bill arrives before the money needed to pay it. This lesson teaches two checks: the whole-period plan and the money available on each date.

Learn the budget and cash-flow terms

A plans income, spending and money set aside. A cash-flow calendar puts money received and paid in date order. The is the money available at the start; a running balance is the amount after each movement.

Income minus expenses and planned saving gives a surplus if positive or a if negative. That whole-period result does not establish that every bill can be paid on time. Money due later is not available earlier.

Carry the balance through the dates

For a small invented example, start with $30. Pay a $20 bill on Wednesday: $10 remains. Receive $40 on Friday: $50 is then available. The Friday payment does not change what was available on Wednesday.

Use opening balance + income received − payments made = balance at this point. Carry that result into the next date. Identify the lowest balance, as well as the final one. In the lesson’s model, a negative balance signals using money not available; no overdraft terms are supplied.

A buffer is accessible money reserved for variation. A changed payment date works only when the case confirms the arrangement. Removing a necessary expense or assuming an unapproved extension hides the problem rather than establishing a workable plan.

When you read the case: Read the opening money, income dates and bill dates together. The first reading check concerns money available on one date. Later you will test the full total and calendar and explain a confirmed adjustment.

Use the method with the case

Two checks for a workable budget

A budget is a plan for receiving, using and setting aside money. A cash-flow calendar puts those movements in date order. Start with the opening balance, add money when it arrives and subtract payments when they leave. The number after each movement is a running balance.

First check the whole period: income minus expenses and planned saving. A positive result is a surplus; a negative result is a . A surplus does not mean that every day is covered. If the second payment arrives after a bill, that later income cannot pay the earlier bill without some arrangement that actually makes money available.

Second check timing: calculate the running balance through the month. The lowest balance is the smallest amount reached after a movement. In this case any negative amount signals that the plan uses money unavailable in the account. It is a planning warning, not a fee calculation. No overdraft terms have been supplied.

Calendar method: opening balance + income received − payments made = balance at this point. Repeat from the new balance, not from zero. A simple sequence of dated balances makes a timing problem visible even when the monthly total looks fine.

Separate a feasible change from a wish

Some amounts are fixed by the case; others can change. Food and transit are required here. Removing them produces a lower total while leaving Sam unable to carry out the stated plan. A confirmed payment arrangement can change a date without changing total cost. A request that has not been accepted cannot be treated as an agreement.

Overextending means committing more money than the plan can support, including repayments that leave too little for other obligations. A small individual payment can contribute to overextension when several commitments fall together. Check their combined effects rather than judging one price in isolation.

A buffer is money left accessible for variation. It has a job even while unspent. However, a buffer target can sometimes need review: preserving it at all costs is not more important than a real essential need. State the trade-off rather than pretending there is no constraint.
