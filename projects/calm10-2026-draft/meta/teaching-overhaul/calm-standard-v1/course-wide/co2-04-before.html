Learn the basics

Recognize a protected-ground concern using evidence

Unfair or upsetting treatment needs careful examination. This lesson teaches how to document a possible concern and identify a suitable review route using supplied fictional records.

Connect the event, effect and protected ground

A is a characteristic protected by the applicable human-rights law; the source record lists the relevant grounds for this lesson. A discrimination concern involves a negative effect connected to a protected ground. A disappointing result alone does not establish that connection.

Look for the employment event, what changed for the worker and the evidence connecting the effect to the ground. An is an interpretation drawn from facts. Keep it separate from the facts themselves. An email explaining a decision can be relevant evidence; a guess about someone’s private motive is different.

Understand accommodation and a review request

is a process to address a barrier connected to a protected need. The actual work requirement, relevant need and reasonable options matter. It does not automatically award a preferred shift or remove the job’s essential work.

In a simple example, a learner may need written confirmation of instructions to perform a task accurately. The question is how the communication barrier can be addressed while keeping accurate work—not whether a label alone proves inability.

Use event → negative effect → possible protected-ground connection → relevant accommodation question → suitable support route. Preserve dates and supplied words, limit private information, and request an individual review rather than guessing intent. The dated source explains process and time limits; no real complaint or confrontation is required here.

When you read the case: When you read the timeline, identify which words or decisions connect the changed work opportunity to the protected ground. The first check asks for that evidence. Later you will distinguish a supported concern from information still needed for a fair review.

Meet the first situation

Being treated badly at work can leave someone uncertain about what to call it or whom to ask. A useful first step is to separate what happened, why a protected characteristic may be connected, and what still needs to be understood. You do not need to decide a legal case before asking for information or support.

You will examine a documented disability-related concern, practise updating a record when an employer provides new information, and then prepare a support plan for a different scheduling and religious- concern. Use the supplied fiction; no personal experience or identity disclosure is requested.

Your route is Meet the situation and read the documents → Use the method with the case → Follow a complete example → Practise with support → Check your understanding → Apply independently → Review and complete. Pause whenever needed, including between the case and the example. All learning can be completed in this course. No interview, confrontation, complaint submission or external account is required. Guided writing and six questions are practice; the independent concern record and support plan form your final evidence.

Vocabulary. A is a characteristic covered by human rights law. An adverse impact is a negative effect, such as loss of a work opportunity. A connection is evidence that a protected characteristic was a factor in that effect. Accommodation is a change that addresses a need linked to a protected ground so a person can participate. An allegation is a concern someone reports; it is not itself a legal finding. An inquiry asks for information; a complaint begins a formal process with its own requirements.

Meet the situation and read the documents

Jules is 18 and works at fictional Clearview Tickets, an Alberta provincially regulated employer. Jules has a hearing disability and normally uses written confirmations of work instructions. Jules is seeking scheduled shifts at the customer counter, where work includes reading booking information, speaking or writing with customers, and processing tickets. The concern concerns access to those shifts.

Document A — Role and training record

Counter-shift criteria, issued September 1, 2026: complete booking training, demonstrate accurate booking entry in the assessment, and be available for the offered shift. Employees can use the approved written instruction checklist.

Training record, September 4: Jules completed the training and met the booking-entry assessment standard. The record shows availability for the two counter shifts advertised for September 14 and 16. No missed qualification is identified in this record. This does not reveal every operational decision the employer may have made.

Document B — Dated communication record

Date Exact supplied record
September 5 Jules emailed supervisor Pat: “I would like the September 14 and 16 counter shifts. Because of my hearing disability, please provide changes to the instructions in writing as well as spoken instructions. I can use the approved checklist.”
September 6 Pat replied by email: “I am not assigning counter shifts to someone with your hearing disability. You can stay on filing.” No individual assessment or accommodation discussion is included in that reply.
September 8 The published schedule assigned Jules to filing and assigned the two counter shifts to other staff. The reasons for the other staff's assignments are not supplied.

Jules saved the role criteria, training record, email thread and schedule. No threat of violence or immediate physical danger is reported. Jules wants advice and a fair review; Jules has not made a formal complaint.

Document C — Supplied workplace support route

An employee may raise a concern with HR coordinator Ash, including a concern involving the employee's immediate supervisor. Ash can receive a written summary and arrange a private discussion. The employee may ask to bring a support person. Requests about work-related access needs can be made in writing. This route is an available option, not a requirement to confront Pat alone.

Document D — In-course human rights records

Alberta Human Rights Commission, “,” “Protected grounds,” and “Rights & Responsibilities”; checked September 28, 2026. Employment is a protected area. A discrimination inquiry examines a protected characteristic, a negative impact and whether the characteristic contributed to that impact. Examples of protected grounds include disability, religious beliefs, race, colour, ancestry, place of origin, gender, gender identity, gender expression, sexual orientation, marital status and family status. The Act's age ground concerns people 18 or older. A concern outside a protected ground may still need another workplace response.

Commission, “Duty to accommodate at work”; checked September 28, 2026. Employers must consider needs connected to protected grounds and take reasonable accommodation steps up to undue hardship. Workers explain relevant needs and cooperate in the process. Assessment is individual; a preferred solution is not automatically the only reasonable one. Disability-related documentation may be needed, but a specific diagnosis does not have to be disclosed to the employer. Undue hardship requires more than ordinary inconvenience.

Commission, “Making a complaint,” “Accessibility,” and “Contact us”; checked September 28, 2026. The Commission provides human-rights information and can explain complaint requirements; it does not give legal advice. Its stated complaint limit is one year after the discriminatory act or treatment, so timely information matters. Support may include accessible formats, interpretation, a support person or adjusted communication arrangements. Availability is discussed according to the person's needs. A formal complaint has its own process; an inquiry does not automatically file one.

Use the method with the case

Use three connected questions

Start with the employment event rather than a label. What opportunity, condition or treatment changed? Then ask which protected ground may be involved and what evidence connects it to the negative effect. The source's three-part test helps focus the record, but this lesson does not replace the full assessment of the facts, explanations and applicable law.

Question Jules's evidence What the evidence does not decide
Is a protected characteristic involved? Hearing disability is stated in Jules's request. Every medical detail or diagnosis.
What is the negative effect? Jules requested two counter shifts and was assigned filing instead. The complete reasons for every other worker's assignment.
What suggests a connection? Pat's email expressly links the decision to disability. The final legal outcome after a complete review.

Not every disappointment establishes discrimination. If two qualified applicants seek one shift, one will not get it; that fact alone does not show a protected-ground connection. At the same time, a rule that sounds identical for everyone can create a barrier for someone with a protected need. “Everyone must receive instructions only by hearing them once” could disadvantage a worker who needs accessible communication. Ask what the rule does in practice.

Do not require proof of a person's private motive

A worker often cannot know what a manager privately intended. Document the words, decisions and effects instead. A stated positive intention does not erase a barrier, and feeling upset does not by itself establish the legal connection. Jules has a specific email worth preserving; there is no need to add guesses about Pat's character.

Use three labels while writing: fact, possible interpretation, information needed. “Pat sent the September 6 email” is a fact within the supplied record. “Disability appears to have been a factor” is a supported concern. “I want the employer's individual assessment and explanation of the decision” identifies needed information. Writing “possible” does not mean dismissing the concern; it shows where a proper review is still required.

Understand accommodation as a process

Accommodation aims to address a barrier connected to a protected need. The worker should explain the relevant work need and participate in exploring reasonable options. The employer should consider it individually and take reasonable steps up to undue hardship. In Jules's case, the request is written confirmation of changed instructions. It is not a request to waive accurate booking work.

The job's actual tasks and available controls matter. An unsupported assumption that a disability makes someone unsuitable is different from examining whether the person can do the work with reasonable accommodation. A request also does not automatically entitle someone to any chosen shift or their preferred solution. The question is whether the opportunity and accommodation need were considered fairly under the applicable duties.

Protect privacy while explaining the need. A support record can state “written confirmations needed for work instructions” without including unnecessary medical history. Other employees' private diagnoses or religious practices are not yours to demand or circulate. Relevant scheduling criteria and accommodation steps can be discussed without collecting a coworker's medical file.

Choose a safe, suitable support route

An internal route such as Ash may help arrange review. If the supervisor is involved in the concern, an alternative recipient can be useful. A worker may ask about an accessible communication method or support person before a meeting. The Commission can provide information about human rights and complaint requirements. The source describes approaching the other party as something to consider; this lesson does not require a worker to confront them first or alone.

Different needs may need different supports. The Commission addresses protected-ground concerns; Employment Standards addresses minimum employment conditions such as wages and hours; Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) addresses workplace health and safety. An immediate threat requires moving to safety and an appropriate emergency response rather than waiting for an administrative complaint process. Our fictional records do not describe such a threat.

Keep dates because formal processes have time limits. The Commission's one-year limit is a reason to seek timely information, not a reason to wait until the last day. An internal discussion should not be assumed to pause that limit. For a real situation, verify the current process and get help calculating deadlines if uncertain; this lesson does not ask you to calculate or submit a real claim.

A different concern: a religious observance and a schedule


Noor is 19 and an ordinary employee at fictional Riverbend Market, a provincially regulated Alberta business. Prepare a concern record and support plan. Do not decide a legal complaint. Use these records only; no personal religious information is requested.


Document F — Request, October 5, 2026. Noor emails scheduler Vic: “I need to attend a religious observance on Saturday, October 17, from 10:00 a.m. to noon. I am requesting accommodation for this religious need. I can work that day from 1:00–5:00 p.m. or discuss another reasonable arrangement. Please let me know what information you need.” Noor's usual Saturday shift is 10:00 a.m.–2:00 p.m.


Document G — Reply and schedule. Vic replies October 6: “We are short-staffed, so the 10:00–2:00 shift stays.” No accommodation options or supporting staffing details are discussed in the reply. The schedule posted October 9 retains Noor's 10:00–2:00 shift. Two coworkers' October 17 shifts have changed, but Noor does not know why, when they requested changes or what duties they cover. No comment attacking Noor's religion is reported. Noor remains employed and has not been disciplined.


Document H — Available route and privacy. Riverbend has a people-services coordinator, Sam, who accepts written accommodation-review requests and can arrange a discussion with a support person. Noor may keep copies of Noor's own request, reply and schedule; private coworker records are not supplied. Noor wants the religious need considered and prefers a written first contact. No immediate physical threat is reported. No review beyond Vic's reply has occurred.
