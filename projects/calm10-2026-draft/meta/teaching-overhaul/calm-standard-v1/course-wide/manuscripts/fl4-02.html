Learn the basics

Learning to verify a request without doing what an unverified sender asks

A convincing message can contain a real organization’s name and still ask for a dangerous action. In this lesson, you will learn to examine the request, identify a concerning pattern and verify through an independent route. We will also distinguish an unverified contact from proof that every contact is fraudulent.

Leah has a bank-looking text and a caller claiming to be CRA. She has not clicked, paid, replied or shared a code. The complete training cards let us examine their instructions without visiting a live message.

First, learn request, evidence and verification. Read the A/B contacts and follow a two-contact response. Practice adds a remote-access demand and an independently confirmed CRA document request. The FINLIT activity then helps classify three other supplied patterns.

The final task introduces Devon’s job message and appointment call. Write one two-contact plan: classify with evidence, specify safe verification and boundaries, and explain what happens after checking. No live contact or transfer is made.

Locate the action and possible loss

A scam uses deception to obtain a benefit. An is a clue worth checking, not proof by itself. Read the verb: send money, reveal a code, install an app, approve access or transfer funds. Then identify what could be lost: money, information, account access or device control.

Pressure can use urgency, a threat, an easy-return promise or concern for another person. Good spelling and a real organization’s name do not establish that the sender represents it.

Break the sender’s control of the check

uses a trusted route obtained separately from the message. A link or telephone number supplied by the suspicious sender may return you to the same deception. Use an already known contact or an official service reached separately.

For a simple example, a message saying “send your sign-in code to keep your account” asks for access information. Pause the interaction and reach the service independently; knowing your name does not verify the request.

Use requested action → exposure → pressure or other clues → separate verification route → confirmed or unresolved status. A category such as job, investment, purchase or family-emergency fraud explains a pattern; the actual request supplies the evidence.

Use the method with the case

Read the requested action first

Start with the requested action. A secret “safe account” transfer and sign-in-code request pose a different concern from a document discussion that permits independent checking. Sender names and caller ID do not establish identity in either case.

Choose a separate route, such as a known bank app or independently reached official verification service. Record exactly what that check confirms. Confirmation of a document request does not automatically authenticate the earlier incoming caller.

Classify by the story and the requested benefit

The label helps explain a pattern; it does not replace evidence. Card C offers investment returns, so investment fraud is the relevant pattern. Card D offers employment but asks the worker to pay, matching a job-fraud pattern. Card E impersonates a loved one in difficulty. Card F offers goods without adequate verification, matching a purchase/merchandise concern. Card G uses a developing relationship to seek funds.

Types can overlap. A supposed romantic partner who later recommends a guaranteed investment combines relationship and investment tactics. An online job that asks you to receive and forward money is different from ordinary paid work, even if it uses a real company's name. The CAFC advises verifying the actual employer independently and not paying deposits to unlock a job. A company existing does not prove that its name is being used by an authorized recruiter.

Break the sender's control over verification

Calling a number supplied only in the message keeps you inside the same contact's story. A separate route changes that. For a bank, use the number on a card or statement already held, or open the known bank app directly. For a family emergency, use the person's previously known number or another independently known contact. For an employer, find the real organization's official contact information independently and ask about the named vacancy and recruiter. Do not install an offered app or send money while checking.

For an investment, the source recommends independent research and registration checks. In Alberta, the provincial securities regulator is the Alberta Securities Commission (ASC). Its CheckFirst resource helps Albertans learn how to check an investment and the person or firm offering it. Registration is one check, not a guarantee of returns or proof that a message’s sender is the registered person. These references are optional; this course does not ask you to evaluate or buy a real investment.

Keep the possibility of genuine contact open

CRA may need supporting tax information and may call. Therefore, “CRA never calls” is not a safe rule. Neither is “they knew my name, so I should answer everything.” The taught process lets a real request be handled through a verified route while denying an impersonator control of the interaction.

Asking for callback details collects a claim to check; it does not verify the claim. End the call before checking. Reach the CRA verification page independently. If its tool cannot verify a direct number, use the official CRA contact route for further checking. A result about a listed number also does not make caller ID impossible to spoof. The next conversation must use a route you have verified yourself.

Read the narrated and visual transcript

Slide 1: Two messages, two requests

Read the action—not just the logo. A “Move your money to our safe account. Send the code.”; B “A new tax notice is available.”. What does each contact want you to do?

Two fictional contacts arrive. A bank-looking text says a transfer is at risk and asks the person to move money to a “safe account” and reply with a one-time code. A second notice says a tax message is waiting and tells the person to sign in through the usual government route. A familiar logo or official-sounding name does not identify the sender. Start by underlining the action each message requests and decide whether it is reversible or sensitive.

Slide 2: Spot pressure and sensitive requests

A cue raises concern; verify before acting. Urgency or threat; Transfer to a “safe” account; One-time code or password; Remote access or unusual payment. Cue ≠ proof • pause before an irreversible action.

Scams often create urgency, secrecy, threats, or an unexpected reward. Requests to transfer money to a “safe account,” buy gift cards, use cryptocurrency, install remote-access software, or share a password or one-time code are high-risk. Poor spelling can be a clue, but polished writing does not prove a message is real. A cue is evidence to slow down, not a complete identity test. Do not click, reply, transfer, or share secrets while pressured.

Slide 3: Verify through a separate route

Do not use the contact path supplied by the suspicious message. PAUSE; Find the official service independently; Ask if the request is real; Act only through a verified route. Known app • saved statement • official website typed independently

For a bank concern, open the known app or use contact details from a bank card or statement. Do not call a number included only in the unexpected text, and do not trust caller ID by itself. For a web notice, navigate to the known official site yourself rather than following a message link. If uncertain, talk to a trusted adult and ask the institution through an independently found route. A search result or advertisement may also be misleading; use the known official domain.

Slide 4: CRA contact needs a precise rule

CRA may contact people; caller ID is not proof. CRA may call, write, or send notifications; Caller ID can be spoofed; Never share passwords or security codes; Ask, end, verify independently if unsure. Use the CRA contact-verification tool or Canada.ca details.

Do not memorize the false rule “CRA never calls.” The Canada Revenue Agency may contact people by phone and other channels. A caller ID that displays a government name still does not prove who is calling. If a person is unsure, they can ask for the caller’s name, office, and callback number, end the call, and check using CRA’s official contact-verification tool or contact details found independently on Canada.ca. CRA may request some information for a legitimate identity check or missing tax information, but never share passwords or one-time security codes with an unexpected caller.

Slide 6: Worked answer: safe without guessing

Verify the contact; do not make an irreversible move. A Do not transfer or share the code; contact the bank independently.; B Navigate to Canada.ca yourself and check for the notice.; Unknown sender identity? Verify before acting.. Appearance is a clue—not authentication.

For message A, the safe-account transfer and code request are high-risk. Do not transfer or reply; contact the bank independently. For message B, the notification may be legitimate, but this fictional card is not proof. Navigate to Canada.ca yourself and check the account through the official route. If a caller claims to be CRA, remember that CRA can call, but caller ID can be spoofed. Ask for callback details, end the call, and verify independently if unsure. The lesson is neither “trust it” nor “all contacts are fake.” It is pause, verify, then act. If a learner already clicked or shared information, the response changes: stop interacting, secure the affected account from a trusted device, and promptly contact the relevant institution through an independently found route. Preserve the message and time, but do not reopen its link to collect evidence. If money was lost, contact local police promptly and report through current Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre guidance. Reporting does not guarantee recovery. A second person offering guaranteed reimbursement for an upfront fee is another reason to pause and verify.
