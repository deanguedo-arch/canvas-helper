Repaying a debt over time through scheduled payments.

### Follow a payment through one row

An amortization schedule records regular payments and the debt remaining after them. The opening balance is unpaid principal before this period’s charge and payment. Interest charged is the period’s borrowing charge. Principal repaid is the part of the payment reducing the debt. The closing balance is what remains afterwards.

For this lesson’s monthly model: opening balance × monthly rate = interest; payment − interest = principal repaid; opening balance − principal repaid = closing balance. That closing balance becomes the next row’s opening balance.

### Try a small example before the schedule

In a separate invented row, opening debt is $200, the monthly rate is 1% and the payment is $22. Interest is $2, principal repaid is $20 and closing debt is $180. Subtracting the whole $22 from $200 would wrongly treat the interest portion as debt repayment.

The amortization period is the planned repayment length. Read the annual rate and the rule for converting it to a monthly rate before calculating. Alex’s and Ren’s supplied models divide the nominal annual rate by 12 and round money to cents.

Use the method with the case

### Follow one row from left to right

Start with the opening balance: the unpaid principal before that month's charge. Multiply it by the monthly rate to find interest. Subtract interest from the payment to find principal repaid. Finally, subtract principal repaid from the opening balance to find the closing balance. That closing balance becomes next month's opening balance.

The same process can be read as a flow:

Opening debt → calculate interest → split the payment → subtract its principal part → carry the balance forward.

For month one, 12% per year divided by 12 gives 1% per month, so $1,000 × 0.01 = $10. The $88.85 payment first covers that $10. The remaining $78.85 reduces principal. Subtracting the whole $88.85 from $1,000 would ignore the interest charge and understate the debt.

### Explain the changing split

The payment stays almost constant, but the split changes. In month two, interest is charged on $921.15 rather than $1,000. The smaller balance produces $9.21 interest after rounding, so $79.64 of the same $88.85 payment can reduce principal. A constant payment does not mean a constant interest charge.

The amortization period is the planned time to repay the loan. Spreading the same debt over more months usually lowers each payment under the same rate and calculation rules. It also leaves some principal unpaid for longer, creating more interest charges. Both effects matter: a payment that cannot be sustained is not made suitable just by having a low lifetime cost.

### Use the tool as a model you can explain

The calculator repeats the row calculation. It does not know whether a purchase is needed, whether income will continue, or whether a real lender will approve an application. ### Slide 1: Why did the balance fall by less?

A payment has two jobs in an amortizing loan. PAYMENT = INTEREST + PRINCIPAL; Interest covers borrowing cost for the period; Principal reduction lowers the balance. The whole payment does not reduce principal.

A fictional statement shows a payment of about 88 dollars and 71 cents, but the balance fell by less than that amount. Where did the rest go? In an amortizing loan, each scheduled payment first covers interest accrued for that period; the remaining amount reduces principal. Because the principal balance changes, the next period’s interest is calculated on a different amount. We will follow the classroom case’s simplified one-thousand-dollar example using a stated Canadian conversion convention.

The model assumes a one-thousand-dollar principal, a nominal annual rate of twelve percent compounded semi-annually, and twelve monthly payments. It excludes fees, missed or extra payments, taxes, and changes to the contract. Under the Canadian convention used in this example, convert the stated nominal rate to an equivalent monthly rate: one plus point-twelve divided by two, raised to the power of two-twelfths, minus one. That is approximately 0.00975879, or 0.975879 percent per month. Product documents can use different terms; verify a real contract.

### Slide 3: Calculate the level payment

Use the converted monthly rate, not 12% ÷ 12. Principal P = $1,000; Monthly rate i ≈ 0.00975879; n = 12 monthly payments. Payment = P × i ÷ [1 − (1 + i)⁻ⁿ] ≈ $88.71344

For equal payments, the payment formula is principal times the periodic rate divided by one minus one plus the rate raised to negative the number of payments. Substitute one thousand, the equivalent monthly rate, and twelve periods. The calculated payment is about 88 dollars and 71 cents when displayed to cents; we keep the unrounded amount internally for the demonstration. Dividing twelve percent by twelve would use a different convention and would not match the stated semi-annual compounding assumption.

### Slide 6: Worked answer and misconception

As principal falls, the interest portion usually shrinks. Month 2 interest ≈ $8.99; Principal reduction ≈ $79.73; Balance ≈ $841.32. Same payment − lower interest = more principal reduction.

Using full precision, month-two interest is about 8 dollars and 99 cents. Subtract that from the calculated payment and roughly 79 dollars and 73 cents reduces principal, leaving about 841 dollars and 32 cents. The common mistake is to calculate month-two interest on the original thousand-dollar balance or to say the entire payment reduces principal. The model’s total interest over twelve exact payments is about 64 dollars and 56 cents before cent-rounding adjustments. These numbers depend on the stated convention and assumptions; they are not a current rate or an offer. If comparing this schedule with another loan, keep principal, payment frequency, and assumptions visible. A smaller monthly payment may simply extend the term and increase total interest. Add fees only when the offer states them, and do not compare a weekly payment with a monthly payment by looking at the number alone. The useful question is what the borrower pays in total, when payments are due, and whether the schedule remains affordable. Any real mortgage or loan uses its own contract and calculation terms; this classroom model is for understanding the balance movement.
