Learn the basics

### Learning to compare a savings balance with the price of its goal

A savings balance can rise while buying less of the same goods. In this lesson, you will learn the difference between the nominal number of dollars and purchasing power. We will compare a known goal price with known money before considering modelled growth.

A fictional basket begins at $100 and later costs $103, while its savings balance grows to $101. These records concern the same items and year. They introduce the price-and-balance comparison; they do not report Canada’s current inflation.

First, learn the two measures. Read the basket and balance, then follow their price-adjusted comparison. The professional purchasing-power clip supports that reasoning. Practice isolates a fee in two otherwise identical model runs. The longer compounding investigation remains optional.

Riley’s required final task uses a confirmed course-material price increase and two suitable purchase choices. Calculate the new price and funding gap, then explain timing, remaining money and a trade-off. Do not use an assumed investment gain to meet the deadline.

### Separate nominal money and purchasing power

A nominal amount is the stated number of dollars. Purchasing power describes what money can buy at the relevant prices. Inflation is a rise in the general price level over time; an individual item’s quotation can change differently.

In a separate invented example, a fixed basket costs $100 initially and $104 later. A balance grows from $100 to $102. The person has two more dollars, but is $2 short of buying the same basket. No money needs to be withdrawn for purchasing power to decline.

### Keep the price and balance calculations separate

A 4% price change uses a factor of 1.04: $100 × 1.04 = $104. To express the later $102 in that original price scale, divide by 1.04, giving about $98.08. This comparison amount is not a bank withdrawal or a new balance.

A fee is another cost. Its effect depends on what amount it applies to and when. When comparing model runs, hold the growth and price assumptions steady if the purpose is to isolate the fee.

Use the goal’s price → available nominal balance → gap → price-adjusted comparison if requested. A calculator’s chosen growth or inflation rate is a scenario input, not a forecast. The long-term compounding investigation remains optional and separate from the required purchasing-power task.

Keep a transaction in the account separate from a change in what its balance can buy. We will use this distinction throughout the required lesson and label the optional growth model separately.

Use the method with the case

## Ask two different money questions

    
- What is the new price? Multiply an old target by 1 plus its percentage change. Here, $100 × 1.03 = $103.

- How much is in the account? Read the nominal balance: $101. Nothing was taken out.

- What can that balance buy in the old price scale? Divide by the price factor: $101 ÷ 1.03 ≈ $98.06 in start-of-year dollars. This is a comparison measure, not a withdrawal.

    

A fee is another cost, but its effect depends on what amount it is charged on and when. Later, you will compare two model runs while holding growth and inflation fixed and changing only the fee. For a real decision, use the actual goal price if known. Do not assume a riskier return will arrive by a deadline.

    

### Keep a model separate from a forecast

Appreciation means an asset rises in value; depreciation means it falls. A calculator's chosen growth rate is an input, not a forecast. Market values can move unevenly and may be negative. Fees, deposits and withdrawals also affect the final balance, so a balance change is not automatically an investment return.

The next investigation is optional. It explains how a modelled balance can grow over many years. The required lesson resumes at [FINLIT’s purchasing-power clip](#fl3-04-video), where you ask what a balance can buy.

But what is it exactly? Basically, it's the snowball effect that happens with money and earning interest, or how interest makes money grow exponentially over time. If you put money into a savings account or investment that earns interest, in the beginning, interest is calculated on the initial amount you started with. But as that money starts to earn interest, you continue to earn interest not only that initial amount, but you begin to earn additional interest on the interest you've already made. And as the total amount of interest earned grows, the more interest you continue to earn. Hence, the snowball effect.

You need to protect your purchasing power of your savings against the effects of inflation. You see, as prices rise, each dollar is able to buy less. Makes sense right? ## Test a fee in the same model

    

Change only the fee so the difference has one cause. Both runs start with $100, add no deposits, last one year, assume 4% effective annual growth and 3% price growth. The model charges a 1% annual fee as one-twelfth of that percentage each month after growth. Values are rounded only for display.

    

**Disclosure: Why isn’t 4% − 1% − 3% the exact result?**

A quoted return before fees is not necessarily what remains for the investor. A fixed-dollar fee and a percentage fee can have different effects. You need the amount, base and timing. This calculator treats an annual percentage fee as one-twelfth of that percentage charged each month after growth and before any new deposit. Real products can use other conventions.

Do not simply subtract unrelated percentages without checking their timing. “4% growth minus 1% fee minus 3% inflation is exactly zero” is a useful rough intuition here, but not the exact model. The fee is applied throughout the year and inflation changes the price scale by multiplication. The tool makes the assumptions explicit so a small difference is explainable.
