Learn the basics

### Learning to count the costs around a major purchase

A headline price rarely tells us everything needed to start and sustain a purchase. In this lesson, you will learn to separate one-time, refundable and recurring amounts, identify missing costs and update a comparison when confirmed information arrives.

Tess is planning a move. The room contribution is only one part of the first day’s cash requirement. We will use the moving record to distinguish money tied up in a deposit from money available for next month’s bills.

First, learn the cost categories. Read Tess’s starting money and quotations, then follow the startup and recurring calculations separately. Supported practice gives three confirmed updates so you can revise the plan without treating hoped-for money as received.

Lee’s final case compares four months of bicycle and bus transport. Calculate ready-to-ride bicycle cost, bicycle cost including storage and bus cost, then explain a choice using the supplied travel need and uncertainties.

### Give each payment a category

An upfront cost is due at the beginning. A recurring cost repeats over the use period. A refundable deposit is paid now and may be returned under stated conditions. It reduces cash available now even when a return is expected later.

For a simple example, $300 first rent and a $300 deposit require $600 initially. If the deposit is later fully returned, that return changes the lasting cost; it does not reduce the $600 needed on the first date. Count the first rent once when calculating a period that already includes it.

### Keep known and unknown costs visible

An ownership cost or use cost helps keep an arrangement usable: for example, maintenance, utilities, storage or insurance where relevant. An estimate needs a basis and time period. A missing amount is unknown, not zero.

First calculate the known payments due by the start date and compare them with accessible money. Then calculate repeated commitments over the stated period, keeping deposit-return assumptions and irregular costs visible. A known minimum exceeding the budget already establishes a gap; a minimum that fits does not establish that every unknown will fit.

Use start-date cash → money remaining → recurring commitments → irregular or unknown costs → feasible change or specific question.

Use the method with the case

### Sort costs by what happens to the money

Tess needs rent, deposit, transit, setup and food at the start. Count all cash due even when a deposit may later be returned. The deposit cannot fund next month’s rent while it remains held.

For continuing affordability, count the recurring amounts again each month and leave unspecified utilities visibly unresolved. A remainder before an unknown cost is not the same as freely spendable money.

Use two views. Cash needed by the start date includes every payment due then, including a deposit. Cost over the use period separates money expected back from money used up, and states return assumptions. A deposit can affect affordability even when it is not a permanent expense.

### Give an unknown its own line

An unknown is a missing figure, not a zero. A known minimum is the total of amounts already established. If the known minimum exceeds available money, you can identify a problem without knowing every extra cost. If it fits, you still cannot call the plan fully affordable until material unknowns are resolved or a justified allowance is supplied.

A quote should state what it includes, how long it applies and when payment is due. “Utilities around something small” is not a quote. “$65 for this month's supplied utility package, due September 1” is usable in a dated exercise.

### Change the plan deliberately

A shortfall can be addressed by reducing an optional cost, choosing a different arrangement, delaying the start or adding confirmed resources. Each change has consequences. A cheaper setup might be suitable; skipping necessary food is not an equivalent saving. Promised extra hours that have not been scheduled cannot be counted as confirmed money.

Cost map: start-date payments → accessible money remaining → repeated monthly commitments → irregular/unknown costs → decision and condition. This keeps “Can I start?” separate from “Can I keep doing it?”

A known monthly storage bill belongs in the plan before an emergency occurs. ### Lee's four-month transport choice

Lee has $550 for travel over four months, with no additional transport money during that time. A bicycle would allow flexible trips along a suitable route supplied in the case. A bus pass also covers all required trips, but its schedule is less flexible. Lee already owns a suitable helmet and does not need to buy one for this case. All listed prices include charges.

Used-bike offer: purchase $340; required secure lock $45; confirmed initial service $65. These are due before riding. A shop has checked fit and basic function; the service addresses the identified maintenance needs. Secure storage costs $18 at the start of each of the four months. No resale money is assumed. Future repairs are not quoted and not included in the base total.

Bus option: $75 at the start of each month for four months. It meets the stated required trips. No other transport fee is included in this exercise.

Lee’s transport case changes the cost categories. The bicycle needs purchase, lock and service before riding, plus four months of storage; bus fares recur monthly. Use confirmed suitability and access, then discuss flexibility and unknown repairs without inventing a resale value.
