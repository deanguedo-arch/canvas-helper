The next-best alternative given up when making a choice.

**Liquidity**

Learn the basics

### Learning to choose what one extra amount should do next

Extra money can increase accessible saving, reduce debt or buy an investment, but it cannot do all three at once. In this lesson, you will learn to compare those jobs using a person’s stated priorities, deadline and risk.

Avery has $80 after essentials and required debt payments. The emergency reserve is below its target, a debt charges interest and an investment has uncertain outcomes. The case supplies a priority order so the recommendation has a clear basis.

First, learn access, debt reduction and opportunity cost. Read Avery’s alternatives and follow the reserve decision. Practice starts with the reserve already funded so you can reconsider the next dollar’s job under changed facts.

Jules’s final repair case gives a fixed deadline and three competing uses for $40. Calculate the repair gap and funded total, then explain the decision and what is postponed. No personal investment or bank action is required.

### Compare purpose, access and uncertainty

Spending buys a good, service or experience. Saving sets money aside for a purpose. Debt repayment reduces an obligation. Investing places money in assets with possible gains and losses. The label alone does not establish suitability.

Liquidity concerns how readily value becomes available money, including time and cost. A reserve may need ready access. An extra debt payment may save future interest while leaving less accessible cash; whether it can be borrowed back depends on the actual terms.

Identify money available for this new choice after the commitments already protected. Do not reallocate rent or a reserve the case says must remain separate.

### Name the alternative you give up

Opportunity cost is the value of the best feasible alternative given up when choosing. It is not the sum of every rejected option. If $20 can either build a reserve or reduce debt, choosing the reserve postpones that debt reduction. The same $20 cannot do both at once.

A return is a gain or loss relative to money invested, considering relevant costs. A pair of possible investment outcomes illustrates uncertainty; it does not establish their probabilities or promise the favourable one.

Use available money → purpose and deadline → access and risk → feasible alternatives → chosen use and opportunity cost. Explain the trade-off using the priorities actually supplied.

### Avery's $80 decision

Avery is a fictional adult with $80 unallocated today, after essentials and required debt payments. This is the only extra money available for this decision.

Situation note: Avery has $20 in an accessible emergency reserve and wants that reserve to reach $100. A separate debt balance is $300 at a fixed 18% annual rate. Its required payment is already covered in the budget. Extra principal payments are allowed without a fee. Reducing its balance reduces later interest under unchanged terms.

Available choices:

- Add the $80 to the accessible reserve; there is no withdrawal fee or lock-in in this fictional account.

Avery's priorities, supplied for this case: First bring the emergency reserve to $100; then reduce costly debt; then consider long-term investing. The investment is not needed to meet a particular deadline.

Use the method with the case

### Compare purposes before returns

Accessible saving remains available under the supplied account terms. An extra debt payment lowers principal but cannot be withdrawn again under this loan. An investment may gain or lose value. These different jobs matter before a return comparison.

Apply Avery’s stated order to the current balances. Then name the opportunity cost: choosing one benefit means giving up another use of this particular $80 for now. That is a trade-off to explain, not necessarily evidence of a bad decision.

Saving sets money aside for a purpose, often with access and stability important for near-term needs. Investing puts money into assets with possible income or growth, alongside risks and possible losses. Debt repayment reduces an obligation. Spending buys a good, service or experience. None of these words alone proves a choice is suitable.

Ask when the money may be needed and what happens if it is unavailable or worth less. Avery's reserve needs ready access. The investment scenarios do not guarantee $80 will be available when an emergency occurs. Even a possible higher return does not answer the access question.

An extra debt payment can avoid future interest under the loan's terms, but it also moves cash into a use that may not be reversible. This case expressly says Avery cannot borrow that paid amount back. A decision about debt and saving therefore compares both cost and access.

### Name the next-best alternative

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up when you choose. It is not automatically the sticker price and not the sum of every rejected option. Use the person's stated priorities to identify the next-best feasible option.

For Avery, if the reserve receives $80, the next-best use is extra debt repayment. The opportunity cost includes the debt reduction and associated interest saving delayed by choosing the reserve. The uncertain investment is a further alternative, not an additional opportunity cost to add to the debt repayment as though both could have been chosen with the same $80.

You can recognise a tradeoff without declaring a choice wrong. “This builds access, but postpones debt reduction” is a more useful explanation than “saving is always good.” Different priorities or circumstances can justify a different allocation.

### Test claims about return

A return is the gain or loss from an investment relative to money invested, considering relevant costs. Avery's $100 scenario would be a $20 gain before any costs outside the stated model; the $60 scenario would be a $20 loss. They demonstrate uncertainty, not the probability of either outcome. A sales headline showing only the gain is incomplete evidence.

### Jules's repair decision

Jules is fictional and needs a bicycle for a confirmed activity starting two weeks from now. A suitable repair costs $100, including all charges, and must be booked and paid by that date. $60 is already reserved for the repair. Jules has $40 extra today after other obligations, with no further money confirmed before the deadline.

An optional outing today costs $40. A fictional investment advertisement suggests putting $40 into an asset that “could grow quickly,” but supplies no guaranteed value or withdrawal date. Jules's stated priority is the repair first, outing second, investment third. The repair price is valid until the deadline.

Jules’s repair needs the remaining $40 within two weeks. Compare the confirmed repair cost with the outing and uncertain investment. Explain the missed alternative honestly, without inventing future income or a guaranteed quick gain.
