Learn the basics

### Learning what a mortgage payment leaves unfinished

A mortgage’s monthly payment does not tell us when the entire debt ends or what remains at renewal. In this lesson, you will learn to distinguish down payment, term and amortization, then read remaining balance and extra-payment conditions.

Lee and Noor have a fictional property quotation and a separate allocation for other ownership costs. The five-year term and twenty-five-year amortization describe different periods. We will use supplied outputs rather than require you to derive the mortgage formula.

First, learn what each period and amount means. Read the quotation and follow the first sixty payments. Practice shortens amortization while holding the term steady, showing the payment and balance trade-off.

Morgan’s final task compares two quotations under a $1,200 loan-payment allowance. Calculate P’s monthly margin and each annual lump-sum privilege, then explain the remaining balance and how an unconfirmed $20,000 extra payment affects the comparison.

### Read the contract and repayment plan separately

The term is the period for which the current mortgage agreement applies. The amortization is the planned time to repay through scheduled payments under the model’s assumptions. A two-year term within a ten-year amortization would not mean the full loan is paid off after two years or the same rate is promised for ten.

The remaining balance is debt still owing at a stated date. At the end of a term, it must be addressed through repayment or an appropriate further agreement. A long-run calculator illustration keeping one rate is a conditional model.

### Look beyond the quoted payment

A principal-and-interest payment covers those loan components. Housing can also involve taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance and transaction costs. Check what a supplied budget limit already includes before using it.

A prepayment is principal paid beyond scheduled payments. A prepayment privilege states permitted amounts and timing without a specified charge. If it uses a percentage, identify the base: original principal, remaining balance and property value are different amounts.

Use borrowed money → contract-period payments → remaining debt at term end → costs outside the payment → flexibility conditions. A payment calculation alone does not establish loan approval or complete housing affordability.

Use the method with the case

### Separate the contract from the repayment plan

Subtract the down payment from the property price to find mortgage principal. The term describes the current rate-and-agreement period; amortization describes the projected repayment length. Finishing the term need not clear the debt.

Use the supplied sixty-payment record to separate total payments, interest and principal reduction. Then read the extra-payment allowance as a contract condition with a base and reset period, not a general rule for all mortgages.

The term is how long the current mortgage agreement applies. Its rate and other conditions are established for that period. At the end, any unpaid balance must be addressed: for example, by repayment or an appropriate new agreement. Do not assume renewal has the same rate.

The amortization is the planned time over which scheduled payments would repay the loan under the model's assumptions. It can be much longer than one term. A five-year term within a 25-year amortization therefore does not promise the same rate for 25 years. A long-run calculator illustration holding a rate constant must say that it is conditional.

Read a quotation as three connected records: money borrowed today → payments during this contract → debt still owing when the contract ends. The remaining balance explains why someone may still need financing after years of payments.

### Know what the payment leaves out

A quoted principal-and-interest payment is not necessarily the entire cost of housing. Taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance and transaction costs may be separate. This exercise supplies a loan-payment limit only after those other costs have been considered; you cannot reuse it as a real affordability approval.

An application may require evidence of income, down payment, existing debts and credit history. The lender assesses its current criteria and the property. Knowing the payment is only part of that assessment. Do not upload personal records or infer eligibility from this fictional calculation.

### Read flexibility as carefully as the rate

A prepayment is money paid toward principal beyond scheduled payments. A prepayment privilege is the amount and timing the contract permits without a specified charge. Some agreements are more flexible than others. A percentage needs a base: M uses original principal, so its 10% allowance is based on $120,000, not the current balance or property price.

Extra principal paid earlier can reduce later interest under unchanged terms, but the amount, timing and any charge matter. This lesson compares permission only; its schedules contain no extra payments. Do not use an unchanged schedule to claim the precise saving from a proposed prepayment.

The term is the period covered by the contract’s stated conditions. Amortization is the planned time to repay the debt. A prepayment privilege describes an allowed extra payment under that contract; unconfirmed future money is not a guaranteed payment.

Lee and Noor start with $120,000 principal. Their first sixty payments include interest and principal reduction, leaving a substantial balance at the end of the five-year term.

The initial loan is $160,000 − $40,000 = $120,000. Sixty regular payments total 60 × $767.77 = $46,066.20. However, not all of that pays down principal: $33,870.12 is interest. Principal repaid is $46,066.20 − $33,870.12 = $12,196.08. Subtracting that from $120,000 leaves $107,803.92, matching the schedule.

M's annual extra-payment allowance is 10% × $120,000 = $12,000. It does not mean $12,000 is available in Lee and Noor's budget; it is a contract permission.

Complete explanation: “M fixes its rate for five years, while payments are calculated over a 25-year repayment plan. After the first five years, $107,803.92 remains because only $12,196.08 of the $46,066.20 paid reduced principal. The contract allows a lump sum up to $12,000 in each contract year without the stated prepayment charge. Future rates and any actual extra-payment saving require a new calculation.”

“The balance must be $73,933.80 because they paid $46,066.20” incorrectly treats interest as principal repayment. The schedule makes the split visible.
