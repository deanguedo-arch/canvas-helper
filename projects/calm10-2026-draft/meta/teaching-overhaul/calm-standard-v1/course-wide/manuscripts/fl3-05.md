### Calculate each gap, then combine the contributions

A goal states what the money is for, how much is needed and when. The gap is target minus money already assigned. A contribution is a planned addition. A combined monthly limit applies to all goals together, not separately to each.

In a simple example, a $100 target with $40 assigned leaves $60. Over three monthly saving dates that requires $20 monthly. If another goal needs $15 monthly, the combined $35 does not fit a $30 limit without an adjustment.

### Use phases and review points

A phase is a period with the same allocation rule. When an earlier goal is funded, its contribution can move to a later goal if the dates permit. At each boundary, carry the existing balance forward; do not reuse opening money as a new contribution.

An action says what moves, where and when. A review point says when to check prices, available money, dates and priorities. “Save more” supplies neither.

Use goals and gaps → available saving dates → combined limit → phased allocations → checkpoint balances → review. Choose planning support for a specific question: a calculator can check a projection, while a provider record can confirm an entry condition. Neither guarantees a personal outcome.

Use the method with the case

### Build one plan from several targets

Subtract Fei’s existing laptop saving from its target, then divide the gap across eight deposits. Allocate only the remaining monthly money to training during that phase. After the laptop is funded, redirect the available contribution.

Check the training balance across both phases rather than divide its opening gap by a contribution that was not available from the start. Then ask which actual cost or condition should be verified, and which support has the role to answer.

Start with each gap: target minus money already assigned to that goal. Then calculate what an even contribution would require before its deadline. Finally add the contributions to check the shared limit. If that fails, a plan needs a change, not more confident wording.

For Fei, the laptop gap is $360 over eight months, requiring $45 monthly. That leaves $25 of the $70 for training during those months. The training goal does not need its own unchanging contribution forever; after the laptop is funded, the full $70 can move to training.

A phase is a period during which the allocation rule stays the same. Name the start and end, each goal's monthly amount and the resulting balance. At a boundary, carry forward the balance rather than starting over or counting the opening money again.

### Include an action and a review point

“Save $70” does not say where it goes or when to review. A stronger instruction names the month-end transfer, the destination and the checkpoint. A separate record for each goal helps prevent double-counting. The money can be tracked without requiring a particular bank feature; a labelled ledger works for this exercise.

A review point asks whether the assumptions still hold. Has the target price changed? Is the monthly amount still available? Has a deadline moved? Has a new work, education, family or health situation changed the priorities? A plan is a working decision, not a promise that life will remain unchanged.

### Select support for the unresolved question

Choose a resource because it can answer the question. A calculator can check a projection but cannot confirm admission requirements. An official program page can state requirements but cannot promise personal investment returns. Product staff can explain an account's terms, while broader planning advice requires appropriate competence and an understanding of how the person is paid.

For a real advisory relationship, verify relevant credentials and registration independently and ask about costs and scope. In this course, all cases use supplied records and simulated questions. Your progress never depends on reaching a teacher, advisor or financial institution.
