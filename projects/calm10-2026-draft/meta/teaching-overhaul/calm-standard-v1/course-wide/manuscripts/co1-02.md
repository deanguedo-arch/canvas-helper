Learn the basics

### Learning how to act usefully within a first-shift boundary

A first shift brings tasks you can begin and others that need access, instruction or approval. In this lesson, you will learn to read a shift brief, recognize a boundary and choose a useful next action. Asking for help can be part of doing the job properly rather than a sign that you should guess.

Rowan is starting at Prairie Library Hub. Morgan, the shift lead, explains what Rowan may do. A visitor request, a spill and a participant list create different decisions. We will use the brief and floor plan to understand the situation before choosing an action.

First, learn what permission, training and private information mean for this shift. Read the brief and map next. I will demonstrate a reservation request that Rowan cannot complete yet. You will practise three other moments, checking each against the supplied instructions rather than assuming one permission covers everything.

For the final response, Rowan can move a supply cart but has not been trained to use its spray. A visitor also asks for someone else’s phone number. You will explain the boundary, immediate action and message for each request in the four labelled fields. This is a written simulation, not a real cleanup task.

### Read responsibility, authorization and training separately

A responsibility is something a role is expected to do. Authorization is permission for a particular action. Training is instruction and practice needed to carry it out. Being asked to help at a desk does not automatically authorize changing a reservation or using unfamiliar equipment.

A boundary identifies what must stay protected, such as accurate records, private participant information or people’s safety. A task can be familiar while a particular action still needs approval.

### Choose a permitted step and a clear message

First identify the situation and relevant boundary. If information or permission is missing, pause that action. Use a safe, permitted task while waiting only when doing so does not leave an immediate hazard unattended. A floor plan can help locate a clear route and the responsible person.

For a simple example, an unready login is a reason to leave the record unchanged and ask for the approved access process. “The login is not ready; I have left the record unchanged. Can you show me the approved process?” identifies the problem, action already taken and needed response.

Use notice → boundary → safe permitted action → specific message → required instruction or approval. The supplied brief determines who may authorize work in these cases.

Use the method with the case

### Read permission and responsibility together

Let’s turn the shift brief into a decision. Begin with the task being requested, then locate the instruction that applies to that task. Reservation access, cleaning training and participant privacy are different boundaries. A safe route on the map tells Rowan where to go; it does not authorize a new task on arrival.

Keep the visitor informed while the restricted action waits. A message should say what was requested, what Rowan has already done and what Morgan needs to confirm. We will use that approach in the reservation example, then test it against the other events at the desk.

### Pause the uncertain task and choose useful permitted work

Pausing one action does not mean abandoning the whole shift. If record access is not ready, Rowan can prepare blank cards or welcome visitors within the brief. If a spill is present, protecting people and contacting Morgan takes priority over routine preparation. The safest immediate step depends on the situation; “do another task” would not be enough if people were still walking into a spill.

The floor plan helps with this choice. It identifies a clear route and a way to contact Morgan without crossing the spill. A diagram is useful when its locations affect action. Here, choosing the short route would contradict the hazard information even if it saved time.

### Make the message actionable

A good short message contains the location or task, the specific uncertainty or risk, what you have done within permission and what you need confirmed. Compare “I need help” with “The reservation login is not ready; I have left the record unchanged. Can you show me the approved process?” The second message allows Morgan to respond to a concrete need.

Decision sequence: notice the situation → identify the boundary → take a safe permitted step → give a specific message → wait for the needed instruction before the restricted task. Do not turn a plausible guess into permission.

- At 9:35, a participant list lies face up. No visitor has asked to see it. Choose place it face down in the secure tray / photograph it for later / read it aloud to check it.

Use Check my choice. Feedback: in moment 1, restocking is permitted and the clear route avoids the hazard; crossing ignores it and record editing is unrelated and restricted. In moment 2, the first option protects the visitor and obtains help; walking through exposes Rowan and leaving fails to protect the approaching visitor. In moment 3, the first option follows the supplied privacy instruction; a photograph creates an unnecessary copy and reading aloud discloses details.
