**Gross pay**

Earnings before deductions for the stated pay period.

**Deduction**

An amount taken from gross pay under the statement's listed categories.

**Net pay**

Gross pay minus the current deductions.

**Year-to-date**

A total accumulated from the beginning of the year to the stated date.

Learn the basics

### Learning to check where a pay deposit came from

Being paid is only the beginning of checking your pay. In this lesson, you will learn to connect a time record, a pay stub and a bank deposit. Each answers a different question: what work was recorded, how pay was calculated and what money arrived.

Mika works at Pine Street Print Shop. We have the hours, pay stub and deposit for one pay period. You do not need your own job or financial records; we will work entirely from the fictional documents supplied here.

First, learn the difference between gross pay, deductions and net pay. Then read Mika’s dates and current-pay column. I will demonstrate the comparison. You will practise with Jules, whose stub adds up internally but may not account for all the recorded hours.

Your final pay-check note uses Ellis’s new records. Enter the hours, gross supported by the time record and net supported by the stub, then explain any discrepancy and what should be checked. These are three distinct results, not three names for the deposit.

### Follow gross pay, deductions and net pay

Gross pay is earnings before deductions. For regular hourly work, use the supplied hours × hourly rate. A deduction is an amount removed from gross pay. Net pay is the amount remaining after the listed deductions.

In a small invented example, three hours at $18 produces $54 gross earnings. If the statement lists $6 of deductions, net pay is $48. These are supplied practice amounts, not tax or contribution rates. Deductions have already been removed from net pay; subtracting them again would count them twice.

### Match the period before comparing records

A pay period identifies the work included. The payment date says when the money is paid. Current means this period; year to date combines payments in the calendar year. Current gross and year-to-date deductions belong to different comparisons.

Use matching time entries → current hours and rate → current gross → current deductions → current net → deposit. A deposit matching the net box does not prove the hours are correct. Read the pay-period dates on the stub and time record before checking the amounts.

If two records disagree, state the specific line and difference. The mismatch does not by itself tell you whether the cause was recording, approval or processing.

### Follow the money

Let’s follow the money from work to deposit. Add the recorded hours and multiply by the supplied hourly rate to find supported gross pay. Separately subtract the current deductions from the stub’s current gross to check its net. Those calculations may agree with each other—or reveal different problems.

Year-to-date figures cover a longer period. They cannot replace the current deduction total. The fictional deduction amounts are supplied for arithmetic, not rules about what every employee owes. Use the professional explanations to understand the document, then return to the case amounts.

Gross pay is earnings before deductions. For the single regular-pay line in this case, multiply hours by the hourly rate. The unit check helps: hours × dollars per hour produces dollars. The decimal 12.50 means twelve and a half hours; it does not mean twelve hours and fifty minutes.

A deduction is an amount removed from gross pay. Some payroll deductions contribute to public programs or tax withholding; others can relate to benefits or an agreed arrangement. The label tells you what is being withheld. It does not by itself prove the amount is correct or that it applies to every worker. In this lesson the amounts are supplied, so add them. Do not try to calculate current tax or contribution rates from memory.

Net pay is what remains after the listed deductions. It is the amount this statement says should be paid. Subtract deductions once: they are already removed from the displayed net amount. If you begin a spending plan with net pay and subtract them again, you make the available payment too small.

One-payment map: time entries → hours × rate → gross earnings → subtract current deductions → net payment. A second check compares that net figure with the actual deposit. A matching deposit can still come from incorrect hours, which is why checking only the last box is insufficient.

### Choose the right column and date

A different stub may include several earnings lines. Overtime is pay recorded for hours treated as overtime under the applicable arrangement; an additional earning might be a separately listed bonus. Read each line's supplied hours, rate and amount before adding gross pay. Do not assume that every worker's overtime threshold or rate is the same.

A second format to read: a fictional adult worker's statement lists regular earnings, 10 hours at $20 = $200; approved overtime earnings, 2 hours at the expressly supplied $30 rate = $60; and a $15 bonus. Gross is $200 + $60 + $15 = $275. These are the statement's supplied terms, not a lesson about legal overtime eligibility. A separately labelled expense reimbursement is money repaid for an expense; inspect its treatment on the actual statement instead of automatically calling every deposit amount wages.

Current means this pay period. Year to date combines payments within the current calendar year. The figures serve different questions. Use current amounts to check this deposit; use the cumulative column to examine totals over the year. Never subtract cumulative deductions from one period's gross.

The pay period and payment date also answer different questions. The period tells you which work is included. The payment date tells you when the money is paid. A shift after September 14 would not automatically belong on this stub simply because payment arrived September 18.

### Describe a mismatch without inventing its cause

If the time record and stub disagree, identify the two records, period and difference. An omitted shift is a possible explanation, but the documents may not tell you whether the cause was entry, approval or processing. A useful question requests a check of a specific line. “You stole my pay” is an unsupported conclusion from a mismatch alone.

### Slide 1: A smaller deposit

Start by finding the period—not by guessing at an error. Pay period  •  May 1–14; 12.5 regular hours × $18.00/hour; Gross earnings  $225.00. What is the statement actually measuring?

Mika expected the weekend deposit to equal hours multiplied by the hourly rate, but the deposit is smaller. The first move is not to accuse anyone or assume every line is correct. Find the pay-period dates and the hours covered. This classroom pay stub is fictional; its dates, hours, dollar values, and deductions are invented. We are learning how to read and reconcile a statement, not estimating a real student’s tax or benefits.

### Slide 2: Calculate the gross amount

Gross pay is before listed deductions. Regular hours     12.5; Hourly rate        $18.00; 12.5 × $18.00 = $225.00. Gross earnings = $225.00

Gross earnings are the starting amount before deductions. For this fictional hourly example, multiply 12.5 hours by $18 per hour. The units cancel: hours times dollars per hour gives dollars. Twelve times eighteen is 216, and half an hour at eighteen dollars is nine, so gross pay is $225. Compare that result with the printed gross line. It matches here, but the match cannot establish that the recorded 12.5 hours are the hours Mika actually worked.

### Slide 3: Classify the listed lines

Add only the deductions shown as deductions. Income-tax withholding     $16.00; CPP line                   $10.80; Employment Insurance (EI) line                     $3.60. $16.00 + $10.80 + $3.60 = $30.40

Now read the labels and determine which lines are deducted from this fictional cheque. Add the three listed amounts: sixteen dollars plus ten dollars and eighty cents plus three dollars and sixty cents. The total is thirty dollars and forty cents. In real payroll, eligibility, amounts, employer arrangements, and current rules depend on the situation. These invented figures are not current contribution rates, tax guidance, or evidence that a real deduction should apply.

### Slide 4: Reconcile net pay

Subtract the deduction total once. Gross     $225.00; Deductions − $30.40; Net        $194.60. $225.00 − $30.40 = $194.60

Net pay is gross pay minus the deductions taken from this payment. Subtract the total once: 225 dollars minus 30 dollars and 40 cents equals 194 dollars and 60 cents. The printed net amount matches. Do not subtract each deduction from the previous line and accidentally omit one. Also check whether the document has a different payment method, an adjustment, or an informational employer-paid amount before assuming that the net line equals the bank deposit.

### Slide 5: Pause: try the calculation

Use the document, then explain the limit. Gross?; Total deductions?; Net? What is still unknown?. Pause and write your calculation before continuing.

Calculate gross pay and total deductions. What does a match establish—and what does it not prove? Pause to work it out.

### Slide 6: Worked answer and next check

Arithmetic can reconcile while a record still needs checking. 12.5 × $18.00 = $225.00 gross; $16.00 + $10.80 + $3.60 = $30.40; $225.00 − $30.40 = $194.60 net. Next check: same dates, actual hours, and applicable records.

The result is 225 dollars gross, 30 dollars and 40 cents in listed deductions, and 194 dollars and 60 cents net. A common misconception is that the deposit amount is gross pay; it is net in this example. Another is that matching arithmetic proves a pay statement is correct. It proves only that these lines add up. For a real discrepancy, compare the same pay dates with time records and the agreement, then ask payroll or check current official guidance. Never use this classroom example as a real deduction rate. Suppose the time record showed 11.5 hours instead of 12.5. At the fictional rate, gross would be 11.5 times 18 dollars, or 207 dollars, which is 18 dollars less than the printed 225. That difference is a question to check against the same dates and actual work record, not proof of intent. A clear follow-up would identify the exact pay period and ask payroll to review the hour entry. The invented deduction lines should not be scaled to estimate a real cheque.
