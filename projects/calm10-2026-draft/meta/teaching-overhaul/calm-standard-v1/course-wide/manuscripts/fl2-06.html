Learn the basics

Learning to test a variable-rate offer beyond its starting payment

A lower starting rate does not tell us what a changing-rate agreement will later require. In this lesson, you will learn to compare a fixed quotation with supplied variable-rate scenarios. We will distinguish a possible path from a promised future rate.

Ari is comparing two $5,000 loans over twenty-four months. The fixed offer keeps its rate; the variable agreement recalculates payments if the rate changes. Ari’s $223 monthly allowance lets us test the later payment as well as total interest.

First, learn what stays fixed and what may change. Read the quotations and follow the supplied 5%-then-7% scenario. Practice changes the second-year rate to 10% so you can explain the effect without guessing future market rates.

Jules’s final comparison supplies two thirty-six-month schedules and a $250 monthly allowance. Calculate the variable offer’s later margin and extra total interest, then explain a recommendation using predictability, cost and scenario limits.

Know what is fixed and what can change

A stays the same for the specified period. A variable rate can change under the agreement. A is the point where the model applies a new rate. Some agreements recalculate the payment; others can hold the payment while changing its split or repayment length.

The case’s written payment rule determines which behaviour to use. A recalculated payment uses the remaining balance and remaining repayment months, because earlier payments have already reduced the debt.

Test the payment and the total separately

A payment margin is available repayment money minus the required payment. With an invented $45 limit and $40 payment, the margin is $5. If a scenario raises the payment to $50, there is a $5 gap. A lower initial payment does not establish that later ones fit.

A here means checking a less favourable supplied scenario. It is not a prediction of what rates will do or a lender’s approval procedure. Hold principal, payment frequency, repayment length and fees steady when comparing the rate paths.

Use rate rule → payment-change rule → supplied scenario outputs → margin in each period → total cost → recommendation. Keep a scenario’s possible outcome separate from a forecast or guarantee.

Meet the situation and read the documents

Ari's two quotations

Ari, a fictional adult, needs to compare financing for the same $5,000 purchase over 24 months. Both agreements have no setup fee, insurance charge or other fee under on-time repayment. Interest uses nominal annual rate/12 each month; payments occur at month-end. No additional advances occur. Ari has $223 a month available for this loan, after other planned costs.

Names used in this comparison: F means Ari's fixed-rate offer; V means Ari's variable-rate offer. The 5%-then-7% scenario shows one possible rate change for the variable offer. These letters only help you find the matching quotation; they are not ratings.

Fixed offer F: 6% annually for all 24 months. Regular payment $221.60; final payment $221.69; total payments $5,318.49; interest $318.49.

Variable offer V: begins at 5%. If its rate changes at month 13, the payment is recalculated to repay the remaining balance over the remaining 12 months. This agreement changes the payment, not the original finish date. Its first 12 payments are $219.36.

Supplied 5%-then-7% scenario: 5% for months 1–12, then 7% for months 13–24. The recalculated regular payment is $221.71; the final payment is $221.70. Total payments are $5,292.83 and interest is $292.83. This is one possible path, not a quotation promising next year's rate.

Read the terms: Under V, what happens at month 13 if the rate changes?

Use the method with the case

Read what changes when the rate changes

Ari’s variable offer changes payment at month thirteen while preserving the original finish date. That contractual mechanism matters: a changing rate does not behave identically under every loan.

Compare early and later payments with the allowance, then compare total interest. The 7% second year is supplied as one scenario; it cannot establish that next year’s rate will be 7% or that the variable option always costs less.

A lower starting rate can be attractive, but comparing only month one leaves out future payments. Compare the entire supplied scenario and test a less favourable one. A higher future rate may reduce the original saving or reverse it. A fixed rate offers rate certainty during its stated period; it does not eliminate income changes, missed-payment consequences or every other risk.

Hold the comparison steady

Use the same principal, repayment length, payment frequency and fee assumptions when isolating a rate choice. Otherwise, a lower payment might be caused by a longer period or smaller loan rather than the rate. Our comparison holds those features constant.

The calculator first pays down the balance at the starting rate. At month 13 it uses the remaining balance, not the original $5,000, for the recalculation. This matters because twelve payments have already reduced principal. We supply complete output records so you can analyse the decision without deriving the payment formula.

Test a budget and a cost separately

Subtract the required payment from the available monthly amount to find the payment margin. A positive margin means the stated payment fits that stated limit. A small margin provides little room if another cost changes. It does not prove the whole financial situation is secure.

Then compare total payments and interest. A loan may have a smaller total under one rate path but a later payment that fails the budget under another. Keep those findings separate so your recommendation can explain the tradeoff clearly.

A variable-rate scenario shows what would happen under a stated change; it is not a forecast of that rate. Monthly margin is the amount left within the payment allowance. Compare the later payment as well as the starting one.

Both fixed and supplied 7% scenario payments fit $223, and that scenario has lower total interest. I retain the scenario qualification rather than turn it into a guaranteed saving.

F's regular payment leaves $223 − $221.60 = $1.40 monthly margin. Its $221.69 final payment also fits. The 5%-then-7% scenario begins with $3.64 margin and later leaves $223 − $221.71 = $1.29. Both fit the supplied budget in this scenario, but neither leaves much room.

Interest in the 5%-then-7% scenario is $292.83 compared with F's $318.49. The difference is $25.66. That comparison includes the whole supplied path and the final adjusted payments.

Completed comparison: “With a 5%-then-7% path, V costs $25.66 less interest than F, and its payments fit Ari's $223 limit. However, the 7% later rate is a scenario, not guaranteed. F fixes its rate for the full 24 months and also fits, with a small margin. I would test a higher later rate before deciding whether the possible saving is worth the uncertainty.”

“V will definitely save $25.66” removes the condition that made the calculation true. “Fixed means cheaper” is also unsupported: the supplied 5%-then-7% scenario costs less. The correct explanation preserves both the observed numbers and their limits.
