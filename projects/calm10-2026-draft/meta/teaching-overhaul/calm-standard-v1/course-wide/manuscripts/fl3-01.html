Learn the basics

Learning to give each savings dollar a purpose and a date

A becomes a plan when you know its remaining gap, deadline and . In this lesson, you will learn to share a limited weekly amount between two goals without promising the same money twice.

Sam wants a winter jacket and a used bicycle. Some money is already assigned to each, and $35 is available weekly after other costs. The jacket date cannot move; the bicycle date can. A separate is not part of either purchase fund.

First, learn to subtract existing goal money and count remaining deposits. Read Sam’s board, then follow a plan protecting the jacket deadline. Practice reduces weekly saving to $25 so you can test what must change.

Lena’s final task compares a fixed activity-fee deadline with a flexible hobby goal. Calculate the weekly fee and eight-week balance, then write a saving instruction that makes the priority and postponement clear.

Find the gap and count the contributions

A target is the required total. An opening amount is money already assigned to that goal. The gap is target minus that assigned amount. The time horizon is how long remains before the money is needed.

In a simple example, a $90 target with $30 already saved leaves $60. Three remaining saving dates require $20 on each for an even plan. Count actually available before the deadline, not every date on the calendar.

Check the shared limit and changing allocations

A is the total available for all the goals together. If one needs $20 weekly and another $10, the combined requirement is $30. A $25 weekly budget cannot fund both even plans as written, although either calculation works by itself.

An allocation assigns part of the available money to a purpose. When one goal is funded, its contribution can move to another goal if the timeline permits. Carry forward money already saved rather than counting it twice.

Use purpose and date → gap → contributions → combined limit → checkpoint balances. Keep a separately protected separate. Short-term targets should not rely on an assumed investment gain arriving by the deadline.

Use the method with the case

Find the gap, then schedule it

Find each goal’s remaining gap by subtracting the money already reserved for it. Divide the jacket gap across the six deposits that arrive before its deadline. That sets the first priority without taking money from the .

Then allocate the remaining weekly amount to the bicycle. After the jacket is funded, its can change jobs. A phased plan records when that redirection happens; it does not count the same deposit for both goals.

A savings goal combines a purpose, target amount and date. The gap is target minus money already allocated. Divide that gap by the available saving periods to find an even contribution. For Sam's jacket, $120 divided by six weekly deposits is $20 per week.

The bicycle gap is $360 − $120 = $240. Dividing by twelve gives $20 weekly. Each calculation works alone, but together they require $40 a week. Sam has only $35. This is the central planning check: add all contributions sharing one budget before declaring either plan feasible.

Change contributions when a goal is finished

A plan need not use the same contribution forever. Once the jacket reaches its target after week six, its $20 weekly allocation becomes available for another purpose. You can redirect money already in the budget without inventing extra income. A timeline helps reveal when that becomes possible.

Use three lines in a plan: opening balance + scheduled contributions = balance at the checkpoint. Check both each goal and each week's total. A final balance that looks right is insufficient if the plan overspends along the way.

Give emergency money a distinct purpose

Saving often supports near-term purchases or unexpected costs where access and stability matter. Investing can support longer-term goals, but values may change and money may not be readily available on a particular date. Do not solve a six-week shortfall by assuming an investment will rise by the exact missing amount.

A separate emergency reserve is not “unused” money merely because it has no purchase date. It has a different job. If a real emergency requires using it, the plan should record that decision and later review replenishment. This case deliberately keeps it separate so the two purchase goals can be evaluated honestly.

A target gap is the amount still needed after current savings. A phase is a period with a particular allocation. Money assigned to one goal cannot also fund another, and a protected reserve stays separate.

Connect the video to this task. Turn the saving idea into an allocation: the example protects the fixed jacket date and assigns the remaining weekly money without counting it twice.

Sam needs another $120 for the jacket, so $20 weekly for six weeks meets its deadline. The remaining part of $35 goes to the bicycle in those weeks, followed by the full available amount afterward.

For weeks 1–6, put $20 into the jacket goal and $15 into the bicycle goal. Each week totals $35. The jacket reaches $60 + 6 × $20 = $180 on time. The bicycle reaches $120 + 6 × $15 = $210.

After the jacket is funded, direct up to $35 weekly to the bicycle. Weeks 7–10 add $140, bringing it to $350. Only $10 more is needed in week 11, so the bicycle reaches $360 then. The remaining $25 from that week's $35 can stay unallocated for a later decision; it is not silently spent or counted in both goals.

Completed plan: “Save $20 for the jacket and $15 for the bicycle in each of weeks 1–6. The jacket is fully funded at week 6. Then save $35 for the bicycle in weeks 7–10 and the last $10 in week 11. Both deadlines are met without using the $120 emergency reserve or exceeding $35 in any week.”

The first pair of separate $20 plans was not wrong arithmetic; it was incomplete coordination. The revised plan keeps the deadlines and shows how contributions move after a goal is met.
