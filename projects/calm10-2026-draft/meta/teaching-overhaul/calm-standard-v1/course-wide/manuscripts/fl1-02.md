Learn the basics

### Learning to separate available money from money already promised

A purchase can be appealing and still compete with something you need the same money to do. In this lesson, you will learn to examine commitments, influences and alternatives before making a money decision. The aim is a reasoned choice, not a rule that enjoying a purchase is wrong.

Ari has $60 for the next fourteen days and is considering a club hoodie. Transit and a needed bag repair already claim part of that money. Friends, family and a countdown advertisement offer different reasons to act.

First, learn how needs, wants and influences affect this specific decision. Read Ari’s budget and offer, then follow my comparison. Supported practice adds a confirmed gift so you can see which conclusion changes and which pressures remain.

The final case gives Ren a damaged bag, a repair option and a new-bag offer. Write a choice, the money remaining and reasons grounded in the deadline, necessary commitments and offer terms. No purchase or personal disclosure is required.

### Separate money held from money available

A commitment is money already needed for a planned obligation. A constraint is a limit the choice must respect. The money visible in an account is not necessarily all available for a new purchase.

For a simple example, someone has $50, with $20 needed for travel and $10 for supplies. Available for a new choice is $50 − $20 − $10 = $20. A $30 item can be paid for physically, but buying it would leave too little for those commitments.

A need is a requirement in the situation; a want is a desire without the same immediate necessity. Identify the function needed. A usable bag and a particular brand of bag do not necessarily serve different essential functions.

### Recognize influences without letting them do the arithmetic

A contextual influence comes from circumstances or relationships, such as transport options or group plans. A psychological influence comes from feelings, thoughts or habits. A marketing influence comes from how an offer is presented, such as a deadline or highlighted price.

These can overlap. Wanting to belong and seeing a limited-time offer can make a purchase feel urgent; they do not increase the available amount. Use commitments → money left → benefit sought → pressure or assumption → feasible choice and consequence. A choice should explain what is protected and what is postponed.

Use the method with the case

### Separate the amount from the pressure

Ari’s $42 transit and $12 repair total $54. The remaining $6 is the part available for a new choice. Having $60 in hand does not mean all $60 can be used without affecting those obligations.

Next ask what each influence contributes. A friend’s photo plan may explain belonging; a countdown does not supply more money. Compare the offer with Ari’s own priorities and whether club participation actually requires the hoodie.

A commitment is money already needed for a planned obligation. Available for this choice means what remains after those commitments, not the whole amount in an account. Ari can physically pay $48 now, but doing so would leave $12 for $54 of commitments. The consequence is a shortfall, not simply a lower balance.

A need is a requirement in the person's situation; a want is something desired without the same immediate necessity. Context matters. A bag can be necessary for carrying supplies, while a particular brand may be optional. It is more useful to identify the job an item must do than to label every version of the item a need. Ari needs usable storage and travel; the case says the hoodie is not needed to join club activities.

### Notice three kinds of influence

A contextual influence comes from circumstances and relationships: travel options, family habits, cultural practices, community expectations or advice. A psychological influence involves how a person feels, thinks or habitually acts: excitement, anxiety about missing out, buying when stressed or assuming a higher price means higher quality. A marketing influence is part of how an offer is presented, such as a countdown or a small weekly price.

These can overlap. Ari's wish to belong is a feeling, the group photo is a social situation, and the deadline is part of the selling message. The deadline may be accurate, but it does not make extra money appear. Ask two separate questions: “Is the deadline real?” and “Does this purchase fit the commitments?” A truthful advertisement can still emphasize the part of a decision that makes buying feel attractive.

### Respect values and examine the claim

A value is something a person considers important. Someone may value shared celebrations, independence, generosity or avoiding debt. Families and communities can hold different views, and individuals within them can disagree. Do not infer a person's beliefs from their background. Use the priorities actually supplied.

A belief such as “using credit always means you can afford something” can be tested: borrowing moves a payment into the future and may add costs. A different belief, “borrowing is always a personal failure,” also overlooks context, such as a planned education expense with a feasible repayment plan. In this lesson there is no borrowing offer or future income available to solve Ari's gap. Inventing one would avoid the decision the records actually support.

Decision path: commitments → money left → benefit sought → pressure or assumption → feasible choice and consequence. A feasible choice fits the supplied money and deadlines. The explanation should show which benefit is kept and which is postponed.
