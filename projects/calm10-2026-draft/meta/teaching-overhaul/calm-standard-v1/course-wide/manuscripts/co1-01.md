Learn the basics

### Learning to show an employer what you can already do

An application becomes more useful when it gives an employer an example they can understand. In this lesson, you will learn to connect a job duty to something a person actually did, then write a truthful statement showing the relevant skill. School and community experiences can provide that evidence even before a first paid job.

We will work with Rowan, who wants a welcome-desk helper job at a community library. The employer needs clear explanations and accurate records. Rowan has notes from earlier activities; we need to decide which actions answer those needs without claiming experience the notes do not supply.

First, learn the difference between a quality claim and an observable action. Then read the library posting and Rowan’s experience notes. I will show how one experience becomes an application sentence. You will practise matching another duty and revise your own sentence using the feedback and annotated model.

The final task introduces a community festival posting and a new school-fundraiser experience. You will choose a festival duty and write Rowan’s application response. The final checklist helps you check context, Rowan’s own action, the result and truthful limits. Everything needed is supplied here; no real application is sent.

### Connect the employer’s task with the applicant’s action

A job posting is an employer’s advertisement for an opening. A duty is work the person would do. An experience record describes what the applicant has already done. These documents have different jobs: one states what is needed; the other supplies possible evidence.

A quality claim names a characteristic, such as “careful” or “helpful.” An observable action tells a reader what happened, such as “checked the names against the list.” A claim becomes more credible when the action fits it.

### Build a short context–action–result statement

Context explains where and why the action mattered. Action names what the person did. Result states what happened afterwards. For a simple example: “During a club event, I checked the room number against the booking sheet and corrected the notice. Visitors reached the booked room.” This could support a checking duty; it does not establish professional management experience.

Match a posting requirement → find a record entry showing a relevant action → explain the context, action and supplied result → remove anything the record does not establish. An applicant can contribute to a team result without claiming to have done everyone’s work.

The posting tells us what the employer wants. The experience notes tell us what Rowan has done. We will now learn how to connect those two kinds of information without substituting a confident adjective for evidence.

## What is a job posting?

A job posting is an advertisement an employer uses to find someone for a job. It usually tells you the job title, the work involved, qualifications or skills, practical details such as hours, and how to apply. Its duties are the tasks the successful applicant would do.

At this hub, signing in means recording that you have arrived. Reservation records list the people who have booked a place in an activity.

Learn how to choose evidence

## Show the action behind a skill

A skill is an ability someone uses to do a task. For example, explaining instructions is a communication skill. An employer cannot tell how Rowan communicates from the sentence “I am a good communicator.” That sentence makes a claim but gives no example.

### A claim on its own

“I am organized.”

This names a quality. It does not explain what Rowan did or why an employer should believe the claim.

### A claim supported by an action

“I created a three-step sign-in order and explained the roles to two helpers.”

The employer can picture the action and ask Rowan how it worked.

That specific example is evidence: information supporting a claim. It must also fit the duty. Explaining sign-in roles is relevant to explaining the library’s sign-in process. Checking an attendance list is more relevant to keeping reservation records accurate.

### Use three parts to make the example clear

ContextExplain where you were and what needed doing. The employer was not there, so give enough background.ActionSay what you personally did. Use verbs such as checked, explained, organized or asked.ResultExplain what happened afterwards. Use an outcome supplied in the notes; do not invent a success.

A result does not have to be a number or an award. “The table opened on time” is a useful result because it tells the employer the preparation was completed when needed.

Describe the original setting accurately. A school example can show a skill that could be used at work, but it does not become library experience. If an example involves other people’s information, describe the task without including their names or private records.
