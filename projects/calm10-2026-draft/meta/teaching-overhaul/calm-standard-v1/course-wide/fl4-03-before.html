Learn the basics

Map what was exposed before choosing a response

When information may have been disclosed, a useful response starts with the confirmed and the connections between services. Learn that distinction before reading the incident record.

Separate information entered from harm confirmed

An exposure means information or access may have left the person’s control. A confirmed event is something the record establishes. Entering a password on a suspicious page supports action to secure affected accounts; it does not by itself establish a stolen identity document or money transfer.

A is the genuine provider’s process for protecting or regaining access. An account connection exists when one service can reset or recover another. Reusing an exposed password can affect more than the first service.

Match controls to the connections

Stop the suspicious interaction and use an independently reached trusted provider route. Identify affected accounts and recovery links, then prioritize using those facts. In a simple model where Account A resets Account B, leaving A exposed can undermine a change to B.

A password change, ending signed-in , checking recovery entries and enabling MFA address different access routes. Follow the supplied service rules; a password change does not necessarily end an existing session.

Use confirmed exposure → affected services and connections → timely protective actions → what was done → unresolved checks. A report can name categories of information without copying actual passwords, codes or identification numbers into coursework.

When you read the case: Read what the person entered and which services share the credential. The first check asks what exposure is established. Later you will explain a protective sequence and distinguish completed actions from results still needing confirmation.

Meet the first situation

If someone has entered information into a suspicious page, shame and panic can delay useful action. A response starts with a more practical question: what information or access was actually exposed? An account password, payment card and downloaded remote-control app create different response needs.

You will work through a password- log, construct a follow-up using a supplied provider guide, and then write a new response for exposed card details and an unrecognized transaction. The final product is a prioritized action plan that states each recipient's role and preserves evidence without exposing secrets again.

Vocabulary. Exposure means information or access may have become available to someone who should not have it. Compromise means security has been breached; an exposure can justify protective action before every outcome is known. A is a continuing signed-in connection. Revoking a session ends that connection using the service's controls. A trusted device is one the person has reason to rely on, rather than one possibly under an unexpected caller's control. An unauthorized transaction is generally a transaction the person did not make or approve; provider agreements and investigation matter.

Meet the situation and read the documents

Finley is a fictional adult with a music-service account and a separate email account. At 3:20 p.m. on October 8, 2026, Finley entered a music-service password on a page reached from an unexpected message. Finley then noticed the page was a look-alike and stopped. The actual password and page address are omitted.

Document A — Exposure log

Question Supplied answer
What was entered? Music-account login name and password only.
Was that password reused? Yes, on Finley's email account. The email is also the music account's recovery address.
Money/card/identity details? No payment, card details, Social Insurance Number (SIN), identity document or tax information was entered. No money loss is known.
Software or device access? No download, app installation or remote-access permission occurred. This does not certify the device's condition; it records what Finley did.
What is available? Finley's own updated phone, not used for the suspicious page, with the known email app and a saved official music-service bookmark.
Access status Finley can still access both services through those known routes. No recovery change is known.
Evidence already available Original message, time 3:20 p.m., and a screenshot without the password. No need to reopen the suspicious page.

Document B — Fictional provider security guide

Both fictional services provide these controls through their genuine settings. This is a supplied case guide, not a claim about every real service's interface.

  1. Change the password to a different strong credential for this service.
  2. Review recovery email/phone entries and ask official support about unrecognized changes.
  3. Review signed-in sessions. Changing the password does not automatically end all existing sessions on these particular services. Use Sign out other sessions to end them.
  4. Turn on supported MFA, following the service's recovery guidance.
  5. If access is lost or a change cannot be corrected, use official account-recovery support reached from the known service, not the suspicious message.

Document C — In-course response and rights records

Canadian Centre for Cyber Security, “Protecting yourself from identity theft online,” “Protecting your digital identity” and “Addressing digital identity theft”; checked September 28, 2026. Identify affected information and contact affected account providers. Change compromised/reused credentials and consider connected recovery accounts. Keep software current and monitor activity. Broader identity-fraud measures depend on what identity information or is involved; do not invent exposed data.

Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC), “Resolving an unauthorized transaction” and “Unauthorized credit and debit transactions: know your rights and responsibilities”; checked September 28, 2026. If card/account misuse is suspected or at risk, notify the institution or card issuer without delay and follow its security/dispute process. Federally regulated institutions must investigate disputed unauthorized transactions; use of an authentication method alone does not establish the customer's responsibility. Agreements, circumstances and prompt reporting matter. The course cannot promise a refund or decide liability.

CAFC, “What to do if you're a victim of fraud”; checked September 28, 2026. Keep relevant documents and messages, contact the institution that transferred money when money is involved, report fraud to local police and through the official fraud/cybercrime reporting route. Reports support a response and investigation; they do not reset an account or guarantee recovery. Immediate danger needs emergency help, not waiting for a report review.

Use the method with the case

Contain the exposure without waiting for certainty

Stop interacting with the suspicious page or caller. Do not send another code, pay to cancel a request or keep testing the page. Protective action can be appropriate even before the person knows whether the information has been used. Finley's password entry justifies securing the affected accounts; it does not justify inventing a stolen SIN.

An action plan should name both the risk and the control. “Fix the phone” is vague. “Use the separate updated phone to open the known email app and secure its recovery settings” tells the reader which route is being used and why. If a device may be under remote control or contain installed malicious software, stop using it for sensitive sign-ins and obtain official device/security support from a different trusted device. Removing an icon is not proof that access is gone. No such installation is reported in Finley's case.

Follow the account connections

Finley's email can reset the music account. If both use the same exposed password, changing only the music password leaves an important route at risk. A defensible sequence is to secure the recovery email first, promptly secure the music account next, and then review sessions, recovery entries and MFA on both. If access were already lost, official recovery support might have to come earlier.

The sequence is based on the supplied relationship, not a universal rule that email always comes before a bank emergency. If money is actively being moved, prompt financial-institution contact may be the most urgent action. Explain your priority with the case facts.

Check whether a change actually closes access

Password changes, session controls and MFA do related but different jobs. In these fictional services, an already signed-in session can remain after a password change. The guide therefore requires reviewing and ending other sessions separately. MFA helps protect future sign-ins; it does not undo information already taken.

The record is not complete when it says “changed something.” Log what was done, when and through which independently reached service. Then note what remains unresolved. If a recovery address is unfamiliar or access is blocked, official provider support has information and controls a classroom model cannot supply.

Separate account security from a transaction dispute

Exposure or observation Recipient/control that fits What that action cannot do alone
Entered/reused password Affected account providers; change credentials, review recovery/session controls. Determine or reverse a card charge.
Exposed card details or unrecognized charge Card issuer/financial institution, reached independently; secure card and investigate disputed activity. Secure an unrelated email password.
Unwanted installed access Official device/security support and affected providers from a trusted route. Guarantee the device is clean merely because an app icon disappeared.
Identity fraud or money loss Relevant providers, police and official fraud-reporting route. Guarantee reimbursement or replace urgent provider contact.

A merchant disagreement is also different from a transaction the person did not make or approve. For example, a person may recognize a purchase but dispute its description or cancellation. The next lesson examines that evidence. Describe the facts truthfully to the issuer rather than labelling every unwanted charge “unauthorized.”

Preserve useful evidence without preserving secrets

A log should include the date/time, contact or merchant label, requested action, amount if any, what was disclosed by category, actions taken and reference numbers. It should not contain the actual password, one-time code, full card number or SIN. Keep a safe original message or screenshot already available; do not reopen a dangerous link to improve a report. Share sensitive originals only through the institution's independently verified secure process if needed in real life, not through coursework or public posts.
