## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Jordan's experience notebook

An experience note records what happened during a particular task. It is different from a reference letter or an interest survey: it supplies actions that a reader can examine. Jordan wrote the notes below after two school activities. All people and events in these notes are fictional.

| Experience | What needed doing | What Jordan did | What happened afterwards |
|---|---|---|---|
| School-event poster | Two classmates could not find the time and location on the first draft. | Jordan moved those details to a larger heading, reduced decorative text and asked the classmates to check the revision. | Both classmates found the time and location without help. |
| Three-person science display | Three parts of a display needed to be ready by Friday. | Jordan asked each person what they could finish, made a shared checklist and arranged a Wednesday check-in. | The group noticed a missing caption on Wednesday, finished it Thursday and had all three parts ready Friday. |

### Jordan's reflection note

“I enjoyed deciding which poster information people needed first. I liked changing the draft after feedback. On the display, I liked making the next step clear, but I found chasing missing work tiring. I would like work with a reasonably predictable schedule. I also want chances to improve an idea after feedback. My current career goal is to investigate work that combines visual problem solving and clear explanations. My life goal is to keep regular time for activities outside work.”

A career goal describes a direction for work or learning. A life goal describes something you want your wider life to support. They can influence each other without being identical.

**Check the notebook.** Which detail is observable evidence of Jordan's action? Select one and use **Check my reading**.

- A. Jordan should become a designer.
- B. Jordan moved the time and location into a larger heading.
- C. Every design job has predictable hours.
- D. Jordan's friend likes the poster.

Feedback: **B** identifies something Jordan actually did. **A** is a recommendation that still needs investigation. **C** is not supplied and cannot be concluded from a school poster. **D** is an opinion; it does not describe the action that made the information clearer.

## 3. Learn the method

### Separate three kinds of clue

An **interest** is something that attracts your attention or that you enjoy doing. A **skill** is an ability used to carry out a task. A **work value** is something you want work to support, such as a predictable schedule, variety, helping people or time to improve your work. “I enjoy drawing” is an interest. “I rearranged information so two readers could find it” gives evidence of a skill. “I want regular hours” describes a value. None of these clues chooses an occupation on its own.

A person can enjoy an activity while needing practice. They can also perform a task well and dislike doing it repeatedly. Jordan's successful checklist suggests organization, while the reflection says repeated follow-up was tiring. Record both. Hiding a difficulty would make the career question less useful.

An **interest or strengths inventory** organizes a person's answers to a set of questions about themselves. Its results describe what the person reported; they do not independently prove a skill or assign a career. Imagine an inventory suggests “creative work” for Jordan. The poster notebook adds actual evidence, while the schedule preference adds a condition to investigate. If a suggested direction conflicts with experience, keep the conflict as a question rather than accepting the label. No inventory account or survey score is needed here.

### Make the action visible

“Jordan is creative” is a claim: a statement you would like a reader to accept. **Evidence** is the information that supports it. Useful evidence names the setting, the person's action and an observable result. The result need not be an award. Two readers finding the information is a result because it shows what changed after the revision.

Be precise about whose action it was. Jordan arranged the check-in; the group finished its display. Writing “Jordan did every part” would give Jordan credit for actions the notebook does not report. You can explain a person's contribution to a team result without pretending they worked alone.

### Build a cautious pathway idea

A **transferable skill** can be useful in more than one setting. Organizing information might matter in a poster, a set of instructions or a customer explanation. Transfer does not mean that someone already knows all the technical work in a new role. A school example provides a reason to investigate; it is not proof of professional expertise.

Use this sequence:

**Recorded action → possible skill → limit of the evidence → question about work.**

For example, “revised a poster after feedback” can support “some skill in making information clearer.” Its limit is one school task. A useful question is whether Jordan enjoys repeated revisions for other people's purposes. That question could change the plan; “Is design good?” is too broad to guide a comparison.

### Connect the idea to a goal

A provisional goal is a goal you can revise as evidence develops. It might be “Compare two roles that use visual explanations” rather than “Choose my lifelong occupation today.” Keep a career goal and a life goal visible when deciding what to investigate. A role might use a skill but conflict with a strongly held work value.

Financial goals can shape the route too. Wanting to keep training costs within a stated budget is different from wanting the highest advertised wage. Later lessons will connect goals to complete cost records. Here you are learning to state the direction and values before assuming a particular occupation, income or training expense will fit.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Turn the poster note into an evidence card

First I identify the task: readers needed to find the time and location on a school-event poster. This context explains why the layout mattered. I then trace Jordan's action: moving the information, reducing decoration and asking for another check. Finally, I use the reported result: both readers found the details without help.

The skill claim should fit these actions. “Jordan has shown some skill in organizing visual information for an audience” is stronger than “Jordan is brilliant.” It is also narrower than “Jordan can manage any design project.” We have not seen Jordan work with a client's budget, professional software or a deadline outside school.

**Completed poster card**

“During a school poster task, Jordan enlarged the time and location and reduced decorative text after two classmates had trouble finding the details. Both readers could then locate the information without help. This suggests some skill in organizing visual information and responding to feedback. It is evidence from one school task, so it does not establish professional design expertise. Jordan could investigate visual communication work and ask how often the work involves revisions and unpredictable deadlines. This would test the career goal against Jordan's wish for regular time outside work.”

Notice that the card does not promise a particular occupation. It explains why a direction is worth exploring. “Jordan has always been an expert designer” would add a history and a level of expertise absent from the notebook. Replacing that sentence with a specific action makes the claim more credible.

## 5. Practise with support

### Build the science-display card

The person and their stated goals remain the same. The evidence changes to the three-person science display. Keep the relevant facts beside your fields: Jordan asked what each person could finish, made a checklist and arranged Wednesday's check-in. The group found a missing caption and completed the display by Friday. Jordan found repeated follow-up tiring.

First select the claim best supported by these facts: **organized a shared task / completed every part alone / enjoys supervising people all day**. Use **Check my claim**. “Organized a shared task” fits the checklist and check-in. “Completed every part alone” contradicts the group record. “Enjoys supervising people all day” ignores the reflection and extends one experience too far.

Write short notes in **Action and result**, **Skill and limit** and **Next question linked to a value**. Then use **Compare with the display model**. You can open it before finishing if you need support.

**Optional hint:** Describe the check-in before naming organization. Use Jordan's reaction to follow-up to make your question specific.

**Model to compare**

- **Action and result:** Jordan made a shared checklist and held a Wednesday check-in. The group spotted the missing caption and completed all three parts by Friday.
- **Skill and limit:** This suggests organization and coordination in a small group. It does not prove Jordan did all the work or enjoys managing people repeatedly.
- **Next question linked to a value:** How much of a possible role involves organizing information, and how much involves chasing other people's work? Jordan wants useful feedback and a predictable schedule, so both the tasks and timing matter.

Your wording can differ. Check that your action comes from this case, your limit does not erase the useful evidence, and your question could distinguish between two kinds of work. Use **Return to my practice** and revise the same fields; writing is not automatically graded.

## 6. Check your understanding

Choose an answer and use **Check answer** for each question. Read the explanation, then change your choice if useful.

### Q1 — Which statement describes a work value?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-01:q1`.

A. “I rearranged a poster.” B. “I enjoy sketching.” C. “I want a reasonably predictable schedule.” D. “Two readers found the event time.”

Answer: C. A is an action. B is an interest. C names a condition Jordan wants work to support. D is a result.

### Q2 — Which claim stays within the science-display evidence?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-01:q2`.

A. Jordan helped coordinate a three-person project. B. Jordan completed everyone's work. C. Jordan would enjoy managing a large staff. D. Jordan never needs support.

Answer: A. A matches the checklist and check-in. B invents individual ownership of all work. C extends a small task to a new setting and preference. D invents an absence of support.

### Q3 — Why include the missing caption in an evidence note?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-01:q3`.

A. To prove Jordan made the original mistake. B. To make the project sound difficult. C. To claim the group failed. D. To show what the check-in helped the group notice and resolve.

Answer: D. A is not recorded. B is not the purpose of evidence. C ignores the completed display. D connects an action to its useful result.

### Q4 — A learner enjoys an activity but dislikes its rushed timing. What should a card do?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-01:q4`.

A. Hide the timing concern. B. Record both the enjoyable action and the difficult condition. C. Rule out the entire occupation. D. Replace the experience with a personality label.

Answer: B. A removes relevant evidence. B helps distinguish task fit from conditions. C generalizes from one experience. D supplies less detail about what happened.

### Q5 — What makes a skill transferable?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-01:q5`.

A. It guarantees employment. B. The action may be useful in another setting, while new knowledge may still be needed. C. It was used for pay. D. It means every occupation has identical duties.

Answer: B. A confuses potential with an outcome. C excludes valid school/community experience. B explains transfer without claiming expertise. D ignores different tasks and preparation.

### Q6 — Which next question could most usefully change Jordan's plan?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-01:q6`.

A. Does the occupation have a short title? B. Is work important? C. How often does this role require urgent revisions outside its usual schedule? D. Would a friend call it impressive?

Answer: C. C tests the schedule value against actual work. B is too broad. A does not address duties or values. D substitutes approval for evidence.

## 7. Apply independently

### Amina's strengths-and-goals card

Amina is considering work involving practical problem solving. These are new fictional documents; do not reuse Jordan's poster or checklist as Amina's experience.

**Amina's experience notes**

| Situation | Amina's action | Result and reflection |
|---|---|---|
| A bicycle brake rubbed during a community workshop. | With the workshop leader present, Amina compared the wheel movement before and after an adjustment and explained the observed difference to a neighbour. | The wheel turned without rubbing during the workshop check. Amina enjoyed finding the cause but needed help selecting the tool. |
| A younger cousin was learning a board game. | Amina replaced a long explanation with one demonstration turn, then asked the cousin to explain the next move. | The cousin completed the next turn without help. Amina liked making the instructions clearer. |

**Amina's goal note:** “I want to explore practical work where I solve problems and explain useful information. I value movement during the day and a predictable time to finish. I would like to develop my technical knowledge before taking responsibility for unfamiliar repairs.”

**Two fictional role sketches for inquiry:** A repair-shop assistant helps identify problems and explain service information; this sketch supplies no actual employer schedule or qualification rule. A recreation activity assistant demonstrates activities and helps participants use equipment; this sketch supplies no actual balance between desk duties and active work. These are possibilities to investigate, not offers or complete occupation profiles.

Create one strengths-and-goals card in the fields below. Use approximately 160–240 words across the fields; useful detail matters more than the count.

- **Two examples and what they suggest:** Explain both actions and results, then identify a supported skill and a growth area.
- **Goals, values and two directions:** State Amina's career direction and life preference; connect both role sketches to evidence.
- **A question that could change each direction:** Ask one specific question for each role, showing why its answer matters.

Use **Compare my card with a supported model** whenever you wish. It does not complete the lesson.

**Supported model:** “At the workshop, Amina compared wheel movement around an adjustment and explained the change. This suggests practical observation and explanation, with tool selection still a growth area. In the game, Amina demonstrated a turn and checked the cousin's understanding; the cousin then took a turn independently. That supports adapting an explanation. Amina wants practical problem-solving work and values movement with a predictable finish time. Repair-shop assistance could use observation and explanation, but the workshop example does not establish independent repair competence. Recreation assistance could use demonstrations, but the amount of active work remains unknown. For the repair route I would ask which duties are supervised at entry and what training is needed. For recreation assistance I would ask how much time is spent in activities compared with desk work and when shifts usually finish. Either answer could change which direction Amina investigates first.”

Another reasonable card may prioritize the roles differently. It must use Amina's supplied evidence and avoid inventing qualifications, hours or results. Revise your entries after comparing.
