## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Ari's next two weeks

Ari is a fictional student with $60 available now. No more money arrives during the next fourteen days. Necessary meals and housing are provided separately and do not come out of this $60. Ari has two commitments before the next payment: $42 for transit to school and $12 to repair the zipper on the bag used daily. There is no usable spare bag. Both commitments must be paid within the fourteen days.

**The group order notice**

“Club hoodie: $48, including all charges. Orders close tonight. The club's regular activities are open to members with or without a hoodie. No returns after a personalized order is placed.”

**Three messages Ari sees**

- Friend: “Most of us are getting one. It'll look good in the group photo.”
- Family member: “I like having the travel money set aside first. It makes the week less stressful.”
- Shop banner: “One night only! Don't miss your chance.”

Ari likes belonging to the club and likes colourful clothing. Ari also wants reliable travel to school. Those are real priorities in the case; neither needs to be mocked to examine the purchase.

**Check the money:** How much remains after the two stated commitments?

A. $60. B. $48. C. $6. D. $18.

Feedback: C follows $60 − $42 − $12. A ignores both commitments. B is the hoodie price. D subtracts transit but leaves the necessary repair unpaid.

## 3. Learn the method

### Separate the amount from the pressure

A **commitment** is money already needed for a planned obligation. **Available for this choice** means what remains after those commitments, not the whole amount in an account. Ari can physically pay $48 now, but doing so would leave $12 for $54 of commitments. The consequence is a shortfall, not simply a lower balance.

A **need** is a requirement in the person's situation; a **want** is something desired without the same immediate necessity. Context matters. A bag can be necessary for carrying supplies, while a particular brand may be optional. It is more useful to identify the job an item must do than to label every version of the item a need. Ari needs usable storage and travel; the case says the hoodie is not needed to join club activities.

### Notice three kinds of influence

A **contextual influence** comes from circumstances and relationships: travel options, family habits, cultural practices, community expectations or advice. A **psychological influence** involves how a person feels, thinks or habitually acts: excitement, anxiety about missing out, buying when stressed or assuming a higher price means higher quality. A **marketing influence** is part of how an offer is presented, such as a countdown or a small weekly price.

These can overlap. Ari's wish to belong is a feeling, the group photo is a social situation, and the deadline is part of the selling message. The deadline may be accurate, but it does not make extra money appear. Ask two separate questions: “Is the deadline real?” and “Does this purchase fit the commitments?” A truthful advertisement can still emphasize the part of a decision that makes buying feel attractive.

### Respect values and examine the claim

A **value** is something a person considers important. Someone may value shared celebrations, independence, generosity or avoiding debt. Families and communities can hold different views, and individuals within them can disagree. Do not infer a person's beliefs from their background. Use the priorities actually supplied.

A belief such as “using credit always means you can afford something” can be tested: borrowing moves a payment into the future and may add costs. A different belief, “borrowing is always a personal failure,” also overlooks context, such as a planned education expense with a feasible repayment plan. In this lesson there is no borrowing offer or future income available to solve Ari's gap. Inventing one would avoid the decision the records actually support.

**Decision path:** commitments → money left → benefit sought → pressure or assumption → feasible choice and consequence. A feasible choice fits the supplied money and deadlines. The explanation should show which benefit is kept and which is postponed.

**In-course FCAC note, read September 28, 2026:** needs and wants depend on a person's circumstances. Identify the function Ari must protect before judging the item. [Optional original, “Evaluate your needs and wants”](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/make-budget.html).

**Hint:** Write “Ari has $60, but only ___ is uncommitted” before discussing whether the hoodie is appealing.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Explain Ari's choice without judging Ari

I first reserve $42 + $12 = $54. That leaves $6. The hoodie costs $48, so buying it from the remaining money would require another $42. Ari does not have that extra money in the case.

The club notice matters as much as the numbers. It says regular participation does not require the hoodie. Ari can keep the belonging benefit of attending club activities while giving up the matching clothing. The group photo and countdown explain why waiting feels uncomfortable; neither changes the available $6.

**Completed decision note:** “I recommend Ari skip this order and protect the $54 for transit and the bag repair. Only $6 is uncommitted, so the $48 hoodie would leave a $42 gap. Ari values belonging, but the notice says the club remains open without the hoodie. The group photo and closing deadline create pressure to buy now. Ari could attend in existing clothing and plan for a later optional purchase when money is available.”

“Only careless people buy club clothing” is not supported. The problem is this budget and timing. With different resources, buying the same hoodie could be a reasonable choice.

## 5. Practise with support

### Change one fact

Keep all of Ari's original facts. Now a relative gives Ari **$50**, explicitly available for any purpose, before the order deadline. There are no new obligations. This is confirmed money, not a promised future payment.

Choose the strongest statement: **the gift removes the deadline / the gift changes what is affordable, but Ari still has a choice / Ari is now required to buy the hoodie**. Use **Check my interpretation**.

Feedback: The middle choice distinguishes means from preference. The first contradicts the order notice. The third turns having money into an obligation to spend it.

Fill **Money left after commitments ($)**, **Money left if Ari also buys the hoodie ($)** and **Ari's revised decision** (2–3 sentences naming one benefit and one trade-off). Use **Check the amounts**, then open **Compare two reasonable decisions**.

Hint: Add the confirmed gift once, then reserve the original $54.

Amount feedback: $56 = $60 + $50 − $54; $8 = $56 − $48. An answer of $110 is money before commitments; $62 subtracts the hoodie before commitments; $6 omits the gift. Other values receive the appropriate operation and a request to retry.

**Two complete models:** “Ari could buy the hoodie and still cover transit and repair, leaving $8. It supports Ari's wish to join the group photo in matching clothing, but leaves little flexibility for another expense.” Alternatively: “Ari could skip the hoodie and keep $56 available after commitments. This provides more flexibility, but Ari gives up the matching clothing. Club participation remains available.”

Revise your explanation so it does not call either reasonable choice a character flaw. It must use the changed facts, not repeat the original $42 shortfall.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — Why is the original $60 not all available for the hoodie?

A. Ari cannot ever spend a gift. B. A budget always forbids clothing. C. The store closes tonight. D. $54 is needed for commitments before more money arrives.

Answer D. A invents a rule; B misrepresents budgeting; C explains urgency, not available money; D accounts for the obligations.

### Q2 — Which is a psychological influence in the original case?

A. Feeling anxious about missing the matching photo. B. The transit price of $42. C. Fourteen days until new money. D. The repair invoice of $12.

Answer A. A identifies a feeling; B and D are costs; C is timing. These facts can interact with the feeling but are not the feeling itself.

### Q3 — “The countdown is real, so the hoodie is affordable.” What is missing?

A. A larger logo. B. Proof that friends approve. C. A comparison of the price with uncommitted money. D. Ari's favourite colour.

Answer C. A and D do not establish affordability; B adds social pressure; C checks the financial claim separately from the deadline.

### Q4 — Which response treats values fairly?

A. Everyone should have identical spending priorities. B. Explain the supplied priority and whether the plan can support it. C. Infer the family's beliefs from its background. D. Ignore social benefits because they are not prices.

Answer B. A and C assume unsupported values; B examines an actual priority and its consequences; D overlooks a genuine benefit that still needs a feasible budget.

### Q5 — Why can two decisions be reasonable after the gift?

A. Arithmetic no longer matters. B. The order is now refundable. C. Buying is compulsory. D. Both can cover commitments, while placing different value on the hoodie and the remaining money.

Answer D. A ignores feasibility; B invents a changed term; C invents an obligation; D explains the different trade-offs.

### Q6 — Which statement about borrowing needs correction?

A. “Borrowing creates a future repayment obligation.” B. “Costs and timing matter.” C. “If a lender offers money, the purchase must fit my budget.” D. “A plan should identify how repayment will happen.”

Answer C. A, B and D are useful planning statements. C substitutes access to credit for evidence that the borrower can repay it.

## 7. Apply independently

### Ren's backpack decision

Ren has $95 for the next three weeks and no other money arriving in that period. $30 is committed to transit and $15 to a school event Ren has already agreed to help fund. Food and housing are covered separately. Ren's backpack strap has broken; Ren needs a bag that can carry books beginning Monday. Today is Thursday.

**Repair shop note:** strap repair $12 including all charges, ready Saturday; the shop inspected the rest of the bag and found it usable. The repaired strap has a 30-day workmanship guarantee.

**New bag notice:** $58 including all charges; in stock today; same book-carrying capacity; 30-day returns for unused bags. A banner says “A fresh bag, a fresh start.” A friend says new gear makes a student look more organized.

**Ren's priorities:** reliable book transport and some money remaining for unexpected school costs. Ren likes the new colour but has not identified any additional function the new bag provides.

Write a decision note with **Choice** (repair / new bag / another feasible action using only the supplied options), **Money after commitments and my choice ($)** and **My reasons and the influence I questioned** (4–5 sentences). Explain feasibility, the function protected and one social or marketing assumption. If you choose another action, specify its supplied cost and availability; do not invent a free replacement.

**Model:** “I choose the $12 repair, leaving $38 after the $45 commitments. It will be ready Saturday before Monday and restores the needed carrying function. A $58 new bag does not fit the $50 remaining after commitments; it would leave an $8 shortfall. The promise of a ‘fresh start’ and the friend's comment connect appearance with organization, but neither provides evidence of an extra carrying function. Repair supports Ren's stated wish to keep some money for unexpected costs.”

A different recommendation needs an equally feasible use of the supplied facts. A claim that Ren should buy now and earn the difference later is not supported by this record. Open the model whenever useful and revise your own fields.
