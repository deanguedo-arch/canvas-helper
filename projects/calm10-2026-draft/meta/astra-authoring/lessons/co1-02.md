## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Rowan's first-shift folder

Rowan is a new helper. Morgan is the shift lead: the person assigned to explain tasks and approve work in this case. Rowan's shift is Monday, 9 a.m.–1 p.m. The first stop is the welcome desk. A **shift brief** is a short set of instructions for that working period; it is not evidence that the new worker already knows every task.

**Prairie Library Hub — first-shift sheet**

**Start now:** welcome visitors, prepare blank activity cards and restock public brochures.

**Wait for Morgan:** do not open or change reservation records until Morgan provides an approved individual login and demonstrates the process. Never use another person's login.

**Spill or blocked route:** stay on a safe, dry route; keep people away from the hazard, place the available cone only if this can be done without entering the hazard, and notify Morgan immediately. Do not attempt cleanup with an unfamiliar product or leave people walking into the hazard. The front-desk extension reaches Morgan without Rowan leaving the desk. The cone is within reach on the dry side of the welcome desk.

**Private information:** keep participant details face down in the secure desk tray when not being used. Do not share another participant's details. Ask Morgan to handle requests through the approved process.

**Equipment:** the supply cart may be moved along the clear route if stable and safe to move. One closed cleaning spray is labelled “Training required before use.” Rowan has not received that training. Permission to move the cart is not permission to use its contents.

## 3. Learn the method

### Read permission and responsibility together

A **responsibility** is something the role is expected to do. **Authorization** is permission to perform a particular action. **Training** is instruction and practice needed to carry it out appropriately. The three are related but not interchangeable. Rowan is responsible for helping at the desk; that does not authorize every action available on its computer.

A boundary tells you what must remain protected while you act. The reservation boundary protects accurate records and controlled access. The information boundary protects participant details. The hazard boundary protects people from entering a dangerous area. A useful response identifies the relevant boundary rather than simply saying “be careful.”

### Pause the uncertain task and choose useful permitted work

Pausing one action does not mean abandoning the whole shift. If record access is not ready, Rowan can prepare blank cards or welcome visitors within the brief. If a spill is present, protecting people and contacting Morgan takes priority over routine preparation. The safest immediate step depends on the situation; “do another task” would not be enough if people were still walking into a spill.

The floor plan helps with this choice. It identifies a clear route and a way to contact Morgan without crossing the spill. A diagram is useful when its locations affect action. Here, choosing the short route would contradict the hazard information even if it saved time.

### Make the message actionable

A good short message contains the location or task, the specific uncertainty or risk, what you have done within permission and what you need confirmed. Compare “I need help” with “The reservation login is not ready; I have left the record unchanged. Can you show me the approved process?” The second message allows Morgan to respond to a concrete need.

**Decision sequence:** notice the situation → identify the boundary → take a safe permitted step → give a specific message → wait for the needed instruction before the restricted task. Do not turn a plausible guess into permission.

In Rowan's case, the training label is a reason to ask before using the spray. The shift sheet supplies the actual case procedure. ## 4. Follow a complete example

### Reservation access is not ready

At 9:10, a visitor asks Rowan to change a reservation. Morgan has not provided an individual login or a demonstration. A co-worker offers a logged-in computer. No immediate hazard is present in this particular moment.

I locate the relevant rule: reservation records must wait for approved access and a demonstration, and another person's login is prohibited. The co-worker's offer does not change those instructions. Rowan can acknowledge the visitor without promising an unauthorized change.

**Completed decision row**

- **Boundary:** record access and accuracy; no approved login or demonstration yet.
- **Immediate action:** leave the reservation unchanged, explain that Morgan must assist, and contact Morgan using the extension.
- **Supporting fact:** the shift sheet says to wait for Morgan and not use another person's login.
- **Message:** “A visitor needs a reservation change. I do not have my approved login or a demonstration, so I have left the record unchanged. Can you help the visitor and show me the authorized process?”

After the request is safely handed to Morgan, Rowan can prepare blank cards while waiting. “Use the co-worker's login, then tell Morgan later” reverses the required order and removes accountability for the change. Being helpful includes respecting the limit and keeping the visitor informed.

## 5. Practise with support

### Three moments at the desk

Use the same shift sheet. These are separate moments; do not assume an action in one has solved the next.

1. At 9:20, the brochure shelf is low; the marked clear route is open. Choose **restock along the clear route / cross the spill to save time / edit a reservation first**.
2. At 9:25, a new visitor approaches the spill; Rowan is on the dry side of the desk and can reach the cone safely. Choose **direct the visitor away, place the cone from the safe side and call Morgan / walk through the spill / leave the desk to find a mop while the visitor continues**.
3. At 9:35, a participant list lies face up. No visitor has asked to see it. Choose **place it face down in the secure tray / photograph it for later / read it aloud to check it**.

Use **Check my decisions**. Feedback: in moment 1, restocking is permitted and the clear route avoids the hazard; crossing ignores it and record editing is unrelated and restricted. In moment 2, the first option protects the visitor and obtains help; walking through exposes Rowan and leaving fails to protect the approaching visitor. In moment 3, the first option follows the supplied privacy instruction; a photograph creates an unnecessary copy and reading aloud discloses details.

Now write **My spill message to Morgan**, 2–3 sentences containing location, immediate protection and the help needed. Use **Compare the message**, then revise.

**Hint:** Name what a visitor could encounter. Do not guess which substance spilled or which chemical would clean it.

**Model:** “There is a spill blocking the short route near the activity room. I have directed visitors to the clear route and placed the cone from the dry side. Please arrange the appropriate cleanup and confirm when the area is safe; I have not used the untrained spray.”

Different words are fine. A message that only says “all fixed” invents cleanup; a message that omits the location makes it harder to respond.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — Why does a co-worker's open computer not solve Rowan's access problem?

A. Computers are never used at work. B. Rowan lacks the approved individual access and demonstration required by the brief. C. The visitor must change the record personally. D. Morgan can never authorize work.

Answer B. A and D invent absolute rules; B names the actual boundary; C invents a visitor permission.

### Q2 — Which fact makes a route useful in the spill decision?

A. It is the shortest line on the page. B. It has the most furniture. C. It goes through the spill. D. It is clear and avoids the identified hazard.

Answer D. A prizes speed over the supplied risk; B is irrelevant; C exposes people; D uses the plan's functional information.

### Q3 — What can “pause” mean when there is no immediate hazard?

A. Leave a restricted record unchanged while doing a permitted task and requesting instruction. B. Abandon every responsibility. C. Borrow another login. D. Treat silence as approval.

Answer A. A combines restraint with useful action; B is unnecessary; C breaches the brief; D invents permission.

### Q4 — Which message is most actionable?

A. “Something is weird.” B. “I'm sure it is fine.” C. “The spray says training required; I have not used it. Who can show me the approved procedure?” D. “The cart exists.”

Answer C. A and D omit the request and boundary; B assumes safety; C states the relevant fact, action and question.

### Q5 — Why keep the face-up list in the secure tray?

A. To hide all mistakes permanently. B. The case brief says participant details must not be exposed or shared. C. To make the list unreadable forever. D. Because every printed document is dangerous.

Answer B. A and C invent purposes; B follows the privacy instruction; D overgeneralizes.

### Q6 — Moving the cart safely permits which further action automatically?

A. Mixing the spray with another product. B. Cleaning every surface. C. Ignoring its label. D. None; using the spray still requires training.

Answer D. A–C infer a new permission from a different task. D keeps transport and chemical use separate.

## 7. Apply independently

### The spray and the information request

At 10:15, Morgan asks Rowan to bring the stable supply cart to the activity room along the clear route. No new spill or blocked route is present. The same closed spray still says “Training required before use,” and Rowan has not been trained. A visitor then asks Rowan to provide another participant's phone number from the desk list. Morgan is in the activity room and can be reached on the extension. The shift sheet's privacy and access rules remain in force.

Complete two decision rows, **Cleaning spray** and **Phone-number request**. For each, write the boundary, safe immediate action, supporting fact from the brief or label, and exact message/question. Do not write only “ask Morgan”; explain what Rowan does while waiting.

Fields: **Cleaning spray: boundary and immediate action**, **Cleaning spray: evidence and message**, **Phone request: boundary and immediate action**, and **Phone request: evidence and message**. Use 2–3 sentences per field. The question concerns using the spray, not whether the approved cart movement is forbidden.

**Complete comparison model**

**Cleaning spray:** “The label requires training and Rowan has not received it. Rowan can move the stable cart on the approved clear route but leaves the closed spray unused. ‘The cart is here, but the spray requires training I have not had. Please confirm who should use it and arrange the approved instruction before I do.’”

**Phone request:** “The brief prohibits sharing participant details. Rowan keeps the list face down in its secure tray and does not provide the number. ‘I cannot share another participant's details. Morgan can help with the approved process.’ Rowan contacts Morgan: ‘A visitor is requesting another participant's phone number; I have not disclosed it. Please handle the request through the approved process.’”

A response can use another clear wording. It must preserve the specific boundaries, not invent permission from urgency or friendliness. Revise after comparing.
