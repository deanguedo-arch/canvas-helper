## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Tess's move-in folder

Tess is a fictional adult planning a first move. Tess has **$1,250 available by September 1**. All listed starting amounts are due by that date. No loan, overdraft or additional money is available unless a later practice update explicitly supplies it.

**Room offer — September 1 start**

Room contribution: $480 for September, then $480 monthly. Deposit: $480, held separately and refundable at the end if the stated return conditions are met. It cannot be used to pay the next month's room contribution. Utilities are additional; the provider's quote has not arrived. This invented agreement is a cost-reading example, not a statement of Alberta tenancy law.

**Starting checklist with quotes**

- September transit pass: $75, repeated monthly.
- Basic setup items: $160 once, including all stated charges.
- September food allocation: $190, repeated monthly under the exercise assumptions.
- Utility cost: unknown, separate from the $480 room contribution.

Tess expects $900 net income each month after moving. This is a supplied planning assumption, not a guarantee about employment. Other living expenses are outside this simplified exercise and would need investigation in a real plan.

**Check the deposit:** How should the $480 deposit appear in the move-in calculation?

A. Leave it out because it may return later. B. Treat it as money available for groceries immediately. C. Add it to monthly income. D. Include it in cash needed now and identify it separately as conditionally refundable.

Feedback: D captures both timing and return conditions. A ignores money tied up at move-in. B spends money being held elsewhere. C confuses an outflow with earnings.

## 3. Learn the method

### Sort costs by what happens to the money

An **upfront cost** is due at the beginning. A **recurring cost** repeats, such as a monthly transit pass. A **refundable deposit** ties up money now with a possible later return under specified conditions. These categories can overlap: the first monthly payment is both an upfront cash requirement and part of an ongoing cost. Count it once in the period being analysed.

An **ownership or use cost** is something needed to keep using the purchase or arrangement: maintenance, storage, utilities, insurance or interest, depending on the situation. A vehicle's advertised price, for example, does not include every future insurance, maintenance and operating expense. The relevant amounts require quotes and a time period; guessing that they are zero hides rather than resolves the uncertainty.

Use two views. **Cash needed by the start date** includes every payment due then, including a deposit. **Cost over the use period** separates money expected back from money used up, and states return assumptions. A deposit can affect affordability even when it is not a permanent expense.

### Give an unknown its own line

An **unknown** is a missing figure, not a zero. A **known minimum** is the total of amounts already established. If the known minimum exceeds available money, you can identify a problem without knowing every extra cost. If it fits, you still cannot call the plan fully affordable until material unknowns are resolved or a justified allowance is supplied.

A quote should state what it includes, how long it applies and when payment is due. “Utilities around something small” is not a quote. “$65 for this month's supplied utility package, due September 1” is usable in a dated exercise.

### Change the plan deliberately

A shortfall can be addressed by reducing an optional cost, choosing a different arrangement, delaying the start or adding confirmed resources. Each change has consequences. A cheaper setup might be suitable; skipping necessary food is not an equivalent saving. Promised extra hours that have not been scheduled cannot be counted as confirmed money.

**Cost map:** start-date payments → accessible money remaining → repeated monthly commitments → irregular/unknown costs → decision and condition. This keeps “Can I start?” separate from “Can I keep doing it?”

**Optional FCAC note, read September 28, 2026:** distinguish an unexpected expense from an occasional expense that can be planned. A known monthly storage bill belongs in the plan before an emergency occurs. [“Setting up an emergency fund”](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/savings-investments/setting-up-emergency-funds.html).

**Hint:** Mark each quote “now,” “repeats,” “may return” or “unknown.” More than one label may apply; the label is not another payment.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Find Tess's known move-in requirement

By September 1, Tess must have $480 room + $480 deposit + $75 transit + $160 setup + $190 food = **$1,385**, before utilities. Available money is $1,250, so the known amounts already exceed it by **$135**. The refundable deposit is still needed on the start date.

I also look beyond move-in. Known repeated costs are $480 + $75 + $190 = **$745 monthly**, plus utilities and any other relevant costs outside this exercise. The $480 deposit and $160 setup are not repeated each month. Comparing $900 income with $745 gives $155 before those remaining costs, not $155 guaranteed spare spending.

**Completed note:** “Tess needs at least $1,385 by September 1, $135 more than the $1,250 available, and utilities are still unknown. The move cannot be treated as funded on the supplied figures. I would obtain the utility quote and examine a lower setup cost or different start arrangement before committing. Known recurring costs are $745 per month; the $155 difference from assumed income must still cover utilities and other relevant costs.”

“The room is only $480, so Tess has $770 spare” ignores the rest of the move-in folder. “Utilities cost zero because no number is written” turns missing information into an invented fact.

### Use two confirmed updates

Tess receives a **$65 utility quote**, due September 1 and repeated monthly for this exercise. A checked second-hand setup package supplies the same needed items for **$90 instead of $160**. No other amount changes. An additional **$130 will be paid into the account August 30** for already completed work; treat this as confirmed received money available before move-in.

Enter **Revised cash needed by September 1 ($)**, **Confirmed cash available by September 1 ($)**, **Known monthly recurring total ($)** and **What can Tess conclude now?** Use **Check the updated amounts** and compare the model.

Hint: Replace $160 with $90; do not add both packages. Add utilities to starting and recurring calculations once in each distinct view.

Feedback: starting requirement $1,380; available $1,380; recurring $810. $1,450 retains the old setup price. $1,315 omits utilities from the revised starting amount. $1,250 omits the confirmed payment. $1,290 recurring wrongly repeats the deposit. For other values, show the relevant sum and invite revision.

**Model:** “Starting payments are $480 + $480 + $75 + $90 + $190 + $65 = $1,380. Available money is $1,250 + $130 = $1,380, so the listed move-in payments are exactly covered with no accessible money left. Recurring costs are $480 + $75 + $190 + $65 = $810, leaving $90 from the assumed $900 monthly income before other costs. The starting plan is tightly funded, not comfortable. Tess should account for missing real-life expenses and a buffer before making a real commitment.”

Revise your note if it calls “exactly covered” the same as “no remaining risk.” The exercise can establish a conditional calculation without guaranteeing a real move is affordable.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — Which is both due upfront and recurring in Tess's folder?

A. The one-time setup. B. September's room contribution. C. The refundable deposit. D. A hypothetical repair with no price.

Answer B. A is one-time; B is the first of repeated payments; C is held once under the stated terms; D is not a supplied scheduled amount.

### Q2 — How should a missing utility price be recorded?

A. $0. B. As income. C. As unknown, with a quote needed. D. As a second deposit.

Answer C. A invents a free service; B reverses the money direction; C retains the uncertainty; D changes its meaning.

### Q3 — What is wrong with repeating the deposit in every monthly budget?

A. It counts a one-time held amount as a recurring payment. B. Deposits are never paid. C. Recurring costs cannot be calculated. D. The room is free after move-in.

Answer A. A identifies the double counting; B and D contradict the supplied terms; C rejects a calculation the records permit.

### Q4 — The revised starting amounts exactly use all cash. What follows?

A. Every real-life expense is covered. B. No unexpected event can occur. C. Future income is unnecessary. D. Listed starting payments fit, but no accessible starting buffer remains.

Answer D. A–C overstate the evidence; D distinguishes a balanced start from resilience.

### Q5 — Which change is supported by the practice update?

A. Skip food with no consequence. B. Replace the $160 setup with the checked $90 package. C. Assume a second $130 payment. D. Remove the utility charge because it repeats.

Answer B. A ignores a required need; B uses a supplied alternative; C invents income; D treats repetition as a reason not to pay.

### Q6 — A transport plan includes monthly storage. Where does it belong?

A. Outside the budget because it is not the purchase price. B. As a guaranteed refund. C. In the cost for each month storage is needed. D. Only in a fraud report.

Answer C. A misses use costs; B invents a return; C matches the recurring obligation; D is unrelated to the described expense.

## 7. Apply independently

### Lee's four-month transport choice

Lee has **$550** for travel over four months, with no additional transport money during that time. A bicycle would allow flexible trips along a suitable route supplied in the case. A bus pass also covers all required trips, but its schedule is less flexible. Lee already owns a suitable helmet and does not need to buy one for this case. All listed prices include charges.

**Used-bike offer:** purchase $340; required secure lock $45; confirmed initial service $65. These are due before riding. A shop has checked fit and basic function; the service addresses the identified maintenance needs. Secure storage costs $18 at the start of each of the four months. No resale money is assumed. Future repairs are not quoted and not included in the base total.

**Bus option:** $75 at the start of each month for four months. It meets the stated required trips. No other transport fee is included in this exercise.

Write a four-month comparison. Fields: **Bike cash needed before the first ride, excluding storage ($)**, **Four-month bike cost including storage ($)**, **Four-month bus cost ($)** and **My transport recommendation** (4–6 sentences). Include money remaining from $550 for each option, one benefit relevant to Lee, and one unknown or condition that matters. If recommending the bike, recognize the small remaining repair allowance.

**Model:** “The bike needs $340 + $45 + $65 = $450 before riding, plus storage. Four months of storage adds $72, making $522 and leaving $28 from $550. The bus costs 4×$75 = $300 and leaves $250. I recommend the bus for this budget because it meets all required trips and leaves more room for an unexpected cost, although the schedule is less flexible. A bike choice would provide more flexible trips, but future repairs are unknown and only $28 remains in the four-month transport fund.”

A bike recommendation may be defensible if it acknowledges the flexibility benefit, the $522 base cost and the limited repair allowance. Do not invent a free storage space, guaranteed resale value or repair warranty. Compare and revise your own note.
