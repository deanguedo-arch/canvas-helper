## 1. Start here

### A starting rate is only the beginning

Two loans can start with different payments and finish with different costs. You will compare a fixed-rate agreement with a variable-rate agreement under two supplied rate paths. A scenario lets you test a possibility; it does not predict future rates.

Your final product is a recommendation using a new pair of fictional loans, their calculated outputs and a monthly limit. Explain both cost and uncertainty. No outside rate search or loan application is required.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Ari's two quotations

Ari, a fictional adult, needs to compare financing for the same $5,000 purchase over 24 months. Both agreements have no setup fee, insurance charge or other fee under on-time repayment. Interest uses nominal annual rate/12 each month; payments occur at month-end. No additional advances occur. Ari has **$223 a month available for this loan**, after other planned costs.

**Fixed offer F:** 6% annually for all 24 months. Regular payment $221.60; final payment $221.69; total payments $5,318.49; interest $318.49.

**Variable offer V:** begins at 5%. If its rate changes at month 13, the payment is recalculated to repay the remaining balance over the remaining 12 months. This agreement changes the payment, not the original finish date. Its first 12 payments are $219.36.

**Supplied scenario V1:** 5% for months 1–12, then 7% for months 13–24. The recalculated regular payment is $221.71; the final payment is $221.70. Total payments are $5,292.83 and interest is $292.83. This is one possible path, not a quotation promising next year's rate.

**Read the terms:** Under V, what happens at month 13 if the rate changes?

A. The debt is cancelled. B. The payment is recalculated for the remaining balance and months. C. The original principal is borrowed again. D. The rate remains fixed because the first payment was known.

B states this agreement's rule. A and C invent events; D confuses a known starting payment with a guaranteed future rate.

## 3. Learn the method

### Read what changes when the rate changes

A **fixed rate** remains the same for the specified contract period. A **variable rate** can change according to its agreement. These terms describe the interest rate; you must also read what happens to the payment or repayment period. In Ari's variable agreement, a change triggers a new payment calculated over the months still remaining.

Other agreements may hold a payment temporarily constant, which changes how much goes to principal as interest changes. Do not assume that “variable” always means the same payment behaviour. The written rule in the case is essential information, not fine print to skip.

A lower starting rate can be attractive, but comparing only month one leaves out future payments. Compare the entire supplied scenario and test a less favourable one. A higher future rate may reduce the original saving or reverse it. A fixed rate offers rate certainty during its stated period; it does not eliminate income changes, missed-payment consequences or every other risk.

### Hold the comparison steady

Use the same principal, repayment length, payment frequency and fee assumptions when isolating a rate choice. Otherwise, a lower payment might be caused by a longer period or smaller loan rather than the rate. Our comparison holds those features constant.

The calculator first pays down the balance at the starting rate. At month 13 it uses the **remaining balance**, not the original $5,000, for the recalculation. This matters because twelve payments have already reduced principal. We supply complete output records so you can analyse the decision without deriving the payment formula.

### Test a budget and a cost separately

Subtract the required payment from the available monthly amount to find the **payment margin**. A positive margin means the stated payment fits that stated limit. A small margin provides little room if another cost changes. It does not prove the whole financial situation is secure.

Then compare total payments and interest. A loan may have a smaller total under one rate path but a later payment that fails the budget under another. Keep those findings separate so your recommendation can explain the tradeoff clearly.

**Optional source note:** FCAC distinguishes fixed rates, variable rates and different payment arrangements. Its *Choosing a mortgage* rate section is an example of why rate type and payment behaviour must both be read. Ari's case is a fictional instalment agreement, not a mortgage or a forecast of lender practice. No outside page is needed.

**Hint:** Make two columns: “fits each monthly limit?” and “costs less under this path?” Answer each before choosing.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Compare F with scenario V1

F's regular payment leaves $223 − $221.60 = **$1.40** monthly margin. Its $221.69 final payment also fits. V1 begins with $3.64 margin and later leaves $223 − $221.71 = **$1.29**. Both fit the supplied budget in this scenario, but neither leaves much room.

V1 interest is $292.83 compared with F's $318.49. The difference is **$25.66**. That comparison includes the whole supplied path and the final adjusted payments.

**Completed comparison:** “With a 5%-then-7% path, V costs $25.66 less interest than F, and its payments fit Ari's $223 limit. However, the 7% later rate is a scenario, not guaranteed. F fixes its rate for the full 24 months and also fits, with a small margin. I would test a higher later rate before deciding whether the possible saving is worth the uncertainty.”

“V will definitely save $25.66” removes the condition that made the calculation true. “Fixed means cheaper” is also unsupported: the supplied V1 path costs less. The correct explanation preserves both the observed numbers and their limits.

### Test a 10% second year

Keep every original input except V's rate in months 13–24. Use **10%** instead of 7%. The first twelve payments remain $219.36. The recalculated regular payment becomes **$225.27**, final payment **$225.23**, total payments **$5,335.52**, interest **$335.52**.

The payment exceeds the limit by $225.27 − $223 = **$2.27**. Interest exceeds F by $335.52 − $318.49 = **$17.03**. If you enter $25.66, you have reused the old scenario's cost difference. If you enter zero for the payment gap, compare the later payment with $223 rather than the lower first-year payment. **Model:** “At 10% in year two, V's regular payment exceeds Ari's limit by $2.27 and costs $17.03 more interest than F. This path makes F more suitable for Ari's fixed monthly limit. It does not prove rates will reach 10%; it shows the plan's sensitivity to that possibility.”

Revise your original recommendation if it treated the lower starting rate as a guarantee. The two scenarios remain visible side by side.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — What does “fixed at 6% for 24 months” establish?

A. The borrower's income is fixed. B. All fees everywhere disappear. C. The rate stays 6% during that stated period. D. Every other loan costs more.

Answer C. A is unrelated; B invents terms beyond the agreement; C identifies what is fixed; D requires a comparison not supplied by the word alone.

### Q2 — Why is a starting rate insufficient for V?

A. Its later rate and payment can change under the agreement. B. Starting rates never affect cost. C. Principal doubles every year. D. All variable rates move upward only.

Answer A. A follows the contract; B ignores first-year interest; C invents borrowing; D treats a possible change as a one-way rule.

### Q3 — What balance is used when V's payment is recalculated at month 13?

A. The original purchase price plus an invented fee. B. Zero. C. The total of all future payments. D. The unpaid balance after the first twelve payments.

Answer D. A ignores principal repaid and invents a fee; B assumes the loan is finished; C confuses debt and future cost; D follows the schedule.

### Q4 — What does Ari's 10% scenario demonstrate?

A. A prediction that rates will be 10%. B. A possible path that exceeds the budget and changes the cost comparison. C. A legal maximum rate. D. An approved payment holiday.

Answer B. A turns a test into a forecast; B describes its purpose; C and D are not supplied.

### Q5 — Why compare final payments as well as regular ones?

A. The last payment is always zero. B. Final payments are optional. C. Cent rounding can produce a slightly different final amount that still must fit. D. The last payment replaces all earlier interest.

Answer C. A, B and D misstate the agreement; C explains the adjusted schedule amount.

### Q6 — Which recommendation is appropriately qualified?

A. “Under the supplied path, this option costs less; another path could change that result.” B. “The starting payment proves lifetime cost.” C. “Variable is always cheaper.” D. “Fixed eliminates every financial risk.”

Answer A. A identifies evidence and uncertainty; B omits future payments; C and D overgeneralize.

## 7. Apply independently

### Jules's three-year comparison

Jules, a fictional adult, compares **$8,000 over 36 months**, with no fees, end-of-month payments and annual nominal rate/12. The available amount is **$250 monthly for this loan**, after other planned costs. Both cases include an adjusted final payment.

| Record | Fixed G | Variable H, supplied scenario |
|---|---:|---:|
| Rate | 6.5% throughout | 5.5% months 1–12, then 8.5% months 13–36 |
| First 12 regular payments | $245.19 | $241.57 |
| Later regular payments | $245.19 | $249.02 |
| Final payment | $245.26 | $248.93 |
| Total payments | $8,826.91 | $8,875.23 |
| Total interest | $826.91 | $875.23 |

H recalculates its payment at month 13 over the remaining 24 months. Its later rate is a scenario. Jules values predictable payments and lower cost, but wants to understand why the lower starting payment is not decisive.

Enter **H's later monthly margin ($)** and **H's extra total interest compared with G ($)**. Write **My recommendation for Jules**, 4–5 sentences comparing fit, cost, rate certainty and a condition that could change the result.

**Complete model:** “H's later regular payment leaves $250 − $249.02 = $0.98, so it fits but with very little margin. Under the supplied path it costs $875.23 − $826.91 = $48.32 more interest than G. G's regular and final payments fit the limit, and its rate remains fixed for all 36 months. I recommend G for Jules's stated priorities. A different actual rate path could change H's total, so I would not claim that this scenario proves variable loans always cost more.”

Do not say H exceeds $250. The important distinction is a narrow margin, not a shortfall. Another recommendation must acknowledge the same costs and uncertainty. Compare and revise.
