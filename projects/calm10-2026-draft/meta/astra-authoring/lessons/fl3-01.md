# FL3-01 — Give a savings goal a job

Complete authored script. Version `2026-09-28.astra.1`. Canonical course unchanged.

## 1. Start here

### A goal needs an amount, a date and a contribution

“Save more” is an intention. A workable savings goal says what the money is for, how much is still needed, when it will be used and where each contribution will come from. You will build a plan for two goals sharing the same weekly money.

Your final product is an eight-week saving instruction for a fictional learner with a fee deadline and a competing hobby goal. No real account, personal budget or outside research is required.

**How to complete this lesson:** Read **Sam's savings board** and the money note. Learn **Find the gap, then schedule it**, follow a plan that changes after the first goal is finished, and test a lower weekly amount. Answer six questions. Complete **Lena's fee deadline**, compare with the model, revise and sign off. Pause after the first plan if needed; your work saves.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Sam's savings board

Sam is fictional. The board separates money by purpose so the same dollars are not promised twice. The figures below are all-in prices for this exercise and remain unchanged during the plan. No interest is included.

| Goal | Money needed | Already set aside for this goal | Deadline |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Suitable winter jacket | $180 | $60 | End of week 6 |
| Used bicycle, with necessary equipment included | $360 | $120 | End of week 12 |

**Weekly money note:** Sam has **$35 at the end of each week** to share between these goals, after other costs. A separate **$120 emergency reserve** is for unexpected essential costs and is not available for the jacket or bicycle in this plan. The week-6 and week-12 deposits arrive before the respective purchase deadlines. The bicycle deadline can be moved if the plan cannot meet it; the jacket deadline cannot.

A savings board is a record of targets and contributions, not a bank statement or a claim that all the money already exists. “Already set aside” is the opening balance for that goal. Future weekly deposits still have to happen.

**Read the board:** How much more is needed for the jacket?

A. $180. B. $120. C. $60. D. $300.

B is $180 − $60. A ignores money already saved; C repeats the opening balance; D adds target and balance rather than finding the gap.

## 3. Learn the method

### Find the gap, then schedule it

A **savings goal** combines a purpose, target amount and date. The **gap** is target minus money already allocated. Divide that gap by the available saving periods to find an even contribution. For Sam's jacket, $120 divided by six weekly deposits is $20 per week.

The bicycle gap is $360 − $120 = $240. Dividing by twelve gives $20 weekly. Each calculation works alone, but together they require $40 a week. Sam has only $35. This is the central planning check: add all contributions sharing one budget before declaring either plan feasible.

### Change contributions when a goal is finished

A plan need not use the same contribution forever. Once the jacket reaches its target after week six, its $20 weekly allocation becomes available for another purpose. You can redirect money already in the budget without inventing extra income. A timeline helps reveal when that becomes possible.

Use three lines in a plan: **opening balance + scheduled contributions = balance at the checkpoint**. Check both each goal and each week's total. A final balance that looks right is insufficient if the plan overspends along the way.

### Give emergency money a distinct purpose

Saving often supports near-term purchases or unexpected costs where access and stability matter. Investing can support longer-term goals, but values may change and money may not be readily available on a particular date. Do not solve a six-week shortfall by assuming an investment will rise by the exact missing amount.

A separate emergency reserve is not “unused” money merely because it has no purchase date. It has a different job. If a real emergency requires using it, the plan should record that decision and later review replenishment. This case deliberately keeps it separate so the two purchase goals can be evaluated honestly.

**Optional reference note:** FCAC's *Setting savings and investment goals* connects a goal's time horizon with appropriate planning. Read September 28, 2026. The on-page board and weekly note supply all required facts; you do not need to browse products.

**Hint:** Fund the earlier non-movable deadline first, then calculate what remains each week and when that allocation becomes available again.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Keep both goals within $35 a week

For weeks 1–6, put $20 into the jacket goal and $15 into the bicycle goal. Each week totals $35. The jacket reaches $60 + 6 × $20 = **$180** on time. The bicycle reaches $120 + 6 × $15 = **$210**.

After the jacket is funded, direct up to $35 weekly to the bicycle. Weeks 7–10 add $140, bringing it to $350. Only $10 more is needed in week 11, so the bicycle reaches $360 then. The remaining $25 from that week's $35 can stay unallocated for a later decision; it is not silently spent or counted in both goals.

**Completed plan:** “Save $20 for the jacket and $15 for the bicycle in each of weeks 1–6. The jacket is fully funded at week 6. Then save $35 for the bicycle in weeks 7–10 and the last $10 in week 11. Both deadlines are met without using the $120 emergency reserve or exceeding $35 in any week.”

The first pair of separate $20 plans was not wrong arithmetic; it was incomplete coordination. The revised plan keeps the deadlines and shows how contributions move after a goal is met.

## 5. Practise with support

### What if only $25 is available each week?

Keep Sam's starting balances, prices and deadlines, but change the weekly amount to **$25 in each of weeks 1–15**. The bicycle's original deadline is still week 12, but Sam can move it to a later week. Protect the jacket deadline: put $20 weekly toward it in weeks 1–6. Put the remaining money toward the bicycle, then direct all $25 weekly to the bicycle in weeks 7–12.

Enter **Weekly bicycle contribution in weeks 1–6 ($)**, **Bicycle balance at week 12 ($)** and **Bicycle shortfall at week 12 ($)**. Write **A feasible change to the bicycle plan**, two sentences. Use **Check the timeline**.

Correct amounts are $5, $300 and $60. The bicycle gets $120 + 6 × $5 + 6 × $25 = $300. If you enter $360, you have assumed extra money that the changed case does not supply. If you allocate $20 to each goal, your $40 weekly total exceeds $25. If you use the emergency reserve, you have changed a protected condition rather than solved this plan.

**Model:** “The bicycle has $300 at week 12 and is $60 short. With $25 available weekly after that, week 13 brings it to $325, week 14 to $350 and a final $10 in week 15 reaches $360. I would move the flexible bicycle deadline to week 15 while keeping the jacket funded at week 6.”

A lower-cost bicycle that meets the need could be another route only if its price and suitability are confirmed. You cannot simply relabel $300 as the original $360 target. Revise your proposed change accordingly.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — Which is a complete savings goal?

A. “Have more money someday.” B. “Never spend.” C. “Buy anything on sale.” D. “Set aside the remaining $120 for a $180 jacket by the sixth weekly deposit.”

Answer D. A lacks amount and date; B is a rule rather than a purpose; C does not define a target; D includes purpose, gap and timing.

### Q2 — Why did the two separate $20 plans fail together?

A. Their $40 combined contribution exceeded $35 available. B. Division never works for saving. C. Sam had no opening balances. D. All goals must share the same deadline.

Answer A. A checks the shared budget; B rejects a useful calculation; C contradicts the board; D invents a restriction.

### Q3 — What makes the original revised plan feasible?

A. Counting the emergency reserve twice. B. Assuming a guaranteed investment gain. C. Redirecting the jacket contribution after that goal is funded. D. Ignoring the bicycle deadline.

Answer C. A and B invent resources; C uses existing money at a later stage; D is unnecessary because the plan meets week 12.

### Q4 — Why keep the emergency reserve separate?

A. It can never be used for anything. B. It is allocated to unexpected essential costs, not these purchases. C. It is not real money. D. It guarantees no emergency will occur.

Answer B. A overstates the restriction; B identifies its purpose in this case; C and D are false implications.

### Q5 — What does a week-12 shortfall establish?

A. The learner is bad with money. B. A price will automatically fall. C. A loan must be taken. D. At least one amount, contribution, resource or flexible deadline needs review.

Answer D. A moralizes an arithmetic gap; B and C assume solutions; D states what planning must address.

### Q6 — Why is a near-term goal unsuitable for a plan relying on a precise market gain?

A. The gain is uncertain and may not be available at the deadline. B. All investments always lose. C. Savings have no purpose. D. The target amount does not matter.

Answer A. A matches the timing problem; B is an unsupported universal claim; C and D ignore goal planning.

## 7. Apply independently

### Lena's fee deadline

Lena, a fictional learner, must pay a **$144 activity fee at the end of week 8**, immediately after that week's deposit. Nothing is saved for the fee yet. **$18 weekly** is available after other costs. Lena also wants to save **$6 weekly for a hobby purchase**, but that purchase has no fixed date and nothing is saved for it yet. No other money, interest, discount or payment extension is supplied.

Enter **Weekly amount needed for the fee ($)** and **Fee balance at week 8 ($)**. Write **Lena's eight-week saving instruction**, 3–4 sentences explaining what happens to the hobby goal, why the plan fits and when to review it.

**Complete model:** “The fee requires $144 ÷ 8 = $18 each week, using the whole available amount. After eight deposits, $144 is ready for the deadline. I would pause the optional $6 hobby contribution for those eight weeks because saving for both would require $24 weekly. Once the fee is paid, Lena can review the hobby target and redirect the available weekly money.”

Choosing to keep the hobby allocation without another confirmed change leaves the fee short: only $12 weekly would remain, totalling $96. Identify the consequence rather than pretending both fit. Compare and revise.
