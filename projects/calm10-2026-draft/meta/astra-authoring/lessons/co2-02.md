# CO2-02 — Stop, identify and control a hazard

Authoring version: `2026-09-28.astra.1`. Assessment reference A2. Fictional worksite records; official Alberta source records checked September 28, 2026. This simulation is not chemical training or a supervised work experience.

## 1. Start here

On a first shift, you may want to show that you can handle a task without slowing anyone down. But a request does not supply the information, equipment or training needed to do it safely. Knowing when to pause is a useful workplace skill.

You will help Devin respond to an unidentified cleaning liquid, practise a follow-up when a coworker offers reassurance, and then create a response plan for a different equipment hazard. Your final product explains the observation, immediate action, report and conditions needed before work resumes.

Your route is **Meet the situation and read the documents → Learn the method → Follow a complete example → Practise with support → Check your understanding → Apply independently → Review and complete**. Read at your own pace; a useful pause is after the example. Responses save automatically. The guided activity and six questions are practice. Only your independent response plan and review sign-offs complete this lesson.

**Vocabulary.** A hazard is a source or condition that could cause harm. Risk concerns how harm could occur and how serious or likely it might be. A control is a measure that removes or reduces exposure to a hazard. A procedure is an agreed way to do a task safely. A concern report states what was observed and what action is needed. Reporting does not require proving a chemical's identity or a legal violation.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

Devin is a new employee at fictional Brookside Community Supplies in Alberta. At 4:10 p.m. on October 6, 2026, supervisor Jo asks Devin to clean a work surface beside a storage area. Devin has not started. The site is provincially regulated.

### Document A — Observation and training record

| What the record establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|
| Jo pointed to a container of liquid with no readable label. | Its identity, concentration or hazard classification. |
| The room feels stuffy; Devin does not know whether the ventilation is operating adequately for this product. | A measured exposure level or proof that the air is toxic. |
| Devin has not received the product information, chemical-use training or procedure for this cleaning task. | Which gloves, ventilation or other controls would be suitable. |
| Jo is helping another worker in the front area. Devin can reach Jo from the clear doorway without handling the container. | Permission to improvise while Jo is busy. |
| Devin has not opened, carried, mixed, smelled deliberately or used the liquid. No injury or visible spill is reported in this written case. | Any reason to perform spill cleanup. |

### Document B — Supplied worksite instructions

> If an assigned task lacks safety information or training, pause it and tell supervisor Jo. Stay out of the affected area when its safety is uncertain. From a safe place, warn another person who may enter; do not take an untrained action to block, move or clean a hazard. Jo will arrange a competent person's assessment and controls. If immediate serious danger develops, move to safety and follow the site's emergency procedure. Devin's orientation identifies the clear front exit and the outside assembly point. This case involves no current injury or emergency alarm.

These are fictional site instructions for the scenario. They do not provide a method for handling any real chemical.

### Document C — In-course rights and responsibilities record

**Government of Alberta, “Young worker rights and responsibilities,” sections “Workplace safety,” “Job training,” and “Emergency training and equipment”; “Employer responsibilities,” “Overview” and “Employing youth.” Checked September 28, 2026.**

Workers have rights to hazard information, participation in safety and refusal of dangerous work. Workers should report unsafe conditions, follow safe procedures and ask for training they lack. Employers must provide the skills, information, competent supervision and safety arrangements needed for the work. Learners still developing the necessary skills require direct supervision by someone who has them. Chemical work requires special training; equipment alone does not replace instruction.

**Government of Alberta, “Refuse dangerous work,” “How it works” and “Worker obligations.” Checked September 28, 2026.**

At provincially regulated worksites, the refusal right concerns a reasonable belief in an undue hazard: a serious, immediate threat actually observed or experienced. A worker should stop the dangerous work and promptly report what they refuse and why. The employer investigates and addresses it. Unless fixed immediately, an investigation report is provided. Safe alternative work the person can reasonably do may be assigned. Unresolved danger can be taken to OHS. Exercising the legal right is protected from reprisal.

This record explains a process; it does not decide that every uncertainty automatically meets the legal threshold. A worker can ask for missing information and training before reaching a legal conclusion.

### Read the documents

**Which sentence states Devin's evidence accurately?** Choose, then select **Check document reading**.

- A. “The container definitely holds a corrosive acid.” Feedback: Its identity and classification are unknown.
- B. “A spill has already injured a coworker.” Feedback: Neither a spill nor an injury is in the written record.
- C. “The container has no readable label, and Devin lacks the product information and task training.” **Correct.** These supplied facts justify pausing to obtain information and controls.
- D. “Jo approved any cleaning method Devin chooses.” Feedback: The site instruction says to pause and report when information or training is missing.

## 3. Learn the method

### Describe what you know without testing the hazard

Observation is useful when it makes the concern concrete: a label cannot be read, training has not occurred, or insulation is visibly damaged. You do not need to get closer, touch an object or deliberately smell a substance to make your report more convincing. Information gathering must not create the exposure you are trying to avoid.

Separate the observation from the possible harm. “The product is unidentified” is established. “Using an unidentified product without instructions could expose someone to harm” explains the concern. “This is definitely a poisonous gas” makes a claim the documents cannot support. Cautious wording can be specific and urgent without exaggeration.

### Use rights as practical actions

The **right to know** connects to requesting the identity, hazard information, training and procedure. The **right to participate** connects to reporting the condition and contributing information during its assessment. The **right to refuse dangerous work** has an Alberta process and threshold, described in Document C. It is more than walking away silently: the employer needs to know which work is being refused and why.

Worker and employer responsibilities fit together. Devin must report and avoid unsafe improvisation. Jo and the employer must arrange appropriate information, assessment, supervision and controls. Devin does not become the chemical expert merely because Devin noticed a problem.

### Choose the immediate action before the final fix

An immediate action keeps exposure from increasing while a competent person works out the fix. For Devin, that means not handling the liquid, staying clear and reaching Jo from the doorway. The final control depends on information Devin does not have. It might involve a verified product, a different task or another trained worker; this lesson cannot select gloves or chemical procedures for an unknown substance.

Use the four-step image as a reminder, with this complete text equivalent:

1. **Identify the potential hazard:** name the observable condition and the information gap.
2. **Pause and avoid guessing:** do not handle or test the hazard; stay clear when needed.
3. **Ask the responsible person:** report promptly and request information, training and controls.
4. **Reassess the remaining risk:** verify the responsible assessment and safe procedure; report unresolved danger and use the applicable refusal process where warranted.

“Reassess” does not mean giving yourself permission because the room looks normal. Work can resume only when the hazard has been addressed and the worker has the competence and controls required for the assigned work. Replacing Devin with another equally untrained person merely transfers the concern.

### Close the loop

A useful handoff identifies **who will act, what must be checked and how the worker will learn the result**. “Someone will sort it out” does not tell the next shift what is unresolved. In a formal refusal, read the employer's investigation report and raise remaining concerns through the process. OHS is the external safety route; Employment Standards deals with matters such as wages and employment hours.

**Optional first-step hint:** “Write one observed fact, one thing still unknown and one action Devin can take from the clear doorway. Avoid naming the chemical.”

**Optional supplied video — A first-shift hazard.** Use the player if a narrated example helps. You may also read the written equivalent beside it. ## 4. Follow a complete example

I begin with Devin's actual position: the work has not started, and Jo is reachable from a clear doorway. That makes it possible to pause and report without handling the container. I do not invent a spill because a general safety illustration happens to show one.

My observation is the unreadable label and missing training. My information request follows from those gaps: product identity, hazard information, approved procedure and appropriate instruction. “Give me any gloves” would jump to equipment before anyone has identified what the equipment needs to protect against.

Here is Devin's complete report and follow-up plan:

> Jo, at 4:10 p.m. I found that the cleaning container beside the storage area has no readable label. I have not been given the product information or training for this task, and I cannot confirm that the ventilation is suitable. I have not handled the liquid. I am pausing the cleaning and staying at the clear doorway. Please arrange a competent person's assessment and explain the identified product, safe procedure, controls and training before assigning this task. I can warn someone approaching from this safe position and do another safe task that I am trained to do. I will record who takes responsibility and ask for the assessment outcome. If serious danger remains, I will promptly state the dangerous work I am refusing and follow the Alberta process, including OHS help if unresolved.

This uses the right to know and participate immediately. It also identifies the refusal route without pretending a classroom paragraph has already made a legal finding. “Use a small amount to see whether it works” is an unsafe alternative because the proposed test itself could cause exposure.

## 5. Practise with support

The observation, location and training record stay the same. At 4:12 p.m., coworker Cam says, “I used that container yesterday. It is probably the usual cleaner.” Jo has not yet assessed the product. No label, training or procedure has been supplied.

Construct a brief follow-up in these saved fields:

1. **What does Cam's comment establish, and what remains unknown?** Write one or two sentences distinguishing the comment from verified identity and controls.
2. **What will Devin say and do now?** Draft two or three sentences to Jo from the safe doorway, including the paused task and a specific request.
3. **What must be confirmed before Devin performs the task?** Name the information, competence and control conditions, plus the follow-up route if danger remains.

Controls: **Show guided hint**, **Compare with the model**, **Revise my response**. Hint: “A report that someone used a container yesterday is evidence of what they said. It does not establish today's contents or Devin's training.”

Comparison feedback: “Look for a continued pause, a report to Jo and a request tied to missing information. If you wrote 'safe because Cam used it,' replace that conclusion with the remaining uncertainties. If you assigned Devin to carry the container for inspection, revise the action so assessment is arranged without Devin handling it.”

**Complete matching model:**

1. “Cam says they used this container yesterday. That does not verify its current contents, ventilation needs or safe procedure, and Devin still has no task training.”
2. “Jo, I am still pausing the cleaning because the container has no readable label and I have not received the product information or training. Cam's comment has not resolved those gaps. Please arrange assessment; I will stay clear and take a safe task I can do.”
3. “Before Devin does the work, the product and hazards need to be identified, the procedure and controls established, and the required training/supervision provided. Devin should receive the outcome and report remaining concerns; serious immediate danger is addressed through the Alberta refusal process and OHS if unresolved.”

Select **Revise my response** to return to the fields. Your task is a report and a decision boundary, not a cleanup plan.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1

Implementation key: `practice:co2-02:q1`.

Which action improves the evidence without exposing Devin to the unknown liquid?

- A. Smell it closely to identify the product. Feedback: Smelling deliberately can create exposure and cannot verify identity safely.
- B. Report the unreadable label and missing training from the clear doorway. **Correct.** Both facts are already known without handling the container.
- C. Mix a drop with the usual cleaner. Feedback: Mixing unknown substances may create a hazardous reaction.
- D. Carry it to Jo so the report is more convincing. Feedback: Handling it creates an unnecessary risk; Jo can arrange assessment.

### Q2

Implementation key: `practice:co2-02:q2`.

Which request most directly uses the right to know?

- A. “Please identify the product and provide its hazard information and the training for this task.” **Correct.** It asks for information needed to work safely.
- B. “Can someone promise nothing will happen?” Feedback: A promise does not supply hazard information or controls.
- C. “Can you give the task to another new worker?” Feedback: That transfers the information gap.
- D. “Can we avoid recording the concern?” Feedback: Avoiding a record does not resolve the hazard.

### Q3

Implementation key: `practice:co2-02:q3`.

What does the Alberta source mean by an undue hazard for the refusal right?

- A. Any task the worker finds inconvenient. Feedback: Inconvenience is not the stated threshold.
- B. Only a hazard that has already caused an injury. Feedback: Waiting for an injury is not required.
- C. A rumour about an unrelated workplace. Feedback: The definition concerns a threat actually observed or experienced at the worksite.
- D. A serious and immediate threat to health and safety actually observed or experienced at the worksite. **Correct.** Apply this specific threshold and the reporting process.

### Q4

Implementation key: `practice:co2-02:q4`.

What is missing from “I am leaving this task because it is unsafe” as a complete handoff?

- A. An accusation about Jo's intention. Feedback: Intent is not established and is not needed to describe the concern.
- B. Devin's guess about the chemical formula. Feedback: Guessing identity weakens the report and may encourage unsafe action.
- C. Prompt notice of the specific work, observed concern and needed response. **Correct.** The recipient needs enough detail to locate and address the issue.
- D. A promise to clean it without training later. Feedback: Delaying untrained handling does not make it safe.

### Q5

Implementation key: `practice:co2-02:q5`.

Jo offers another task while the concern is assessed. Which response fits the record?

- A. Accept any alternative, even without the necessary skills. Feedback: Alternative work must be safe and within what the worker can reasonably do.
- B. Consider the task if it is safe and Devin can reasonably perform it. **Correct.** A paused hazardous task does not prevent suitable safe work.
- C. Return to the liquid because an alternative exists. Feedback: The original concern has not been resolved.
- D. Ask another untrained worker to handle the liquid first. Feedback: That exposes someone else to the same unresolved concern.

### Q6

Implementation key: `practice:co2-02:q6`.

Which follow-up best closes the loop?

- A. Assume silence means someone fixed it. Feedback: Silence does not identify an assessment or control.
- B. Resume when a coworker says the room looks normal. Feedback: Appearance does not establish safe information, competence and controls.
- C. Delete the report so the next shift will not worry. Feedback: That hides an unresolved condition.
- D. Confirm the responsible assessment, controls and required training; review the report and raise remaining danger. **Correct.** This checks what actually changed before resuming.

## 7. Apply independently

### A new hazard: the damaged vacuum cord

Imani is a new employee in fictional Alder Display Shop, an Alberta provincially regulated workplace. Create a **safe-response handoff** using the following complete records. Do not perform a real inspection, repair or test.

**Document D — Observation, October 9, 2026, 5:20 p.m.** Imani is asked to vacuum a dry sales-floor area. The vacuum is switched off and already unplugged. Before touching it, Imani sees a split in the cord insulation with metal visible. No one has been shocked; there is no fire, smoke, water or immediate injury. Imani stands in a clear area and has not moved, plugged in or tested the machine. Supervisor Ren is at the nearby service desk and can be reached safely.

**Document E — Training and instructions.** Imani has ordinary vacuum-use instruction, but no electrical repair training or authority to repair equipment. The shop's written procedure says: “Do not use damaged electrical equipment. Report the equipment and location to Ren; Ren arranges removal from service and assessment by a competent person. Workers must not improvise repairs. A replacement may be used only after its condition and safe use are confirmed.” A coworker says, “Wrap it in tape and finish quickly.” No replacement has yet been supplied.

Write the handoff in four fields:

- **Observed condition and possible harm** — state the specific condition and explain why energizing or using the device could expose someone to harm. Keep observations separate from predictions.
- **Immediate action and report** — draft what Imani should say to Ren, identifying time, place, task and what has been paused. Include an action that avoids exposure.
- **Information, control and competence before resuming** — explain who arranges the response and what must be confirmed. Address the tape suggestion.
- **Unresolved-danger follow-up** — connect the concern to at least two worker rights and name the Alberta safety route if serious danger remains. One short paragraph is enough.

Success means a specific report, no testing or improvised repair, appropriate responsibility for controls, and a follow-up that does not silently pass damaged equipment to another worker. You may describe a safe replacement or another safe task; do not assume either has already been supplied.

**Compare with a complete model**

> At 5:20 p.m. on October 9, Imani observed split insulation with metal visible on the unplugged vacuum cord in the sales-floor area. Using or energizing damaged electrical equipment could cause harm; no shock or fire has occurred in this case. “Ren, I have paused the vacuuming and have not touched, plugged in or tested the machine. The cord is visibly damaged. Please arrange its removal from service and competent assessment under the shop procedure.” Imani stays clear and can warn someone approaching from a safe position. Taping is not an authorized repair, and Imani has no repair training. Ren must arrange assessment; before Imani resumes vacuuming, suitable safe equipment and the instructions/competence for it must be confirmed. Imani can ask for that information through the right to know and report the concern through participation. If directed to do work reasonably believed to pose an undue hazard, Imani should promptly explain the refusal and follow Alberta's process, including OHS help if unresolved. Safe alternative work may be considered. Nothing in this report requires Imani to diagnose the electrical fault.
