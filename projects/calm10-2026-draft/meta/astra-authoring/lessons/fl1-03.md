# FL1-03 — Build a flexible budget

Complete script for lead review. Version `2026-09-28.astra.1`. Canonical course unchanged.

## 1. Start here

### Will the money be there on the day it is needed?

Sam's planned monthly spending is lower than monthly income. That sounds reassuring, until several bills arrive before the second payment. A budget needs both totals and dates.

You will read a money calendar, find its lowest balance and revise a plan using confirmed options. Your final product is a dated plan for Nico that covers the stated commitments without spending money before it arrives. All budgets are fictional and all amounts are Canadian dollars. A calculator is available; the challenge is deciding what belongs in the calculation.

**How to complete this lesson:** Read **Sam's October money calendar**. Learn **Two checks for a workable budget**, then follow the complete revision. In **Handle an unexpected repair**, change the plan and check its balances. Answer six questions, then complete **Nico's November plan**, compare the model and sign off. You may pause after Sam's revision; saved practice and final work remain separate.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Sam's October money calendar

Sam, a fictional adult learner, starts October with **$80 already in the account**. Net income arrives as $450 on October 1 and $450 on October 15. Income is available before any outgoing payment on the same date. The $80 is an opening balance, not another monthly wage. No overdraft, credit or other income is available in this case.

**Planned payments**

| Date | Payment | Amount | What can change? |
|---|---|---:|---|
| October 1 | Room contribution | $420 | A confirmed no-fee option permits $210 on October 1 and $210 on October 16. |
| October 3 | Food for first half of month | $90 | Required amount/date in this case. |
| October 8 | Phone | $45 | Required amount/date. |
| October 10 | Transit | $80 | Required amount/date. |
| October 16 | Food for second half | $90 | Required amount/date. |
| October 20 | Course supplies | $60 | Required amount/date. |
| October 24 | Transfer to savings | $70 | Can be reduced for this month. |
| October 28 | Recreation | $35 | Can be reduced for this month. |

Housing and food amounts are exercise assumptions, not suggested living-cost estimates. Sam wants to finish the month with at least the original $80 account buffer. Transfers to savings move money out of this spending account but do not destroy it; keep them in the cash-flow calculation because that money is no longer available here.

**Check the calendar:** Which money can Sam use on October 8?

A. Both $450 payments, because they fall in the same month. B. Only money received by October 8, after earlier payments. C. Any amount below total annual earnings. D. The second payment if Sam has already written it into a budget.

Feedback: B respects availability. A and D count a future payment too early. C ignores both this account's dates and its actual receipts.

## 3. Learn the method

### Two checks for a workable budget

A **budget** is a plan for receiving, using and setting aside money. A **cash-flow calendar** puts those movements in date order. Start with the opening balance, add money when it arrives and subtract payments when they leave. The number after each movement is a **running balance**.

First check the whole period: income minus expenses and planned saving. A positive result is a surplus; a negative result is a shortfall. A surplus does not mean that every day is covered. If the second payment arrives after a bill, that later income cannot pay the earlier bill without some arrangement that actually makes money available.

Second check timing: calculate the running balance through the month. The **lowest balance** is the smallest amount reached after a movement. In this case any negative amount signals that the plan uses money unavailable in the account. It is a planning warning, not a fee calculation. No overdraft terms have been supplied.

**Calendar method:** opening balance + income received − payments made = balance at this point. Repeat from the new balance, not from zero. A simple sequence of dated balances makes a timing problem visible even when the monthly total looks fine.

### Separate a feasible change from a wish

Some amounts are fixed by the case; others can change. Food and transit are required here. Removing them produces a lower total while leaving Sam unable to carry out the stated plan. A confirmed payment arrangement can change a date without changing total cost. A request that has not been accepted cannot be treated as an agreement.

**Overextending** means committing more money than the plan can support, including repayments that leave too little for other obligations. A small individual payment can contribute to overextension when several commitments fall together. Check their combined effects rather than judging one price in isolation.

A buffer is money left accessible for variation. It has a job even while unspent. However, a buffer target can sometimes need review: preserving it at all costs is not more important than a real essential need. State the trade-off rather than pretending there is no constraint.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Repair Sam's timing problem

The planned payments total $420 + $90 + $45 + $80 + $90 + $60 + $70 + $35 = **$890**. Income totals $900, so the month adds $10 to the original $80. Ending with $90 looks possible on totals alone.

Now I follow the dates. After the first income, Sam has $530. Room payment leaves $110; food leaves $20; phone takes the plan to **−$25**; transit takes it to **−$105**. The second income comes too late to prevent those shortfalls. I have found the problem before guessing at a solution.

The room agreement already supplies a permitted alternative. Splitting that payment produces this completed calendar:

| Date/event | Balance after event |
|---|---:|
| Opening | $80 |
| October 1 income | $530 |
| October 1 room, $210 | $320 |
| October 3 food | $230 |
| October 8 phone | $185 |
| October 10 transit | $105 |
| October 15 income | $555 |
| October 16 room, $210, then food, $90 | $255 |
| October 20 supplies | $195 |
| October 24 saving | $125 |
| October 28 recreation | $90 |

The account stays nonnegative. The lowest balance during the month after the first payment arrives is $90; including the starting point, the minimum is the opening $80. The important pre-payday balance is $105. State which interval you mean when reporting a minimum.

**Completed recommendation:** “Use the confirmed $210/$210 room arrangement. It leaves $105 just before the October 15 income, covers all listed payments and ends at $90. Total monthly spending and saving remain $890. I changed timing using an available agreement, not the amount of income.”

“Just pay the phone late” is unsupported: no extension is supplied. The split-room option is defensible because its dates and no-fee permission are written in the case.

## 5. Practise with support

### Handle an unexpected repair

Keep the split-room plan. A necessary **$60 repair is now due October 6**, and Sam must pay it then. Sam still wants an $80 ending balance. All other required payments remain the same; saving and recreation are adjustable. This is the only new expense.

First choose: **delete required transit / reduce flexible amounts by at least $50 / count the October 15 income twice**. Use **Check my change**. Reducing flexible amounts by $50 repairs the ending-buffer gap; deleting transit violates the case; duplicating income invents money.

Enter **Revised saving transfer ($)**, **Revised recreation ($)** and **My repair plan and two balance checks**. You may use a combined saving/recreation total of $55 or less; explain any reduction. Check the balance immediately after October 10 and the month-end balance.

**Hint:** The original ending balance is $90. The repair takes $60 from it. How much must remain unspent to bring $30 back to the $80 target?

**Model:** “I reduce saving from $70 to $35 and recreation from $35 to $20, keeping $50 in the account. After October 6's repair, the balance is $170; phone leaves $125 and transit leaves $45. The later payments, with the smaller transfers, bring the ending balance to $80. Required costs and the repair are covered. Saving grows more slowly this month and recreation is reduced.”

The numeric check accepts any nonnegative saving/recreation values totalling at most $55, within their original caps. It reports the resulting ending balance: $30 + ($105 − revised saving − revised recreation). If their total is $60, feedback explains that ending $75 misses the target by $5. If a field exceeds its original amount, explain the supplied adjustment is a reduction. If either value is blank, request it without treating blank as zero. The October 10 balance remains $45 regardless of these later reductions.

Revise your plan so both timing and the chosen trade-off are explicit. Do not describe lower saving as money already earned.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — Why did the first plan fail despite a $10 monthly surplus?

A. Savings are not money. B. October has no second payment. C. Some payments were due before enough income arrived. D. Every positive budget is wrong.

Answer C. A misstates saving; B contradicts the record; C identifies timing; D generalizes from one flawed schedule.

### Q2 — How should the opening $80 appear?

A. As the starting balance once. B. As income on both paydays. C. As a new monthly wage. D. As an expense.

Answer A. A carries existing money forward; B and C double-count it; D subtracts an available asset.

### Q3 — What makes the room-payment revision usable?

A. Sam would prefer it. B. All landlords always allow it. C. It erases half the room cost. D. The supplied agreement confirms both dates and no fee.

Answer D. A alone is not an agreement; B invents a universal rule; C ignores the second instalment; D uses a specific documented option.

### Q4 — With the repair, why does cutting $50 restore the ending target?

A. It removes the repair. B. $90 − $60 + $50 = $80. C. It increases the second wage. D. It proves there will never be another surprise.

Answer B. A denies a required expense; B reconciles the balance; C invents income; D goes beyond a one-month plan.

### Q5 — Which is a warning of overextension?

A. A budget lists every payment date. B. A savings goal exists. C. Combined commitments use more money than is available when due. D. A person compares two options.

Answer C. A, B and D are planning actions; C identifies a commitment problem.

### Q6 — Sam writes “minimum $90” without stating the interval. What should be clarified?

A. Whether the $80 opening balance is included. B. Whether $90 means a year. C. Whether money has any units. D. Whether every expense can be cancelled.

Answer A. A makes the range of the calculation explicit; B changes the figure into a time; C ignores the dollar label; D is unrelated to defining a minimum.

## 7. Apply independently

### Nico's November plan

Nico starts with $30. Net income arrives **$560 on November 1** and **$340 on November 20**, before payments on those days. No credit or other income is available. Nico must finish with at least $30 accessible.

| Date | Payment | Amount |
|---|---|---:|
| November 2 | Room contribution | $300 |
| November 4 | First food purchase | $100 |
| November 10 | Required work boots, needed November 11 | $120 |
| November 12 | Phone | $45 |
| November 18 | Transit | $80 |
| November 22 | Second food purchase | $100 |
| November 26 | Saving transfer | $60 |
| November 30 | Leisure | $40 |

**Confirmed shop option:** the same boots can be collected November 10 for $60 that day and $60 on November 24, with no interest or fee. This is a payment obligation, not a discount. No other dates or required amounts can change. Saving and leisure can be reduced if your plan explains why.

Create **Nico's dated plan**. In **Payment option**, choose full payment / confirmed two-payment option. In **My dated balances and recommendation**, show the balance after each event from November 1 through 30, identify the tightest point, give the ending balance and explain whether the requirements are met. A compact list is fine. Complete **Balance after November 18 ($)** and **Ending balance ($)** to highlight your checks.

**Complete comparison model:** “I use the confirmed two-payment boot option. Balances are: opening $30; November 1 income $590; room $290; first food $190; first boot payment $130; phone $85; transit $5; November 20 income $345; second food $245; second boot payment $185; saving $125; leisure $85. The lowest balance is $5 on November 18. The ending $85 exceeds the $30 target and all required purchases happen on time. The plan is feasible with little room for a surprise before November 20. Paying the full $120 on November 10 would instead leave −$55 on November 18.”

Reducing later saving cannot by itself repair the earlier −$55; explain the dates in any alternative. This supplied plan does not assume the shop's arrangement is typical of real sellers.
