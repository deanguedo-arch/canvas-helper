# FL3-06 — Revise a plan when life changes

## 1. Start here

### Keep the record; change the next action

A plan can be reasonable when written and still need revision after an unexpected cost. You will separate what has already happened from what can change next. The task is to recalculate from the actual balance, explain the adjustment and set a new review point.

Your final product is a revision note for a fictional saving plan after a necessary expense. It will show the current balance, remaining gap and feasible future contributions. No personal loss, income or account information is required.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Ari's original plan and transaction record

Ari is fictional and wants **$350 by the end of month 6** for a purchase. The plan began with **$200**, then scheduled **$25 at each of six month-ends**. No interest or fee applies. The deadline was initially treated as fixed.

**What actually happened:**

| Event | Change | Balance |
|---|---:|---:|
| Start | Opening $200 | $200 |
| End of month 1 | Deposit $25 | $225 |
| End of month 2 | Deposit $25 | $250 |
| Immediately after month 2 | Necessary repair, withdrawal $80 | $170 |

**New budget information:** Ari can normally save **$30 in each remaining month** after other costs. A confirmed temporary change can instead free **up to $45 monthly for months 3–6**, without missing essential costs or required payments. There is no other money available. The repair has already been paid and is not refundable in the case.

**Read the record:** Which balance should the revised plan start from immediately after the repair?

A. $170. B. $250. C. $350. D. $200.

A includes both deposits and the withdrawal. B ignores the repair; C is the target, not current money; D ignores the actual history.

## 3. Learn the method

### Update from the present balance

A **revision** changes a future action in response to evidence. It does not rewrite earlier transactions. Start with the actual balance, subtract it from the target and count the deposits still available before the deadline. This creates a new gap and a new required contribution.

For Ari, two months have passed. Only four month-end deposits remain before month six. Using six deposits in the revised calculation would pretend time already elapsed is still available. Similarly, restarting at the old $200 balance would erase both saving progress and the repair cost.

### Change a constraint openly

There are several possible levers: contribution, target amount, deadline or a confirmed resource. Each has consequences. A higher contribution must fit the revised budget. A lower target must still meet the need. A later date must actually be flexible. A resource must be confirmed rather than hoped for.

Sometimes no combination satisfies every original condition. Naming the conflict is better planning than hiding it. A revision note should say which condition changes and why. It should also distinguish a temporary adjustment from an ongoing one so that next month's plan does not accidentally repeat a one-time cost.

### Review goals as circumstances change

Education, work, relocation, family responsibilities and retirement can change a person's purposes, time horizons and ability to take risk. A plan made for one life stage may need different priorities later. Reviewing does not mean changing every goal whenever a headline appears; it means checking relevant evidence against the decision's assumptions.

For investments, a market value change can affect a plan without being a deposit or withdrawal. A $500 holding worth $450 has depreciated by $50; that record does not reveal whether it will recover. Keep contributions, withdrawals, fees and market changes distinct. A short-term bill should not rely on an assumed rebound.

A useful revision note follows **actual event → present balance → remaining need and time → feasible change → review trigger**. This is the second representation of the method; each part will appear in your final response.

**Optional reference:** FCAC's budget guidance recommends comparing a plan with actual income and spending and updating it as circumstances change. Read September 28, 2026. All required records and alternatives in this lesson are supplied.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Keep Ari's month-six deadline

After the repair, the balance is $170. The target gap is $350 − $170 = **$180**. With four deposits remaining, $180 ÷ 4 = **$45 monthly** is required.

The normal $30 amount would add only $120, ending at $290 and leaving $60 short. The supplied temporary budget change permits $45, so the revised plan can still meet the deadline. This conclusion relies on that confirmation; it does not assume every person can simply save more.

**Complete revision:** “The $80 repair leaves $170 after month two. To reach $350 at month six, Ari needs another $180 across four deposits, or $45 monthly. I would use the confirmed temporary adjustment for months 3–6 and check after month 3 that the first revised deposit happened and the $45 remains available. If it no longer fits, I would revisit a flexible date or target rather than erase the repair from the record.”

“Continue $25 and everything is fine” ignores the changed balance. “Start the original six-month plan again” changes the deadline without saying so. A complete revision preserves both the history and the condition being changed.

## 5. Practise with support

### Keep $30 monthly and move the date

Use a changed version: Ari cannot make the temporary $45 adjustment, but the purchase deadline can now move. The balance after month two is still $170, the target is still $350, and **$30 monthly** is available from month three onward. No interest or additional resource is supplied.

Enter **Deposits still needed** and **New completion month**. Write **My revised instruction**, two sentences stating what changes and what stays. Use **Check the revised date**.

Six $30 deposits provide the remaining $180. They occur in months 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8, so the new date is **month 8**. Feedback on month 6: six deposits are needed after month two, not from the beginning. Feedback on four deposits: four at $30 leave the plan $60 short. Feedback on six deposits of $45: that exceeds this changed version's available amount.

**Model:** “Keep the $350 target and actual $170 balance, but change the deadline from month 6 to month 8. Deposit $30 in each of months 3–8, and review after month 3 to confirm both the deposit and the new deadline remain workable.”

### Optional budget-shock experiment

A separate fictional monthly budget has income $600, essentials $400, flexible spending $80, required debt payment $50 and saving $70. It initially balances. A one-time **$60 repair** creates a gap. Load this example into **Test a budget change** only if you want the additional experiment.

The case confirms that up to $60 of this month's flexible spending can be postponed without affecting essentials. Choose **reduce flexible spending** and enter **$60 reduction**. The revised flexible amount becomes $20, and the budget balances while keeping debt and saving allocations. Choose a review note such as “return to the ordinary budget next month if the repair does not recur.” Reducing saving is another possible adjustment, but its goal and recovery date must be reconsidered. New borrowing cannot be treated as free income; no loan approval or terms are supplied here.

The optional experiment is separate from Ari's balances. Do not move its $600 income into Ari's savings plan. Compare and revise your required practice instruction.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — Which number starts a revision after a withdrawal?

A. The old target. B. The original opening balance regardless of events. C. A hoped-for future gain. D. The actual balance after recorded events.

Answer D. A is the desired finish; B erases history; C is unconfirmed; D describes the present resources.

### Q2 — Why count only four deposits after Ari's month-two repair for the original deadline?

A. Two of the original six months have already passed. B. Repairs always last four months. C. The interest rate is 4%. D. The target is $400.

Answer A. A uses the timeline; B–D invent unrelated facts.

### Q3 — What makes a higher contribution feasible?

A. Writing it in bold. B. Wanting the target more. C. A confirmed budget change that leaves required costs covered. D. Assuming another person will pay.

Answer C. A and B do not supply money; C checks the constraint; D invents support.

### Q4 — A one-time repair cost should be:

A. Removed from history. B. Recorded this month, with the next month's budget reviewed rather than automatically repeating it. C. Counted every month forever. D. Treated as investment growth.

Answer B. A hides an event; B preserves timing; C invents recurrence; D misclassifies the transaction.

### Q5 — An investment falls from $500 to $450 without a deposit or withdrawal. What is known?

A. It will recover next week. B. The investor withdrew $50. C. A lender owes a refund. D. Its recorded market value depreciated by $50; future value is unknown.

Answer D. A predicts without evidence; B contradicts the record; C invents an obligation; D names the change and limit.

### Q6 — What belongs in a complete revision note?

A. The changed fact, recalculated gap, feasible action and review trigger. B. Only “try harder.” C. A new target without explaining why. D. An invented guaranteed return.

Answer A. A makes the plan actionable; B is vague; C hides the decision; D replaces evidence with a promise.

## 7. Apply independently

### Devon's changed plan

Devon is fictional and wants **$360 by the end of month 6**. The original plan began with **$150**, then proposed $40 in months 1–5 and the last $10 in month 6. The first **three $40 deposits actually happened**, bringing the balance to **$270**. Immediately after month three, a necessary **$90 expense** was paid from the fund. It is not refundable.

Three monthly deposits remain before the fixed deadline. Devon's usual available saving is **$40 monthly**, but a supplied budget review confirms a temporary **$60 monthly** can be set aside in months 4–6 without missing other obligations. No interest, gifts or investment returns are supplied. The target price and deadline remain unchanged.

Enter **Balance after the expense ($)**, **Remaining target gap ($)** and **Required contribution for each remaining month ($)**. Write **Devon's revision note**, 4–5 sentences explaining the actual history, why the usual amount falls short, the confirmed adjustment and a review point.

**Complete model:** “The balance after three deposits is $270, and the $90 expense leaves $180. The remaining gap is $360 − $180 = $180 across months 4–6, requiring $60 monthly. Continuing the usual $40 would add only $120 and finish at $300, $60 short. I would use the confirmed temporary $60 allocation and check after month 4 that the first revised deposit occurred and the next two are still affordable. I would retain the expense in the record and revise the plan again if the available amount changes.”

The original month-six $10 deposit no longer fits the changed balance. Copying it from the old plan would miss the purpose of revision. Compare your note, then make any changes needed.
