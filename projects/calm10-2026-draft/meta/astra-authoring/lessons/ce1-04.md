## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Noor's decision note

Noor enjoys finding patterns in information and wants to explore database-related work. Noor can currently travel within 20 kilometres of the fictional community of Meadowcross. Moving is not included in the current plan. Noor has not yet researched training requirements. The immediate decision is whether the evidence supports further investigation, not whether to accept a job.

A **labour market** concerns work and workers: occupations, employment, vacancies and conditions affecting them. A **forecast** is an estimate about a specified period. A **vacancy** is an unfilled position; an advertisement is one way it may be publicized. An employment count is the number reported as employed in a defined group, not the number of openings.

## 3. Learn the method

### Attach a label to every number

A number without its measure can mislead. “2,470” might mean employed people, advertised vacancies or dollars; those would answer very different questions. Before using a number, identify **what**, **which occupation/group**, **where** and **when**. Also identify who published it and what the source says it does not cover.

Use a source label such as: “reported employment count; NOC 21223; Alberta; forecast source 2024–2026; ALIS page retrieved September 28, 2026.” That label is longer than a headline because it preserves the facts needed to interpret the figure.

### Keep three clocks separate

The **source period** is the span of time described by the data or forecast. The **update date** records a revision to a page or section. The **retrieval date** is when you read it. A forecast labelled 2024–2026 does not become a forecast for 2026–2028 when someone opens it in 2026.

Different dates on a page are a reason to label information carefully. Do not silently choose the newest date and attach it to every item. For a future real decision, the original source would need checking again. For this lesson, analyze the dated record as supplied.

### Compare questions before comparing answers

| Record | Question it can help answer | Question it cannot settle |
|---|---|---|
| Provincial forecast | What pattern was projected for this occupational group and period? | Will one person be hired in a particular town? |
| Employment count | How many people are reported as employed in the group? | How many vacancies are open today? |
| Local advertisement search | What advertisements did this search capture on its date? | What is every employer planning or hiring privately? |

Both a favourable forecast and a small local search can be accurate. They cover different geography, time and measures. A low advertisement count can reflect a small local market, search terms, sources checked or the search date. These are possible explanations to investigate, not facts the log has already established.

### Include the cost of living when interpreting wages

**Cost of living** means the expense of meeting everyday needs in a place and household situation: for example, housing, food, utilities and transportation. Childcare or other needs may also matter. A higher advertised wage does not automatically leave a person with more money after those costs. Hours, the length of a job and payroll deductions also affect the income available.

**Dated source record — checked September 28, 2026:** ALIS advises interpreting salary information alongside location and whether work is temporary. Its moving guide identifies housing availability and costs, transportation and childcare as questions to investigate. [Optional salary-context source](https://alis.alberta.ca/look-for-work/labour-market-information/separating-the-best-lmi-from-the-rest/) and [optional community-planning source](https://alis.alberta.ca/succeed-at-work/manage-change-and-transitions/plan-before-you-move-for-work-or-school/).

For Noor, a provincial opportunity label does not answer whether work within the stated travel area would be affordable. The supplied records contain no wage offer or household expense quotes, so they cannot establish a cost-of-living comparison. A later real comparison would need dated local costs and income on the same time basis. There is no extra calculation or outside search in this lesson.

### Turn uncertainty into a next step

A conditional conclusion says what the evidence supports now and which condition might change it. “Investigate further within the travel limit” is different from “there will definitely be a job.” It is also different from dismissing the occupation after one search.

Labour evidence is only part of a career decision. Noor's preparation, interest, transport and costs still matter. A source may suggest an opportunity while leaving those personal constraints unanswered. The goal is to organize evidence so a plan can be revised when new information arrives.

**Optional first-step hint:** Ask whether a number describes people already working or positions being advertised. If you cannot answer, reread the field heading before calculating anything.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Explain Noor's apparently conflicting records

I first identify Record N1 as Alberta group information. Its Hot label belongs to the stated three-year forecast period. The 2,470 figure is an employment count. Neither tells me how many entry opportunities are inside Noor's travel area.

Record N2 answers a narrower question: what two employer boards displayed using two search terms on September 24. It found two advertisements. I can preserve that fact without claiming it is the entire local market.

**Completed interpretation:** “Noor's records do not necessarily conflict. N1 is an Alberta forecast/group record for 2024–2026; it is not a count of Meadowcross vacancies or a prediction that Noor will be hired. N2 found two advertisements within the stated search limits on September 24. That small snapshot matters because Noor currently has a 20-kilometre travel limit, but it cannot establish every employer's future plans. I would continue investigating related entry roles and preparation using the supplied record limits. A later, broader search could change the picture. I would not recommend moving or committing to training from these two records alone.”

“There are 2,470 jobs waiting for Noor” changes employment into vacancies. “Two advertisements prove the occupation has no future” generalizes a small snapshot to every place and year. Both fail because their conclusions exceed their records.

## 5. Practise with support

### Add one new search date without inventing a trend

Noor repeats the same fictional search on October 1, 2026. The same area, search words and two boards are used. No open advertisements appear that day. The log does not establish whether September's positions were filled, withdrawn or advertised elsewhere.

Select the statement supported by the new log: **the whole occupation disappeared / this search found zero open advertisements on October 1 / both earlier jobs were definitely filled**. Use **Check the claim**. The second statement preserves the observation. The first expands to an occupation-wide claim. The third invents a reason for the change.

In **Observation with scope/date**, record what changed. In **What the two searches cannot establish**, explain the limit. In **A useful next comparison**, describe another piece of evidence that would help Noor's travel-limited plan. You are drafting a research step, not carrying it out outside the course.

**Optional hint:** Start “Using the same search limits…” and keep the reason for the change separate from the observation.

**Compare with the search-log model**

“Using the same search limits, Noor found two open advertisements on September 24 and none on October 1. This shows a change in the captured advertisements, not that the occupational group vanished or that we know what happened to each position. A further log across more employers, with the same area and dates recorded, would help test whether the first results reflect a narrow source list. Entry requirements for related roles would also help Noor judge which findings are usable.”

Revise in the same fields using **Return to my practice**. A useful next comparison should add information; simply opening the same record again without a new question would not resolve the limit.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — What is the best label for a provincial number of employed people?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-04:q1`.

A. Vacancies today. B. Guaranteed offers. C. Training seats. D. Employment count for the defined group and place.

Answer: D. A changes the measure. B invents personal outcomes. C describes education capacity. D preserves the meaning.

### Q2 — A 2024–2026 forecast is read in September 2026. Which period should the note record?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-04:q2`.

A. 2024–2026. B. 2026–2028 automatically. C. Any three years the reader prefers. D. Only the retrieval day, with no forecast period.

Answer: A. A preserves the source period. B resets it without evidence. C makes it arbitrary. D loses the period being described.

### Q3 — Why can few local advertisements coexist with a favourable provincial forecast?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-04:q3`.

A. Local searches always lie. B. Forecasts guarantee hidden openings. C. The records use different measures, places and time windows. D. A forecast counts only local advertisements.

Answer: C. A dismisses useful observations. B invents vacancies. C identifies the scope difference. D misdefines a forecast.

### Q4 — What should be done when one page uses a broader NOC group?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-04:q4`.

A. Treat every related role as identical. B. Keep the group label and limit with the claim. C. Remove the code from the note. D. Assume the number applies to one employer.

Answer: B. A ignores within-group variation. B makes the scope visible. C hides useful context. D narrows the data without evidence.

### Q5 — Which conclusion follows from Noor's October 1 search?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-04:q5`.

A. No open advertisements were captured by that search on that date. B. Every employer stopped hiring. C. Both earlier roles were definitely filled. D. No one in Alberta can enter this occupation.

Answer: A. D and B exceed the search coverage. C invents why advertisements disappeared. A reports the observation accurately.

### Q6 — Which plan uses labour evidence responsibly?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-04:q6`.

A. Ignore transport because the employment count is large. B. Commit because of one favourable label. C. Keep investigating preparation and local options while recording what could change the plan. D. Choose whichever number is largest.

Answer: C. C combines evidence and constraints. B treats a forecast as assurance. A removes a relevant condition. D compares size without meaning.

### Ellis compares two design directions

| E2: Web Designer, ALIS, Employment & Advancement | Hot; fewer than 1,500 employed in Alberta; 2021 NOC 21233, Web designers | Source period 2024–2026; section update March 31, 2022; retrieved September 28, 2026. Reported employment is not a vacancy count. |

[Optional Graphic Designer source](https://alis.alberta.ca/occinfo/occupations-in-alberta/occupation-profiles/graphic-designer/); [optional Web Designer source](https://alis.alberta.ca/occinfo/occupations-in-alberta/occupation-profiles/web-designer/).

**E3 — Fictional Lakeford log:** On September 26, 2026, a search of three fictional employer boards using the two occupation titles captured three distinct graphic-design advertisements and four distinct web-design advertisements inside Lakeford. Requirements were not yet recorded. No claim is made about unadvertised jobs, future openings or other employers.

Write **E1 annotation** and **E2 annotation**, each identifying measure, occupational group, place, source period, retrieval date and a limit. Then write **Ellis's evidence-based next step** in about 100–150 words. Explain why the employment counts, forecast labels and local advertisements cannot be ranked as though they were one measure. State what Ellis should investigate before choosing training. No outside search is required.

**Compare with a supported response**

“E1 is an Alberta group record for NOC 52120. Its employment count is 6,570 and its forecast label is Cool for 2024–2026; the section shows March 22, 2023 and was retrieved September 28, 2026. E2 covers NOC 21233 in Alberta, with fewer than 1,500 employed and a Hot label for the same source period; its section shows March 31, 2022 and the same retrieval date. Neither count is Lakeford vacancies. The larger employment count does not by itself establish stronger future demand. E3 is a limited one-day advertisement search, with three and four results, not complete local hiring evidence. Ellis can investigate both directions further. I would compare the advertised duties and entry requirements with Ellis's interests and preparation before selecting training. Another dated search could test the local picture, but these records do not guarantee an offer or make one direction automatically suitable.”

It is acceptable to prioritize one direction for further investigation if you identify a relevant reason and keep these limits. Revise your annotations or conclusion after comparing.
