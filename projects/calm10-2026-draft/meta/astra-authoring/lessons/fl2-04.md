# FL2-04 — Choose a repayment strategy you can sustain

## 1. Start here

### Give every repayment dollar a job

When two debts compete for the same money, “pay more” is not a complete plan. You need the balances, rates, required payments and a realistic amount available. You will compare two strategies, then test an offer to borrow more in the hope of earning a return.

Your final product is a repayment instruction for a fictional adult: allocate a monthly budget, explain a priority and test whether a proposed investment could cover its new loan. No borrowing, betting or investment account is involved.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Jo's two debt statements

Jo is a fictional adult with $120 a month available for debt payments after essential costs and a small emergency saving allocation. Both accounts are up to date. Neither has a fee or early-repayment penalty in this exercise. No additional purchases are made.

| Account | Opening debt | Fixed nominal annual rate | Required monthly payment |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | $300 | 8% | $25 |
| B | $900 | 19% | $45 |

The model posts interest monthly using annual rate/12, rounded to cents. Each required payment stays constant until that account's remaining balance plus interest is smaller. Unused payment money then moves to the other debt that month. The remaining part of the $120 budget is an extra payment. The full schedules are supplied; you do not need to derive them.

| Same $120 monthly budget | Highest-rate balance first | Smallest balance first |
|---|---:|---:|
| Months to clear both | 11 | 11 |
| Total interest | $96.35 | $108.65 |
| Total paid | $1,296.35 | $1,308.65 |

**Read the statements:** Before choosing an extra-payment priority, how much of the $120 is already needed for the two minimums?

A. $45. B. $70. C. $120. D. $50.

B is $25 + $45. A omits account A; C includes the extra money as though all of it were required; D is the money left after minimums, not the minimum total.

## 3. Learn the method

### Protect the minimums, then choose a priority

A **minimum payment** is the least required under that account's current terms. Choosing a priority for extra money does not give permission to skip another required payment. Jo first sets aside $70, leaving $50 to direct deliberately.

With **highest-rate-first repayment**, extra money goes to the debt with the higher interest rate while the other minimum continues. Each dollar of that balance removed avoids a relatively larger future charge under these fixed-rate assumptions. With **smallest-balance-first repayment**, extra money goes to the smaller debt. Clearing an account sooner may help someone stick to a plan, even if this costs more interest. Compare the actual schedules rather than assuming one strategy always changes the payoff month.

For Jo, both finish in 11 months, but highest-rate-first costs $12.30 less interest. This is a mathematical comparison under the supplied terms, not a judgement about Jo's character. Motivation, irregular income and past-due accounts can change practical priorities. A plan should be both affordable and repeatable.

### A shortfall needs a different decision

If available money is below required payments, choosing where to put “extra” money cannot fix the shortfall. Identify the gap. Review the budget and, in a real situation, seek the creditor's current options before assuming an extension or changed terms. Do not invent income or promise to miss essential costs. A lump-sum payment can reduce debt if it is genuinely available; spending the same emergency reserve twice cannot.

An **emergency fund** is accessible money set aside for unexpected costs. Its purpose is to reduce the need for new borrowing after a shock. An affordable contribution and a debt plan may coexist. The amount depends on circumstances; this lesson supplies Jo's available repayment money after that allocation.

### Test the promise of a shortcut

**Leverage** means borrowing to buy an investment. The investment may rise or fall; the debt remains payable under its agreement. If someone borrows $500 and must repay $540, an investment worth $600 at sale leaves $60 after repayment. If it is worth $450, selling it leaves a $90 shortfall. These are two invented outcomes, not forecasts. Borrowing increases exposure and adds a fixed obligation to an uncertain return.

Gambling, lotteries and sports betting also cannot supply dependable repayment income. The possible prize is not money already available. For example, four fictional $5 losing tickets cost $20 and produce no repayment money. A winning story does not establish how often winning happens or cover losses. Do not insert hoped-for winnings into a budget as though they were confirmed earnings.

**Optional reading record:** FCAC's *Paying back your debt* describes maintaining minimums while selecting a repayment priority. OSC Investor Office's *What are ways to borrow money for investing?* explains that investment losses do not cancel the borrowing obligation. Read September 28, 2026. The complete numerical cases here are original and supplied on this page.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Write Jo's first-month payment instructions

The required payments are A $25 and B $45. The remaining $50 goes to B under highest-rate-first, making A $25 and B $95. The payments still sum to $120. Choosing smallest-balance-first instead makes A $75 and B $45. Both satisfy the minimums; they use the extra money differently.

**Completed recommendation:** “I would start with A $25 and B $95 each month, subject to the final-balance adjustment. This covers both minimums and directs $50 extra to B's 19% balance. The supplied schedule costs $96.35 interest, $12.30 less than smallest-balance-first, while both finish in 11 months. I would review the budget if income changes rather than treating $120 as guaranteed forever.”

“Put all $120 on B” leaves A unpaid and does not follow the model. “Borrow to invest and use the profit” replaces a known repayment budget with an uncertain future outcome. Neither is the same plan with a faster payoff.

## 5. Practise with support

### The available amount falls to $90

Jo's balances, rates and minimums are unchanged, but a confirmed ongoing cost reduces the debt-payment budget to **$90**. This is a new calculation, not the old 11-month payoff schedule. Keep highest-rate-first.

Enter **Extra available after both minimums ($)**, **Payment to A ($)** and **Payment to B ($)**. Write **What the earlier schedule can no longer promise**, one or two sentences. Use **Check the allocation**.

Correct amounts are $20 extra, A $25 and B $65. Feedback on $50 extra: that belongs to the old $120 budget. Feedback when A is below $25 or B below $45: cover both required payments before allocating extra. Feedback when the sum exceeds $90: the instruction spends money the revised case does not supply. A correct sum with all extra on A is a different strategy; explain it rather than calling it highest-rate-first.

**Model:** “The revised budget leaves $90 − $70 = $20 extra, so I pay A $25 and B $65. The old payoff time and $96.35 interest total were calculated with $120 payments. I would run a new schedule before stating a new payoff date; smaller payments generally leave balances outstanding longer.”

**One more change:** If the budget fell to $60, the $70 minimum total would exceed it by $10. Choose **identify the $10 gap and review options**, **call the gap extra money**, **promise the old payoff date**, or **assume one creditor waived its payment**. Only the first follows the known facts. Revise your explanation if it silently keeps the old schedule.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — Highest-rate-first means:

A. Skip all other minimums. B. Pay the largest balance regardless of rate. C. Maintain minimums and direct extra money to the highest-rate debt. D. Borrow more automatically.

Answer C. A violates other obligations; B describes a different criterion; C states the strategy; D adds debt rather than allocating repayments.

### Q2 — What does Jo's schedule comparison actually show?

A. Both take 11 months, with $12.30 less interest under highest-rate-first. B. One finishes a year earlier. C. Neither charges interest. D. The smallest balance has the highest rate.

Answer A. A uses both supplied outputs; B invents a time gap; C ignores costs; D contradicts the statements.

### Q3 — Why might someone choose smallest-balance-first?

A. Minimums become optional. B. It guarantees the least interest. C. Rates no longer matter. D. Closing an account sooner may support motivation, with a cost tradeoff to compare.

Answer D. A–C are not features of the strategy; D gives a plausible reason without denying the cost.

### Q4 — A $60 budget faces $70 of minimums. What is known?

A. There is $10 extra. B. There is a $10 shortfall requiring review. C. The debt disappears. D. An extension is already approved.

Answer B. A reverses the subtraction's meaning; B describes the gap; C and D invent relief.

### Q5 — A borrowed investment falls in value. What happens to its loan?

A. The repayment obligation remains unless the actual agreement changes it. B. It automatically falls by the same percentage. C. The lender must provide a refund. D. The investment is guaranteed to recover before repayment.

Answer A. A separates asset value and debt; B and C invent contract terms; D turns an uncertain future into a guarantee.

### Q6 — Why exclude hoped-for betting winnings from a repayment budget?

A. Every wager wins eventually. B. A prize advertisement confirms income. C. Winnings are uncertain and stakes can be lost. D. Only losses above $100 count.

Answer C. A and B confuse possibility with dependable income; C explains the planning problem; D invents a threshold.

## 7. Apply independently

### Dara's repayment decision

Dara, a fictional adult, has two current debts. Account C owes **$200 at 10% annually**, minimum **$20 monthly**. Account D owes **$600 at 18% annually**, minimum **$30 monthly**. A budget supplies **$100 monthly for these debts**, after essentials and planned emergency saving. No fees or extra-payment penalties apply, and no account is past due.

A separate proposal says Dara could borrow **$600**, buy an investment and repay **$648 in full after one year**. There are no other charges in that proposal. A supplied stress scenario says the investment could be worth **$540 at that time**. That is a scenario, not a forecast or guaranteed sale price. Dara's priority is to reduce existing debt cost without adding an obligation that depends on an investment gain.

Enter **First payment to C ($)**, **First payment to D ($)** and **Shortfall if the proposed investment sells for $540 ($)**. Write **My repayment instruction and response to the proposal**, 4–5 sentences. Allocate the existing $100 and explain the strategy. Do not invent a payoff date; no full schedule for Dara is supplied.

**Complete model:** “I allocate C $20 and D $80: $50 covers the minimums and the other $50 targets D's higher 18% rate. I would continue checking the balances and budget before promising a payoff date. The new loan would require $648 even if the investment sold for $540, leaving a $108 shortfall. I would decline that proposal under Dara's stated priority because it adds debt dependent on an uncertain return. The $100 repayment budget is not spare money available to pay both the old debts and the new loan.”

Another allocation may be explained as smallest-balance-first (C $70, D $30), but it must acknowledge the different priority and cannot claim proven lower interest. Revise your recommendation with the supplied facts.
