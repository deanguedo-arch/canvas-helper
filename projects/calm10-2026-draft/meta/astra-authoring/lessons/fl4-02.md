# FL4-02 — Recognize scams and verify independently

Version `2026-09-28.astra.1`. Assessment A7. Fictional messages and names; no actionable scam addresses, QR codes, telephone numbers or payment destinations. Official source records checked September 28, 2026.

## 1. Start here

A familiar logo, a person's name or professional writing can make a message look convincing. The more useful question is what the sender wants you to do. A request to move money, reveal a code or install software deserves a pause even when the message looks polished.

You will classify several common fraud approaches, follow a bank-message and CRA-call example, practise choosing an independent verification route, and then prepare a response to a new job offer and a possibly genuine bank contact. Your product will identify the request, evidence, uncertainty and safe next step. The aim is careful verification, not assuming every unexpected contact is fraudulent.

Follow **Meet the situation and read the documents → Learn the method → Follow a complete example → Practise with support → Check your understanding → Apply independently → Review and complete**. Pause between the examples if useful. All required documents are supplied and answers save automatically. Guided work and six questions are practice; the final response plan and sign-offs form your evidence.

**Vocabulary.** Phishing is a deceptive contact designed to obtain information, money or access. Impersonation means pretending to be a trusted person or organization. A warning cue is a reason for concern, not complete proof of identity. Independent verification uses a route found separately from the questionable contact. A one-time code can authorize access or a transaction; it is not a harmless reference number.

Optional reference disclosure: **Official scam and CRA guidance**. The local dated records are sufficient. No student must open a website, call anyone, install software or send money.

## 3. Learn the method

### Read the requested action first

A scam often tries to move a person from uncertainty to an irreversible action quickly. Identify the verb: **send, transfer, install, reveal, deposit, approve**. Then identify what could be lost: money, account access, personal information or control of a device. This is more reliable than judging a logo or spelling alone; convincing messages can be well written.

Pressure can be emotional as well as financial. A threat of closure, a promise of easy earnings, fear for a relative or a claim that love requires payment can all discourage checking. Recognizing the mechanism helps you pause without blaming the person receiving it. These approaches are designed to exploit ordinary hopes and responsibilities.

### Classify by the story and the requested benefit

The label helps explain a pattern; it does not replace evidence. Card C offers investment returns, so investment fraud is the relevant pattern. Card D offers employment but asks the worker to pay, matching a job-fraud pattern. Card E impersonates a loved one in difficulty. Card F offers goods without adequate verification, matching a purchase/merchandise concern. Card G uses a developing relationship to seek funds.

Types can overlap. A supposed romantic partner who later recommends a guaranteed investment combines relationship and investment tactics. An online job that asks you to receive and forward money is different from ordinary paid work, even if it uses a real company's name. The CAFC advises verifying the actual employer independently and not paying deposits to unlock a job. A company existing does not prove that its name is being used by an authorized recruiter.

### Break the sender's control over verification

Calling a number supplied only in the message keeps you inside the same contact's story. A separate route changes that. For a bank, use the number on a card or statement already held, or open the known bank app directly. For a family emergency, use the person's previously known number or another independently known contact. For an employer, find the real organization's official contact information independently and ask about the named vacancy and recruiter. Do not install an offered app or send money while checking.

For an investment, the source recommends independent research and registration checks with the provincial securities regulator or the national registration search. Registration is one check, not a guarantee of returns or proof that a message's sender is the registered person. This course does not ask you to evaluate or buy a real investment.

### Keep the possibility of genuine contact open

CRA may need supporting tax information and may call. Therefore, “CRA never calls” is not a safe rule. Neither is “they knew my name, so I should answer everything.” The taught process lets a real request be handled through a verified route while denying an impersonator control of the interaction.

Asking for callback details collects a claim to check; it does not verify the claim. End the call before checking. Reach the CRA verification page independently. If its tool cannot verify a direct number, use the official CRA contact route for further checking. A result about a listed number also does not make caller ID impossible to spoof. The next conversation must use a route you have verified yourself.

### Use a short decision record

| Part | Example from Card A |
|---|---|
| Request | Transfer the balance and reveal a code. |
| Cues | Time pressure, secrecy, “protected account” story, access code. |
| What is unknown | Actual sender and whether there is a real bank issue. |
| Immediate boundary | No transfer, code sharing or message link use. |
| Independent check | Known bank app or contact details on the existing card. |

The result of verification may be “request confirmed,” “fraud attempt confirmed,” or “still unresolved.” If unresolved, do not treat pressure as evidence. If exposure has already occurred, shift promptly to the response process in the next lesson rather than continuing the conversation.

**Optional hint:** “In your response field, begin 'This contact asks for…'. Naming the action often makes the next step clearer.”

## 4. Follow a complete example

For A, I ignore the polished appearance and read the request. Moving a balance to a stranger's “protected account” and sending a sign-in code could give away funds and access. The warning cues justify refusing those actions now. I can do that without first proving the sender's identity.

For B, the request is different. CRA can seek supporting information, and the caller offers details for checking. That is compatible with a genuine call, but it remains an unverified claim. I use the official process before discussing personal tax information.

**Complete response record:**

> A resembles bank-investigator impersonation: it requests a balance transfer and a sign-in code under time pressure and secrecy. Leah should not transfer, share the code, reply through the text or install anything. Open the known bank app or use the number on the existing bank card to ask whether the bank has a real concern. The source record supports that separate route; a logo does not authenticate this sender.
>
> B may be genuine because CRA can call about tax documents. Leah should request the claimed name, office and callback details, say the contact will be checked, and end the call without sharing personal information. Reach CRA's verification page independently and use its official checking/contact route. If the number is not in the tool, obtain further official verification because direct employee numbers may be absent. Only continue the real matter through a verified channel; do not assume a caller-ID label or a polite tone proves authenticity.

“Block all government contact forever” would leave possible real business unresolved. “Send the code to show cooperation” would hand the unverified sender a sensitive tool. The plan protects access while keeping a route for legitimate service.

## 5. Practise with support

Keep Leah's A/B contacts. A second message from A says, “Install this remote-support app so we can secure your account.” No software has been installed. For B, this new **fictional verification result** is supplied: Leah independently reaches CRA using the official route; that service confirms a document request exists and explains a secure way to handle it. The original incoming caller's identity has not separately been established.

Write three saved responses:

1. **What changed in A's request, and what boundary remains?** Explain the added device-access risk.
2. **What did the independent check of B establish?** Distinguish a confirmed matter and secure route from proof about the original caller.
3. **Write Leah's next action for each contact.** Include a reason and avoid using the original contact's unverified route.

Controls: **Show guided hint**, **Compare with the model**, **Revise my response**. Hint: “A now requests device access. B's official check confirms a task to handle securely, not every fact about who first called.”

Comparison feedback: “If you recommend installing the app to verify A, reverse that order: the risky access is what the contact wants. If you mark all of B fraudulent despite the supplied official result, update your conclusion. If you treat the original caller as verified, narrow what the new evidence actually establishes.”

**Matching model:**

1. “A adds a request for remote access, which could expose the device or accounts. Leah still should not install, share codes or move money.”
2. “The independently reached CRA service confirmed a real document request and a secure route. It did not establish the identity of the original incoming caller.”
3. “For A, contact the bank using the existing app/card route and preserve the messages safely. For B, use the secure route provided during the independently verified CRA interaction to address the actual document request; do not send information back through the original unverified contact.”

## 7. Apply independently

### Devon's job offer and appointment call

Devon is a fictional adult. Prepare one **two-contact verification plan**. Neither contact has been acted on.

**Document D — Unrequested job message, October 12.** “I recruit for Summit Parcel, a real-looking company name. You are approved for remote order-checking work. Deposit $90 to unlock the work dashboard today; then receive customer payments in your personal account and forward them to our operator. Use only our chat, because the public office does not handle this program.” Devon did not apply and has no verified recruiter contact. No actual address, link or payment information is supplied.

**Document E — Bank appointment call, October 12.** Devon requested an appointment through the known bank app two days earlier. A caller says, “I am confirming the time of your requested appointment. You can end this call and check it through your usual bank channel.” The caller gives a proposed time and asks for no password, code, payment, software installation or personal document in this exchange. Caller identity is still unverified. Devon has the original appointment request in the known app and a current bank card.

Use four fields:

- **Classify the job contact with evidence** — identify the pattern and at least two supplied warning cues. Explain why a real company name would not settle it.
- **Plan the job verification and boundary** — specify what Devon should not do and an independently found employer route/question. No live search or message is required.
- **Evaluate and verify the appointment contact** — explain why it could be genuine, what is still unknown and exactly which supplied separate route Devon can use.
- **State what happens after checking** — distinguish confirmed, rejected and unresolved requests and one dated source principle that guides the plan.

**Compare with a complete model**

> The unrequested work message matches job-fraud warning patterns: it demands a deposit to unlock work and asks Devon to receive and forward money through a personal account. It also discourages independent contact. A real company can be impersonated. Devon should not deposit, forward funds, install a dashboard or provide documents. Independently find the actual employer's official contact and ask whether this vacancy and recruiter/program are authorized; do not use only the message's chat. If not verifiable, do not proceed with the risky requests.
>
> The appointment call could be genuine because Devon made an appointment request, and this exchange asks for no sensitive action. The sender is still unverified. Devon can end the call, open the previously installed bank app directly to review the original request, or use the number on the bank card to confirm the appointment. Handle a confirmed appointment through that verified channel. If the institution rejects the request, preserve/report the suspicious approach; if it remains unresolved, do not reveal secrets or pay to resolve the uncertainty. The September 28 source record supports independent verification rather than judging a contact by appearance or treating every call as fake.
