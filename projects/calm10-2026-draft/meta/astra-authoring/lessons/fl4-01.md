# FL4-01 — Digital footprints and financial security

Version `2026-09-28.astra.1`. Assessment A7. Fictional profile/settings records; official Canadian guidance checked September 28, 2026. No real profile, password, code or location is requested.

## 1. Start here

A photo can say more than its caption. A public schedule, recognizable place and reused password create different kinds of exposure. They also need different controls: changing who can see a post does not change the password used to sign in.

In this lesson you will audit a fictional profile, connect each clue to a plausible financial-security risk, and recommend several controls with clear limits. After a complete example and guided revision, you will build a different person's privacy-and-account plan. You will never need to open or change a real account.

Follow **Meet the situation and read the documents → Learn the method → Follow a complete example → Practise with support → Check your understanding → Apply independently → Review and complete**. Pause after the example if helpful. Practice is optional for completion; the final fictional audit and your review sign-offs become your evidence. Writing saves automatically.

**Vocabulary.** A digital footprint is information left by online activity or collected through digital services. An inference is something a viewer might work out from that information. A credential, such as a password, helps prove access to an account. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) adds another kind of proof when signing in. A recovery route helps an account holder regain access. Social engineering uses deception to obtain information or prompt an action, sometimes using personal facts to sound trustworthy.

| Available control | What it does in this fictional app |
|---|---|

## 3. Learn the method

### Separate the clue from the conclusion

Begin with a supplied detail: “P1 names Tuesday at 4:10 and shows the community-centre sign.” Then state a plausible inference: “A viewer may connect Casey to a regular practice trip.” Finally state a limit: “This does not reveal an exact home address or prove that any viewer intends harm.” This sequence keeps the audit useful without turning uncertainty into certainty.

Financial security enters the picture because familiar details can make an impersonation more convincing. A message saying “your team registration needs a payment before Tuesday practice” might feel credible to someone whose team and routine are public. Knowing the routine would not authenticate the sender. A clue can support targeting without directly exposing a bank password.

### Match each control to its job

Different controls work on different parts of the situation:

| Exposure | Relevant control | Remaining limit |
|---|---|---|
| A recurring public place/time combination | Remove or rewrite the routine caption; replace the revealing image; narrow the audience. | Someone may already have copied the older post. |
| Unneeded ongoing app location access | Deny or restrict the permission in the fictional guide. | This does not erase information already posted. |
| Reused password | Create a different strong credential for each affected service. | One account's change does not update another account. |
| Password alone as sign-in protection | Enable supported MFA and understand the recovery route. | MFA does not make a deceptive payment request legitimate. |

A plan with three privacy settings and no response to password reuse is incomplete. So is a plan that changes the password but leaves a public schedule unchanged. **Layered protection** means choosing controls that cover different exposures, not adding several versions of the same control.

### Minimize information while keeping the purpose

An audit does not require giving up all online connection. Ask what the post is meant to do. Casey wants to share a team experience; a later post saying “Good practice today” with a neutral background can do that without publishing a recurring departure time. Audience choice adds another layer. It is reasonable to remove a post entirely too; explain the purpose and trade-off.

Checking permissions uses the same question: what function needs this access? Document A says Casey is not using a feature requiring continuous location. That makes restriction a proportionate recommendation. A request for permission is not proof that an app is malicious; it is a reason to understand and limit access.

### Make the review a repeatable habit

A one-time cleanup is useful, but settings and habits can change. Connect a review to an event: before posting an image, after installing an app or when a service announces a security change. A healthy digital habit includes enough pause to check a request, and space to ask for support if pressure makes thinking difficult.

An unexpected code or sign-in alert would change the task from prevention toward incident response. Do not share or approve an unexpected code. Reach the service independently and review its security information. A code alone would not prove an attacker succeeded; a mistyped number or attempted access could also explain it. This case reports no such alert, so do not invent one in Casey's audit.

## 4. Follow a complete example

I read P1 and the audience setting together. A time in a private draft is different from the same time in a public post. Casey's record makes P1 public, so the place/time inference is relevant. I do not infer Casey's exact address from a community-centre sign.

Next I separate content from access. Casey's password reuse could connect the social and music accounts if that shared credential were stolen. Removing the photo does not resolve reuse. I therefore recommend content changes, permissions and account controls separately.

**Complete audit:**

> P1 combines a recurring Tuesday departure time with a recognizable community-centre sign and a public audience. A stranger may infer part of Casey's routine and use team details in a convincing payment message. The post does not prove an exact home address or a current compromise. Casey could remove the routine caption and replace the image with a neutral background, then share later with approved contacts. Casey should also restrict unneeded continuous location permission; that reduces new app access but does not remove existing image clues or copies.
>
> The social and music accounts reuse a password. Casey should use a different strong credential for each through the independently opened services, consider a password manager, enable supported MFA on the social app and confirm the recovery route. These reduce access risk; they do not authenticate every message. A repeatable habit is to check photo backgrounds, time/location detail and audience before posting, and review permissions after app changes. This plan uses the Canadian source record checked September 28, 2026; actual settings must be checked in the actual service.

“Turning the account private guarantees nobody can share the old photo” fails because the fictional control guide explicitly preserves that limitation. “The team badge proves identity theft has happened” also goes beyond the record.

## 7. Apply independently

### Morgan's public art profile

Morgan uses a fictional art-sharing app and a ticket app. Create a **privacy and financial-security audit** from these new documents.

**Document D — Profile extract.** A public bio says, “Find me selling prints at Harbour Hall every Saturday, 10:00 a.m.–2:00 p.m.” A photo of a packed order shows Morgan's full mailing address on the shipping label. No payment card or bank information is visible. Morgan wants to keep sharing finished artwork, but does not need to publish a home return address or a recurring attendance schedule.

**Document E — Settings and habits.** The art and ticket accounts use the same password, which is not shown. MFA is already enabled on the ticket account and off on the art account. The art app can access all photos; its supplied control guide allows Morgan to select only the particular photo being uploaded. Morgan has seen no unexpected sign-in, payment or recovery change. Both apps support separate password changes; changing one does not change the other. Removing a photo changes the displayed copy but cannot guarantee removal of earlier screenshots.

Use four fields; a few clear sentences per field are sufficient:

- **Two specific public clues and their possible misuse** — name the shipping-label and recurring-bio clues, link each to an inference or impersonation possibility, and state what is not known.
- **A revised bio and photo-sharing decision** — write replacement bio text and describe a photo change that preserves the art-sharing purpose without the unnecessary private detail.
- **Account and permission controls with reasons** — address reused credentials, the differing MFA settings and all-photos access. Do not invent actual passwords or codes.
- **A review habit and limits** — give a repeatable time/trigger for review, one dated source principle used and a limit on what the plan can guarantee.

**Compare with a complete model**

> The shipping label exposes a full mailing address, while the bio links Morgan to a recurring place and time. A stranger could use those details in a delivery-payment or event-fee impersonation. No account access or financial loss is established. A replacement bio could say, “I make prints and share new work here; event announcements are posted when needed.” Replace the order photo with an image of the art alone, without a shipping label or other identifying background. This preserves the sharing purpose and removes avoidable detail from the displayed profile.
>
> Give the art and ticket services different strong credentials, changing each through its independently reached service. Keep the ticket account's MFA and enable the supported extra layer on the art account. Restrict photo permission to selected images because that supports Morgan's stated use. Before posting, check labels, backgrounds, recurring times and audience; revisit permissions after app changes. The September 28 Canadian source record supports matching privacy and account controls to distinct risks. Old screenshots may remain, and these controls do not prove that future contacts are genuine.

Different bios are acceptable if they preserve the stated art-sharing purpose and remove unnecessary routine/address exposure. A vague “be careful online” statement does not identify the controls for this case.
