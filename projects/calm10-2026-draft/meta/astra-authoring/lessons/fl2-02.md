# FL2-02 — Calculate interest with the right time unit

Complete script for lead review. Version `2026-09-28.astra.1`. Canonical course unchanged.

## 1. Start here

### Twelve percent of what, and for how long?

An interest rate needs a principal amount and a time period before it can tell you a cost. A 12% annual rate does not mean paying 12% of the principal every month. You will calculate a deliberately simple loan, then explain where that model stops being useful.

Your final product is a calculation note for a new fictional offer: show interest, principal plus interest, a missing fee and what can be concluded about a repayment limit. A calculator is provided. No real borrowing, rate lookup or personal financial information is needed.

**How to complete this lesson:** Read **Mina's simple-interest agreement** and check its time unit. Learn **Match the rate to the time**, follow the three-month example and practise changing the duration and rate. Answer six questions, then complete **Imani's four-month offer**. Compare, revise and sign off. You can pause after the worked calculation; all fields save.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Mina's simple-interest agreement

Mina is a fictional adult borrowing **$600** for exactly **three months**. The agreement expressly uses **12% simple interest per year**, with months counted as twelfths of a year. No interim payment is made; the full principal stays outstanding until the end. Interest is not added to principal during the period. Principal and interest are paid together at the end of month three. There is no setup fee or other charge in this example.

This is a mathematical teaching agreement, not an offer from a lender. Real products can calculate interest daily, add fees or use changing balances. Use this model only with the stated assumptions.

**Terms on the agreement**

- Principal, amount borrowed: $600.
- Annual rate: 12%, or 0.12 as a decimal.
- Duration: three months, or 3/12 of a year.
- Interest rule: principal × annual decimal rate × years.
- Repayment at the end: principal + interest.

**Check the time unit:** Which duration belongs with the annual rate in this formula?

A. 3 years. B. 36 years. C. 0.25 year. D. 12 years.

Feedback: C is 3/12 of a year. A treats months as years; B multiplies when conversion requires dividing; D substitutes the number of months in a year for the actual duration.

## 3. Learn the method

### Match the rate to the time

**Simple interest** is calculated on the stated principal without adding earned or charged interest to that principal during the model period. Let P mean principal, r mean annual rate written as a decimal, and t mean time in years. Then **I = P × r × t**. I is the interest charge, not the whole repayment.

A percent means “per hundred.” Twelve percent becomes 12/100=0.12. Multiplying $600 by 12 rather than 0.12 would charge twelve times the principal for one year instead of twelve hundredths. Check whether the size of the answer makes sense: at 12% annually, one full year's simple interest on $600 is $72. Three months is only a quarter of that year, so the charge should be a quarter of $72.

The rate and time must use the same unit. With an annual rate, divide the number of months by 12. Three months is 0.25 year; six is 0.5 year. Do not apply the whole annual amount to every month. A useful alternative representation is **annual interest ÷12 × number of months**, which gives the same result under this agreement's month rule.

### Keep three amounts distinct

**Principal** is the amount borrowed. **Interest** is the charge calculated by the formula. **Principal plus interest** is the repayment before any additional fee. With a separate fee, add that fee once to the total paid. Do not call the whole repayment “interest,” and do not assume a missing fee is zero.

Write units beside each step. “600×0.12×3/12=18” becomes more meaningful as “$18 interest over three months.” A sentence about what the number means is part of the answer, not decoration around it.

### Change one assumption at a time

For a fixed principal and simple annual rate, doubling the time doubles interest. Doubling the rate or principal also doubles interest when the other two stay constant. These relationships help catch errors, but they do not establish the behaviour of every loan.

If payments reduce the principal, later interest may apply to a smaller balance. If interest is added to a balance and later interest is charged on that enlarged amount, the process involves **compounding**. The following lesson uses a reducing-balance repayment schedule. It would be wrong to apply this unchanged-principal formula to that whole schedule without accounting for payments.

**Model boundary:** this agreement uses months/12, not an actual-days convention. A real offer must state its own calculation and fee terms. No external page is needed to solve the fictional agreement.

**Hint:** Calculate a full year's interest first, then take the fraction of a year. This can be easier to reason through than entering every symbol at once.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Calculate Mina's three-month cost

The agreement supplies P=$600, r=0.12 and t=3/12. First, $600×0.12=$72 for a full year at the stated simple rate. Three months is one-quarter of a year, so $72×0.25=**$18 interest**. Repayment is $600+$18=**$618**.

**Completed calculation note:** “The $600 remains outstanding for three months. At 12% simple annual interest, I=$600×0.12×3/12=$18. With no fees, Mina repays $618 at the end of the three months. The $18 is the borrowing charge; $600 is the return of principal.”

An answer of $216 comes from $600×0.12×3. It treats three months as three years. An answer of $72 uses a whole year. Neither matches the agreement's duration, even though the multiplication itself was carried out correctly.

Now you will change the duration rather than the person or calculation rule. Keep the unchanged assumptions visible when comparing results.

## 5. Practise with support

### Change the duration, then the rate

**Practice 1:** Mina instead keeps the same $600 for **six months** at the same 12% simple annual rate. There are still no interim payments or fees. Enter **Six-month interest ($)** and **Six-month repayment ($)**. Use **Check the duration change**.

Feedback: $36 interest and $636 repayment. $18 repeats the three-month result. $72 uses twelve months. $432 treats six months as six years. Other answers receive the full-year amount $72 and the 6/12 time fraction, then a retry prompt.

**Practice 2:** Keep six months and $600, but change the annual rate to **9%**. Enter **Interest at 9% for six months ($)** and **Explain the comparison** (2–3 sentences identifying what changed and what stayed constant). Use **Check the rate change**, then compare the model.

Feedback: $27 interest. $54 is one year's interest; $36 retains the old rate; $270 reflects a decimal-place error. Other answers receive P600, r0.09, t6/12 and a prompt to retry.

**Model:** “At 12%, six months produces $36 interest and $636 repayment. Holding the $600 and six months constant,9% produces $600×0.09×6/12=$27 interest, $9 less. Repayment would be $627 with no fees. The change isolates the rate, so I can attribute the lower interest to that rate difference in this model.”

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — Which decimal represents 9%?

A. 9. B. 0.9. C. 0.009. D. 0.09.

Answer D. A is 900%; B is 90%; C is 0.9%; D divides 9 by 100 correctly.

### Q2 — Why use months/12 with an annual rate?

A. It converts the duration to the rate's yearly unit. B. It removes all interest. C. It changes principal into income. D. It proves every lender uses this exact method.

Answer A. A explains the unit match; B and C misstate the operation; D extends a teaching assumption to all products.

### Q3 — Which is the interest, not the total repayment, in Mina's original agreement?

A. $600. B. $18. C. $618. D. $72.

Answer B. A is principal; B is the three-month charge; C is repayment; D is a year's simple interest.

### Q4 — If time doubles while principal and simple annual rate stay fixed, what happens to interest?

A. It becomes zero. B. It is divided by two. C. It doubles under this model. D. The principal automatically doubles.

Answer C. A and B contradict proportional time; C follows the formula; D confuses interest with the amount borrowed.

### Q5 — What changes when regular payments reduce a loan's balance?

A. The original principal formula must be reconsidered because interest may apply to a smaller outstanding balance. B. Every payment is interest only. C. The rate has no meaning. D. Fees always disappear.

Answer A. A identifies the changed assumption; B–D invent features not implied by repayment.

### Q6 — An offer omits its setup fee. What can your calculated principal-plus-interest amount establish?

A. The exact all-in total. B. That the fee is illegal. C. That the fee is zero. D. A subtotal before the missing fee, with the fee still to verify.

Answer D. A and C turn an unknown into zero; B makes an unsupported legal conclusion; D states the calculation's limit.

## 7. Apply independently

### Imani's four-month offer

Imani, a fictional adult, is considering an **$800** loan for **four months** at **9% simple interest per year**. The principal stays unchanged until repayment at the end; no interim payments or compounding occur. Use months/12. The offer mentions one **setup fee**, but its amount has been left blank. There are no other charges under on-time repayment in this fictional offer. Imani can pay at most **$835 in total**, including that fee. The fee is paid at repayment for this exercise, not deducted from the advance.

Fields: **Interest for four months ($)**, **Principal plus interest before the fee ($)**, **Largest setup fee consistent with the $835 limit ($)** and **My calculation and conclusion** (3–4 sentences). Show the formula, explain the time unit and state whether the exact total is known. You are not choosing a real loan.

**Complete model:** “Four months is 4/12 of a year. Interest is $800×0.09×4/12=$24, so principal plus interest is $824 before the setup fee. The fee could be at most $835−$824=$11 to remain within Imani's stated total limit. The actual total is unknown until the fee is supplied; $824 alone does not establish that the offer fits.”

Do not write that the fee is $11. That is the greatest fee the budget could accommodate, not the lender's missing figure. Compare and revise your conclusion.
