# FL4-04 — Compare the promise, the price and the terms

Version `2026-09-28.astra.1`. Assessment A7. Every business, offer, message and transaction in this lesson is fictional. Official consumer information checked September 28, 2026. This is a document-based planning exercise, not a legal ruling or an actual complaint.

## 1. Start here

A low advertised price can change when a shopper reaches checkout. A subscription can also sound flexible in an advertisement while a later term describes a costly commitment. A useful consumer response identifies the exact contradiction, preserves the documents and asks the right organization for the kind of help it can provide.

You will compare a tablet advertisement with a fully supplied checkout, write a supported response to a changed offer, and then prepare a new subscription-dispute file. Your final product will explain the disputed terms, include a clear message to the business, and choose suitable escalation routes without promising a refund.

Follow **Meet the situation and read the documents → Learn the method → Follow a complete example → Practise with support → Check your understanding → Apply independently → Review and complete**. Pause after the price comparison if useful. All writing stays in the course; do not buy anything, contact a business or submit a complaint. The guided work and six questions are practice. The independent file and four sign-offs complete the lesson. Fields save automatically.

**Vocabulary.** An advertised price is the price presented to attract a purchaser. A mandatory fee is a charge the purchaser cannot decline while buying that offer. An optional addition can be declined. Drip pricing involves advertising an unattainable price because mandatory fixed fees are added later; the specific federal rule described here treats government-imposed charges on purchasers, such as sales tax, differently. A consumer dispute is a disagreement about a transaction or its terms. A remedy is a requested solution; requesting it does not establish that the business must provide it in every case.

Optional disclosure: **Consumer-source records and live references**. Everything needed is supplied below. Official live links are optional for checking a current real situation.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

Mina is a fictional adult in Alberta considering a tablet from Clearview Devices, a fictional business selling online from Alberta. Mina has reached checkout but has not agreed to purchase or paid. The tablet model and quantity are the same in each document. No accessory, delivery upgrade or other optional item was selected.

### Document A — Saved advertisement, October 12, 2026, 10:00 a.m.

> Clearview Devices — Cedar tablet, one tablet. **$120 before sales tax. Mandatory store fees included.** Standard pickup included. Offer available today while stock remains.

### Document B — Checkout, October 12, 2026, 10:05 a.m.

| Checkout line | Amount | Supplied condition |
|---|---:|---|
| One Cedar tablet | $120.00 | Same model as the advertisement. |
| Setup fee | $25.00 | Automatically added; no decline control. |
| Processing fee | $5.00 | Automatically added for every payment method. |
| Subtotal before tax | $150.00 | No other item or fee. |
| Sales tax | $7.50 | Supplied tax line for this fictional checkout. |
| Total | $157.50 | Nothing has been paid. |

The tax amount is supplied so the receipt is complete. This lesson asks you to analyze the store fees, not infer or research a tax rate.

### Document C — Saved business reply, October 12, 2026, 10:15 a.m.

> Both the $25 setup fee and the $5 processing fee are required for every purchaser of this tablet. Neither can be removed. Neither is a government charge. The $120 advertisement refers to the tablet line only. You can decide whether to continue at checkout.

### Document D — Dated in-course consumer records

**Competition Bureau, “Drip pricing”; checked September 28, 2026.** The Bureau explains that an advertised price made unattainable by mandatory fixed fees is contrary to the Competition Act, apart from government-imposed charges on purchasers such as sales tax. Variable fees can also raise concerns. A report about deceptive marketing can be made through the Bureau's official site. An individual learner cannot determine a legal outcome from a short case.

**Government of Alberta, “Unfair business practices”; checked September 28, 2026.** Alberta's Consumer Protection Act addresses unfair practices, including misleading representations and pricing. Its protections can apply before, during or after a transaction, even when a purchase has not been completed. Keep the offer, terms, receipts and communications that show the concern. These fictional parties and the offer are in Alberta; this course does not generalize Alberta jurisdiction to every online purchase.

**Government of Alberta, “File a consumer complaint against a business”; checked September 28, 2026.** Start by trying to resolve a concern with the business and retain the record. Alberta's Consumer Investigations Unit assesses possible violations within its authority. The described complaint route begins with the Consumer Contact Centre and a complaint ID before the portal stage. The unit does not recover a consumer's financial losses, mediate the dispute or renegotiate the contract. A complaint is not a guaranteed refund procedure.

**Financial Consumer Agency of Canada, “How credit cards work,” “Credit card purchases”; checked September 28, 2026.** Compare the statement with receipts. For a mistake, contact the merchant; if the problem is not corrected, contact the financial institution for assistance. Explain the facts accurately. Disagreement with recognized purchase terms is different from saying a transaction was never made or approved.

## 3. Learn the method

### Compare the same offer at each stage

Begin with the product, quantity and choices. A difference caused by selecting a larger model or optional delivery does not establish the same issue as a required charge added to an unchanged offer. Mina's documents hold those details constant: one Cedar tablet, standard pickup and no optional additions.

Then compare prices on the same basis. The advertisement says $120 before tax; checkout says $150 before tax. Subtracting those amounts isolates the store-fee difference. Comparing $120 directly with the tax-inclusive $157.50 would mix the fee concern with a separate supplied tax line.

Write the arithmetic with labels. A dollar difference is useful evidence only when the reader understands what the two amounts represent. Do not replace the comparison with “the business is stealing”; that is an accusation about conduct or intent that these documents do not establish.

### Read what a condition actually does

A condition matters when it changes a consumer's practical choice. A charge called “service,” “processing” or “setup” is still mandatory in this case because the customer cannot decline it. A small font or a final opportunity to abandon checkout does not make the advertised $120 purchase available.

Subscriptions require the same close reading. “Monthly payment” describes when money is charged; it does not by itself prove that the commitment lasts only one month. Compare statements about duration, renewal, cancellation and fees across the advertisement and the accepted terms. If they conflict, identify both statements. Do not hide an inconvenient term, and do not assume its presence automatically resolves a misleading-advertisement concern.

### Build a concern that someone can investigate

Use **promise → conflicting document → effect → requested response**. Name the date and document, quote only the short wording necessary, and state the amount or condition affected. Then ask for a specific response, such as an accurate written quote, correction of a disputed charge, or confirmation of cancellation terms. A focused request makes it easier to understand the disagreement.

Keep copies already available. A later corrected advertisement is useful new evidence but does not replace the earlier version in the chronology. A record should distinguish “requested a correction,” “business acknowledged the request” and “correction confirmed.” These are different stages.

### Match the route to the job

| Need | Suitable route in these cases | Limit to state |
|---|---|---|
| Clarification, correction or a requested refund/cancellation | The business through the contact in the saved order or independently reached official service | A request is not a promised outcome. |
| Possible Alberta consumer-law violation | Alberta Consumer Contact Centre and, where appropriate, Consumer Investigations Unit process | The unit assesses enforcement matters; it does not recover losses or mediate a personal dispute. |
| Possible deceptive advertising, including drip pricing | Competition Bureau's independently reached official reporting route | Reporting the marketing concern does not itself correct a card statement or cancel a subscription. |
| Unresolved recognized credit-card purchase problem | Card issuer/financial institution through the existing card or genuine app | Provide the terms, receipt and merchant correspondence; do not invent an unauthorized transaction or guaranteed reversal. |

The routes can serve different purposes for the same documents. There is no need to submit a report to every organization for every disagreement. Explain what assistance is being sought and whether a purchase or payment has even occurred.

**Optional first-step hint:** “Underline ‘before sales tax’ in both price records. Compare the two subtotals first, then name which fees account for the difference.”

## 4. Follow a complete example

I check that Mina selected the same tablet and no extras. I then read the advertisement's promise: mandatory store fees are included in $120 before tax. The business reply confirms that both added fees are required and are not government charges. That removes two possible explanations—optional choices and tax—for the $30 difference.

My calculation is **$25.00 + $5.00 = $30.00** in required store fees. Equivalently, **$150.00 − $120.00 = $30.00** above the advertised pre-tax price. The supplied checkout total is consistent: **$150.00 + $7.50 = $157.50**. I do not subtract the full total from the advertised amount and call all of the difference a prohibited fee.

**Complete concern and action note:**

> On October 12, 2026, Mina saved the $120-before-tax Cedar-tablet advertisement stating that mandatory store fees were included. The same tablet with no optional additions reached a $150-before-tax subtotal because checkout required a $25 setup fee and $5 processing fee. Your 10:15 a.m. reply confirms both are unavoidable store charges. The required store-fee difference is $30. Please explain and correct the discrepancy, and provide an accurate written price including all mandatory store fees before Mina decides whether to buy. No purchase or payment has occurred.
>
> Retain the advertisement, checkout and reply with their times. The supplied facts raise a drip-pricing concern under the Bureau's record and an Alberta misleading-pricing concern; this note does not declare a final legal finding. If the concern remains unresolved, Mina can use Alberta's consumer-information/complaint route and report the marketing concern to the Competition Bureau through their independently reached official pages. No card charge exists to dispute. Neither report itself promises compensation.

An unsupported alternative would say “the bank must refund $157.50.” Mina has paid nothing, and the sources do not establish an automatic refund. A useful response follows the actual stage of the transaction.

## 5. Practise with support

Continue Mina's case. Documents A–C remain part of the record. The business now sends this **new document at 11:00 a.m. on October 12**:

> We have replaced the advertisement with “Cedar tablet: $150 before sales tax; all mandatory store fees and standard pickup included.” The revised checkout shows one $150 pre-tax price, the supplied $7.50 tax line and a $157.50 total. There are no other required fees. The tablet is still available. Mina has still not purchased it. We have not offered a $120 price.

Write in these three fields:

1. **Compare the revised advertisement and checkout** — calculate their pre-tax difference and state what has changed.
2. **Write Mina's next decision note** — explain one reasonable choice and what the correction does not establish.
3. **Update the evidence and route plan** — identify which earlier and later records to retain and which action still fits if Mina wishes to raise the earlier marketing concern.

Use **Show a first-step hint** if needed: “A corrected current price can make the new offer clearer while leaving an earlier version in the evidence history.” Select **Check my comparison** for the following targeted feedback: “Did you compare $150 with $150? Did you preserve the original $120 document? Did you keep ‘may choose to buy’ separate from ‘must receive the old price’?” Then open **Compare with a complete model**.

> The revised advertisement and revised checkout both state $150 before tax, so the current pre-tax difference is $0. The corrected advertisement now includes the required store fees. Mina can decline the $157.50 total because the price exceeds the intended budget, or decide it is acceptable and choose to purchase; no budget or competing offer is supplied, so neither choice is forced. The correction does not establish that Mina has purchased, received a refund or become entitled to the earlier price.
>
> Save the original 10:00 advertisement, 10:05 checkout, 10:15 reply and 11:00 correction. The current comparison is resolved on the supplied figures, while the earlier marketing record remains available if Mina chooses to seek Alberta consumer guidance or report it to the Competition Bureau. There is still no card transaction for an issuer to reverse.

## 7. Apply independently

### Omar's subscription file

Omar is a fictional 19-year-old Alberta resident. SoundShelf is a fictional Alberta online audio-library business. The issue concerns a recognized subscription and conflicting cancellation representations. No password theft, card theft or unfamiliar merchant is reported.

**Document E — Advertisement saved October 1, 2026.** “SoundShelf: $9 each month. Cancel anytime. No exit fee.” The same plan appears in the following records.

**Document F — Checkout and accepted terms, October 1.** The payment summary shows $9 due that day and future billing on the first day of each month. Below the summary, the terms say: “This plan has a twelve-month commitment. Ending before the commitment ends requires a $45 early-exit fee.” Omar accepted the checkout; the record does not say whether Omar noticed that sentence. The receipt shows the initial $9 payment and the same business name. No tax or other fee is part of this exercise.

**Document G — Cancellation correspondence.** On October 20, Omar wrote through the business contact on the saved receipt: “Please cancel before the November 1 renewal. Your advertisement said cancel anytime with no exit fee. Please confirm cancellation with no further charge.” On October 22, the business replied: “We will end the plan on November 1. We will charge the November $9 payment and a $45 early-exit fee under the commitment term. We will not waive either.” There is no further explanation of why another monthly payment is due after the October 20 request.

**Document H — Statement and follow-up.** The genuine card statement shows a November 1 SoundShelf charge of $54. On November 2, Omar sent the business the advertisement, October 20 request and statement and asked it to correct the $54 charge and confirm no future billing. On November 4, the business replied: “We stand by the October 22 reply. The subscription is closed; there will be no future monthly charges.” No refund is shown. The issuer is available through the existing physical card or genuine app. No issuer dispute has yet been opened. Every date is a supplied fictional case fact, even though the source records were checked earlier.

Prepare one **subscription concern and escalation file** in four fields. Short paragraphs or labelled bullets are suitable; include the exact dates and amounts needed to make the disagreement understandable.

- **Compare the promise, terms and charge** — identify the two conflicting cancellation statements, show how the charged total is composed and distinguish what remains disputed from what has been confirmed.
- **Write Omar's concern summary** — address a reader who has not seen the case. State the requested correction and evidence without claiming a legal decision or inventing that the subscription was never accepted.
- **Choose recipients and independently reached routes** — explain the roles of the business, issuer, Alberta consumer route and Competition Bureau as relevant. Distinguish assistance with the charge from enforcement/marketing information.
- **Set an evidence and follow-up plan** — list the dated documents to retain, state what response or result remains pending, and explain the limit on promises.

**Compare with a complete model**

> The October 1 advertisement promises $9 each month, cancellation at any time and no exit fee. The accepted checkout terms instead describe a twelve-month commitment and $45 early-exit fee. Omar accepted that checkout; whether the commitment sentence was noticed is not known. The November 1 charge is $9 + $45 = $54. Your October 22 reply said you would charge another $9 and $45, and my November 1 statement shows $54. I requested correction on November 2; the November 4 refusal confirms closure but no refund. I request correction of the disputed $54 and a written explanation of the cancellation/charging basis. Attached in a real secure process would be the dated advertisement, terms, receipt, cancellation request, replies and statement.”
>

Omar can now contact the issuer through the existing card or genuine app, describe this as a recognized subscription with a disputed charge/cancellation representation, and ask about the applicable assistance or dispute process. Do not claim the card was stolen or guarantee a chargeback. Alberta's official consumer-complaint page supplies the Contact Centre/complaint-ID route for assessment of a possible consumer-law issue; the Consumer Investigations Unit does not recover Omar's loss or mediate the contract.
