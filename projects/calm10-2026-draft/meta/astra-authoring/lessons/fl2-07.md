## 1. Start here

### Five years can be part of a twenty-five-year plan

A mortgage quotation can name both a five-year term and a twenty-five-year amortization. Those numbers answer different questions. You will read them alongside the down payment, remaining balance and rules for extra payments.

Your final product is a comparison note for two fictional mortgage quotations. Explain the payment, balance at the end of the term and a planned extra payment. This is a document-reading exercise, not a recommendation to buy a home or a mortgage approval.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Lee and Noor's quotation

Lee and Noor are fictional adults considering a teaching-example property price of **$160,000**. They have **$40,000 for a down payment**, leaving **$120,000 mortgage principal**. Other purchase and ownership costs have a separate allocation and are excluded from this loan-only illustration; their exclusion does not mean homes have no taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs or legal costs.

**Quotation M:** fixed 6% nominal annual interest compounded semi-annually, monthly payments, **five-year term** and **25-year amortization**. Regular payment: **$767.77**. During the first 60 months, scheduled payments total **$46,066.20**, of which **$33,870.12** is interest. The balance after payment 60 is **$107,803.92**. No extra payment or fee is included in these outputs.

**Extra-payment condition:** up to **10% of original principal per contract year** may be paid in one additional lump sum without a prepayment charge. Unused allowance does not carry forward. A larger extra payment may incur a charge whose calculation must be requested. These are invented terms for this quotation, not a rule for every mortgage.

A **mortgage** is borrowing secured on property. Failing to meet its terms can put that property at risk. **Down payment** means the buyer's contribution to the purchase price; it is not the same as a regular loan payment.

**Read the quotation:** What ends after five years here?

A. The stated contract term. B. Every debt automatically. C. All ownership costs. D. The 25-year amortization calculation.

A identifies the five-year term. B ignores the supplied remaining balance; C confuses ownership and borrowing; D changes the stated repayment horizon.

## 3. Learn the method

### Separate the contract from the repayment plan

The **term** is how long the current mortgage agreement applies. Its rate and other conditions are established for that period. At the end, any unpaid balance must be addressed: for example, by repayment or an appropriate new agreement. Do not assume renewal has the same rate.

The **amortization** is the planned time over which scheduled payments would repay the loan under the model's assumptions. It can be much longer than one term. A five-year term within a 25-year amortization therefore does not promise the same rate for 25 years. A long-run calculator illustration holding a rate constant must say that it is conditional.

Read a quotation as three connected records: **money borrowed today → payments during this contract → debt still owing when the contract ends**. The remaining balance explains why someone may still need financing after years of payments.

### Know what the payment leaves out

A quoted principal-and-interest payment is not necessarily the entire cost of housing. Taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance and transaction costs may be separate. This exercise supplies a loan-payment limit only after those other costs have been considered; you cannot reuse it as a real affordability approval.

An application may require evidence of income, down payment, existing debts and credit history. The lender assesses its current criteria and the property. Knowing the payment is only part of that assessment. Do not upload personal records or infer eligibility from this fictional calculation.

### Read flexibility as carefully as the rate

A **prepayment** is money paid toward principal beyond scheduled payments. A **prepayment privilege** is the amount and timing the contract permits without a specified charge. Some agreements are more flexible than others. A percentage needs a base: M uses original principal, so its 10% allowance is based on $120,000, not the current balance or property price.

Extra principal paid earlier can reduce later interest under unchanged terms, but the amount, timing and any charge matter. This lesson compares permission only; its schedules contain no extra payments. Do not use an unchanged schedule to claim the precise saving from a proposed prepayment.

**Optional official reading record:** FCAC's mortgage guidance distinguishes contract term, amortization and prepayment conditions. Read September 28, 2026. It supports these reading questions; the complete fictional quotation supplies the numbers and conditions used here.

**Hint:** Circle the words “term,” “amortization” and “original principal” before calculating. Each determines which number answers a different question.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Explain the balance after five years

The initial loan is $160,000 − $40,000 = **$120,000**. Sixty regular payments total 60 × $767.77 = **$46,066.20**. However, not all of that pays down principal: $33,870.12 is interest. Principal repaid is $46,066.20 − $33,870.12 = **$12,196.08**. Subtracting that from $120,000 leaves **$107,803.92**, matching the schedule.

M's annual extra-payment allowance is 10% × $120,000 = **$12,000**. It does not mean $12,000 is available in Lee and Noor's budget; it is a contract permission.

**Complete explanation:** “M fixes its rate for five years, while payments are calculated over a 25-year repayment plan. After the first five years, $107,803.92 remains because only $12,196.08 of the $46,066.20 paid reduced principal. The contract allows a lump sum up to $12,000 in each contract year without the stated prepayment charge. Future rates and any actual extra-payment saving require a new calculation.”

“The balance must be $73,933.80 because they paid $46,066.20” incorrectly treats interest as principal repayment. The schedule makes the split visible.

## 5. Practise with support

### Shorten amortization to 20 years

Keep the $120,000 principal, fixed 6% rate, five-year term and monthly calculation convention. Change only amortization to **20 years**. The new regular payment is **$854.63**. First-60-payment total is **$51,277.80**, interest **$33,032.95**, and remaining balance **$101,755.15**. No extra payments or fees occur. Lee and Noor's supplied loan-payment allowance is **$860 monthly**.

Enter **Increase in regular monthly payment ($)**, **Reduction in balance at month 60 compared with M ($)** and **Monthly margin under $860 ($)**. Write **What the shorter plan changes**, two sentences. Use **Check the comparison**.

Targets: $854.63 − $767.77 = **$86.86** more each month; $107,803.92 − $101,755.15 = **$6,048.77** less debt at month 60; $860 − $854.63 = **$5.37** margin. If you enter $51,277.80 as principal repaid, subtract its interest first. If you say the shorter plan is unaffordable, compare its payment with the actual $860 limit; the issue is a small margin, not an excess.

**Model:** “The shorter amortization raises the monthly payment by $86.86 but leaves $6,048.77 less debt at the end of the same five-year term. It fits the supplied $860 payment allowance with only $5.37 to spare. Both still have an unpaid balance, and neither quotation guarantees the rate after year five.”

Revise any sentence that treats the end of the term as the end of repayment.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — The down payment is:

A. Every monthly payment combined. B. The interest charge. C. A guarantee of approval. D. The buyer's contribution toward the purchase price before the remaining amount is financed.

Answer D. A and B describe different amounts; C overstates what money alone establishes; D names its role.

### Q2 — A five-year term with a 25-year amortization means:

A. The current contract applies for five years within a longer planned repayment period. B. The rate is guaranteed for 25 years. C. No balance can remain after five years. D. There are only five monthly payments.

Answer A. A separates the two periods; B and C add promises absent from the quotation; D confuses years with payment count.

### Q3 — Which amount reduces principal in M's first five years?

A. All $46,066.20 paid. B. Only the $33,870.12 interest. C. Payments less interest, $12,196.08. D. The property price.

Answer C. A ignores the interest portion; B identifies a charge; C calculates the debt reduction; D is not a repayment.

### Q4 — A 10% allowance based on $120,000 original principal equals:

A. $1,200. B. $12,000. C. $16,000. D. Whatever money happens to be saved.

Answer B. A uses 1%; B uses the stated base; C uses property price; D confuses affordability and permission.

### Q5 — What should a calculator's 25-year total at an unchanged rate be called?

A. A guaranteed future bill despite a five-year term. B. An approved loan application. C. A property appraisal. D. A conditional illustration assuming that rate throughout.

Answer D. A hides renewal uncertainty; B and C are different processes; D identifies the assumption.

### Q6 — What remains to check before making an extra payment above the privilege?

A. The agreement's charge and permission terms, rather than assuming the extra payment is free. B. Nothing; all extra payments are free. C. Whether the house has changed colour. D. Only the original advertisement headline.

Answer A. A addresses the relevant condition; B invents a universal rule; C and D do not establish current terms.

## 7. Apply independently

### Morgan's two quotations

Morgan, a fictional adult, considers a **$240,000** property with a **$60,000 down payment**, leaving **$180,000** principal. Both quotations fix 5% nominal annual interest compounded semi-annually for a **five-year term**, with monthly payments and no fees or extra payments included in the supplied schedules. Morgan's loan-payment allowance is **$1,200 monthly**, after separate allocations for other costs. This is not a lender's approval.

| Quotation | P | Q |
|---|---:|---:|
| Amortization | 20 years | 25 years |
| Regular payment | $1,182.83 | $1,046.89 |
| Balance after 60 payments | $150,080.74 | $159,313.45 |
| Interest during those 60 payments | $41,050.54 | $42,126.85 |
| Annual single lump-sum privilege | 10% of original principal | 15% of original principal |

Both privileges reset each contract year and do not carry forward. Larger lump sums may incur an unspecified charge. Morgan hopes to make **one $20,000 extra payment in year three**, but that money is not confirmed. Morgan prefers faster principal reduction if monthly payments fit.

Enter **P's monthly margin ($)**, **P's annual privilege ($)** and **Q's annual privilege ($)**. Write **My comparison for Morgan**, 4–6 sentences addressing fit, remaining balance, the proposed extra payment and the limit of the evidence.

**Complete model:** “P fits the $1,200 allowance with $17.17 monthly margin. It leaves $150,080.74 after five years, $9,232.71 less than Q, while both still require a plan for the unpaid balance. P allows $18,000 annually and Q $27,000, so a single $20,000 payment would exceed P's privilege by $2,000 but fit Q's. I prefer P for the stated faster-repayment priority if Morgan accepts its narrow monthly margin, but I would check its charge or adjust the proposed payment before relying on a $20,000 lump sum. The extra money is unconfirmed, and these schedules do not calculate its effect or guarantee a renewal rate.”

Q can be justified for greater monthly and prepayment flexibility, provided its higher remaining balance and first-term interest are acknowledged. A reasoned comparison is the product; the case does not determine one universally correct mortgage.
