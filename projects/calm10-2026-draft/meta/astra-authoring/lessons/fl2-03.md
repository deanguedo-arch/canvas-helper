# FL2-03 — Read a loan payment schedule

## 1. Start here

### Where does a loan payment go?

A payment can contain both an interest charge and repayment of the amount borrowed. You will follow those two parts through a schedule, then use a calculator to compare repayment lengths. A smaller monthly payment can help a monthly budget while increasing the total cost.

Your final product is a comparison of two fictional loans for the same purchase. You will identify the monthly payment, total borrowing cost and remaining balance after the first payment, then explain a choice using a supplied budget. All rates and terms are teaching examples, not available offers.

**How to complete this lesson:** Read **Alex's repayment schedule**. Learn what each column means, follow the first two payments, then change the repayment length in **Try 24 months**. Answer six checks. Complete **Ren's equipment loan comparison**, review the model and sign off. Pause after the calculator comparison if needed; your entries save. ## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Alex's repayment schedule

Alex, a fictional adult, borrows $1,000. The agreement charges a fixed nominal annual rate of 12%, divided by 12 for a monthly rate of 1%. Interest is charged on the opening balance each month. A payment is made at the end of every month. No further money is borrowed, no payments are missed and no fees apply. The loan is scheduled to finish in 12 months.

An **amortization schedule** is a row-by-row record of how regular payments reduce a debt. “Opening” means before this month's interest and payment; “closing” means after them. Money is rounded to cents at each interest posting. The last payment is adjusted to clear the remaining cents.

| Month | Opening balance | Interest charged | Payment | Principal repaid | Closing balance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $1,000.00 | $10.00 | $88.85 | $78.85 | $921.15 |
| 2 | $921.15 | $9.21 | $88.85 | $79.64 | $841.51 |

**Schedule summary:** 11 payments of $88.85 and a final payment of $88.84; total paid $1,066.19; total interest $66.19. Open **All 12 payments** to inspect the complete schedule. The visible rows and summary supply everything needed for the teaching.

**Read the document:** Which figure says how much of the first payment reduces the original debt?

A. $88.85. B. $10.00. C. $78.85. D. $1,066.19.

C is the principal repaid. A includes interest as well as principal; B is the first interest charge; D is the sum of all payments, not one payment's principal portion.

## 3. Learn the method

### Follow one row from left to right

Start with the opening balance: the unpaid principal before that month's charge. Multiply it by the monthly rate to find interest. Subtract interest from the payment to find principal repaid. Finally, subtract principal repaid from the opening balance to find the closing balance. That closing balance becomes next month's opening balance.

The same process can be read as a flow:

**Opening debt → calculate interest → split the payment → subtract its principal part → carry the balance forward.**

For month one, 12% per year divided by 12 gives 1% per month, so $1,000 × 0.01 = $10. The $88.85 payment first covers that $10. The remaining $78.85 reduces principal. Subtracting the whole $88.85 from $1,000 would ignore the interest charge and understate the debt.

### Explain the changing split

The payment stays almost constant, but the split changes. In month two, interest is charged on $921.15 rather than $1,000. The smaller balance produces $9.21 interest after rounding, so $79.64 of the same $88.85 payment can reduce principal. A constant payment does not mean a constant interest charge.

The **amortization period** is the planned time to repay the loan. Spreading the same debt over more months usually lowers each payment under the same rate and calculation rules. It also leaves some principal unpaid for longer, creating more interest charges. Both effects matter: a payment that cannot be sustained is not made suitable just by having a low lifetime cost.

### Use the tool as a model you can explain

The calculator repeats the row calculation. It does not know whether a purchase is needed, whether income will continue, or whether a real lender will approve an application. Read its principal, annual rate, months and fee inputs before trusting its answer. This lesson uses equal monthly payments, a fixed rate and end-of-month payments. Real agreements may use different timing or calculation conventions.

A fee paid separately adds to total cost. It does not become loan principal in this model. If a real fee is added to the loan, it can also attract interest; that would require a different principal input.

**Optional reference:** FCAC's *Personal loans* explains comparing loan terms and charges. Its page supports the comparison questions; Alex's agreement and the supplied schedule determine these figures. You do not need to open it to complete the task.

**First-step hint:** Find the opening balance and monthly rate before looking at the payment. This prevents you from treating the payment itself as the amount on which interest is charged.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Explain Alex's first two payments

The first row begins with $1,000. Interest is $10, leaving $88.85 − $10 = $78.85 to repay principal. The debt falls to $1,000 − $78.85 = $921.15. The second row starts with that exact amount. Its interest is $921.15 × 0.01 = $9.2115, rounded to $9.21. Principal repaid is $88.85 − $9.21 = $79.64; the closing balance is $841.51.

**Completed explanation:** “Alex pays $88.85 in each of the first two months. The first payment reduces principal by $78.85 and the second by $79.64 because the second interest charge is smaller. The full schedule totals $1,066.19, which is $66.19 above the $1,000 borrowed. I used the schedule's adjusted final payment rather than multiplying $88.85 by 12 and ignoring the one-cent adjustment.”

“Alex owes $911.15 after month one” is unsupported: it subtracts the entire $88.85 payment from the $1,000 balance. Of that payment, $10 pays interest, so only $78.85 reduces the principal. Show the split once, then subtract only the principal portion from the debt.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — What becomes the next row's opening balance?

A. This row's payment. B. This row's closing balance. C. The original principal every time. D. Total interest for the whole loan.

Answer B. A is money paid, not debt remaining; B carries the unpaid debt forward; C ignores repayments; D is a cumulative cost rather than a balance.

### Q2 — A payment is $70 and the interest portion is $8. How much principal is repaid?

A. $78. B. $70. C. $8. D. $62.

Answer D. A adds the charge instead of removing it from the payment; B ignores interest; C identifies interest itself; D is $70 − $8.

### Q3 — Alex's second interest charge is smaller because:

A. The opening balance is smaller at the same monthly rate. B. The annual rate secretly changed. C. A fee was refunded. D. Interest ends after one payment.

Answer A. A follows the agreement and rows; B and C invent changes; D contradicts the continuing interest column.

### Q4 — What is the strongest comparison of Alex's two schedules?

A. The smaller payment proves the cheaper loan. B. Both total exactly $1,000. C. The 24-month loan lowers monthly payments but raises total interest. D. Only the first payment matters.

Answer C. A confuses cash flow and total cost; B ignores interest; C states both observed effects; D ignores remaining obligations.

### Q5 — A separate $20 fee is paid at setup. In this lesson's calculator, where does it belong?

A. Nowhere because it is not interest. B. In total borrowing cost, once, outside the amortized principal. C. In every month's interest. D. As a subtraction from the amount owed.

Answer B. A omits a real cost; B follows the separate-fee convention; C counts it repeatedly; D treats a charge as a repayment.

### Q6 — Why inspect an adjusted final payment?

A. It guarantees all real offers have identical fees. B. It proves no interest is charged. C. It changes a monthly rate into a yearly rate. D. Rounding can leave a final amount slightly different from the regular payment.

Answer D. A–C describe unrelated claims; D explains why the exact schedule total may differ from regular payment times months.

## 7. Apply independently

### Ren's equipment loan comparison

Ren is a fictional adult comparing two ways to finance the **same $1,800 equipment purchase**. Both offers use 9% nominal annual interest divided by 12, end-of-month payments, no further advances and no missed payments. Both charge a **separate $20 setup fee**, paid from a dedicated $20 setup allowance; it is not added to principal. Ren can devote **up to $160 each month to this loan**, after all other planned costs. Ren prefers the lower total cost if the payment fits that limit.

| Sum of loan payments | $1,888.95 | $1,973.58 |
| Interest in first month | $13.50 | $13.50 |

Explain monthly fit, the total-cost difference and one limitation of using a model. You can reproduce the outputs in the calculator; use the supplied records if you do not.

**Complete model:** “Offer A costs $1,888.95 + $20 − $1,800 = $108.95 to borrow; Offer B costs $193.58. A saves $84.63 overall. Its regular $157.41 payment and $157.44 final payment both fit the $160 monthly limit, although the regular margin is only $2.59. The first A payment repays $157.41 − $13.50 = $143.91 principal, leaving $1,656.09. I recommend A under Ren's stated preference and available budget, while recognising that a future income change or a different actual fee/rate would require a new comparison.”
