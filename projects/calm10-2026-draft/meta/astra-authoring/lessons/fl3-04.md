# FL3-04 — Check what your money will buy

Complete authored script. Version `2026-09-28.astra.1`. Canonical course unchanged.

## 1. Start here

### More dollars can still buy less

A balance can grow while prices grow faster. You will compare dollar amounts with purchasing power, then test the effect of a fee in a supplied calculator. The purpose is to understand a model and revise a goal, not predict the next year's inflation or investment return.

Your final product is an updated saving instruction after a fictional course-material price changes. You will calculate the new cost, find the gap and choose a feasible adjustment.

**How to complete this lesson:** Read **A basket and a balance**, learn **Compare growth on the same time basis**, and follow the purchasing-power example. Use the model to test a fee, then answer six checks. Complete **Riley's materials target**, compare, revise and sign off. The calculator has an equivalent supplied output record; no outside account or spreadsheet is required.

## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### A basket and a balance

A fictional basket contains the same quantities of the same items at both dates. It costs **$100 at the start of the year** and **$103 at the end**. A separate **$100 saving balance grows to $101** over that year, with no deposits, withdrawals, fees or tax in this illustration.

The basket is a teaching model, not Canada's actual Consumer Price Index (CPI). The 3% change is not a current inflation report. A single person's purchases can change differently from an economy-wide average.

**Calculator assumptions:** Starting amount $100; monthly deposit $0; period 1 year; annual effective growth 1%; annual fee 0%; annual inflation assumption 3%. The model applies a constant monthly equivalent growth rate and month-end deposits. It reports dollar balance and purchasing power in start-of-year dollars. Actual returns and prices need not follow constant rates.

**Read the record:** At year-end, can the $101 balance buy the unchanged $103 basket?

A. Yes, because the balance grew. B. No, it is $2 short of that basket's price. C. Yes, because all percentages cancel. D. No, because all $101 has disappeared.

B compares amounts at the same date. A notices growth but ignores price; C invents an offset; D confuses reduced purchasing power with losing the entire balance.

## 3. Learn the method

### Compare growth on the same time basis

**Inflation** is an increase in the general level of prices over time. **Purchasing power** describes what money can buy. A particular price change is useful for a specific goal, but by itself does not measure all inflation. Bank of Canada's *Understanding inflation* explains this distinction through price-level and basket measures; read September 28, 2026.

The **nominal balance** is the number of dollars shown. A **purchasing-power amount** expresses those dollars relative to prices at a chosen starting date. In our model, the balance grows 1% while the basket grows 3%. The nominal number rises, but its ability to buy the original basket falls.

For a target price, a 3% increase means multiply the original amount by 1.03. For purchasing power after that increase, divide the later balance by 1.03. These operations answer different questions. $100 × 1.03 asks what the basket now costs; $101 ÷ 1.03 asks what the balance is worth in the earlier price scale.

### Account for fees as well as growth

A quoted return before fees is not necessarily what remains for the investor. A fixed-dollar fee and a percentage fee can have different effects. You need the amount, base and timing. This calculator treats an annual percentage fee as one-twelfth of that percentage charged each month after growth and before any new deposit. Real products can use other conventions.

Do not simply subtract unrelated percentages without checking their timing. “4% growth minus 1% fee minus 3% inflation is exactly zero” is a useful rough intuition here, but not the exact model. The fee is applied throughout the year and inflation changes the price scale by multiplication. The tool makes the assumptions explicit so a small difference is explainable.

### Use scenarios without turning them into promises

**Appreciation** means an asset rises in value; **depreciation** means it falls. A calculator's chosen growth rate is an input, not a forecast. Market values can move unevenly and may be negative. Fees, deposits and withdrawals also affect the final balance, so a balance change is not automatically an investment return.

When a goal's actual price changes, update the target using that specific information. You can revise the contribution, choose a confirmed suitable lower-cost alternative, or change a flexible deadline. Do not assume a riskier investment will produce exactly the missing amount by a fixed date.

**Hint:** Label every number with its date: “price now,” “price later,” “balance later.” Compare price and balance at the same point before adjusting for purchasing power.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Explain $101 in the new price scale

The basket rises by $103 − $100 = $3, or 3% of its starting cost. The balance rises by $1, or 1%. The $101 balance cannot buy the $103 basket; it needs another $2.

The calculator divides $101 by 1.03, producing **$98.06 in start-of-year purchasing power** after rounding. This is a comparison measure, not a withdrawal from the account. The account still contains $101.

**Complete explanation:** “The nominal balance increased from $100 to $101, but the unchanged basket now costs $103. The balance is $2 short of buying it. In the model's original price scale, $101 ÷ 1.03 is about $98.06, so purchasing power fell even though the dollar balance rose. The 3% is a scenario assumption, not a claim about current Canadian inflation.”

An answer that says “the bank took $2.94” confuses a price-adjusted comparison with a transaction. Nothing in the record says money was removed.

## 5. Practise with support

### Test a fee in the same model

Choose **Load fee comparison**. Set starting amount **$100**, monthly deposit **$0**, years **1**, annual effective growth **4%**, annual fee **1%**, inflation **3%**. Predict whether a fee will leave more or less money than the same model without a fee, then select **Calculate**.

**Supplied output record:** With the fee, nominal balance **$102.96**, purchasing power **$99.97**. With fee changed to 0% and all other inputs unchanged, nominal balance **$104.00**, purchasing power **$100.97**. Outputs round at display only. The fee convention is stated above; these are not real product quotations.

Enter **Displayed nominal difference caused by the fee ($)** and select **Which comparison is supported?**: **same gross return, lower retained balance with the fee**, **fee creates extra return**, **the two cases use different starting money**, or **the result predicts next year**. Write **What the tool helps me see**, two sentences. Use **Check my comparison**.

The displayed difference is $104.00 − $102.96 = **$1.04**. If you enter exactly $1, you have treated the fee as a single charge on the original $100 instead of the supplied monthly convention. If you say the fee causes a $4 loss, you have confused gross growth with the difference between scenarios. The first comparison is supported; the others misstate a cost, a controlled input or the status of a scenario.

**Model:** “With the same starting money and 4% gross growth assumption, the fee reduces the displayed final balance by $1.04. The fee case ends at $102.96 but only $99.97 of start-of-year purchasing power under 3% inflation. The tool shows the combined effect of its stated assumptions, not a guaranteed return.”

For a simpler target check, a fictional $300 item rising by 4% costs $312. If $300 is saved, the gap is $12. Use this method in the independent task, then revise your fee explanation if necessary.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — Purchasing power describes:

A. Only the number printed on a banknote. B. A guarantee of investment growth. C. What money can buy at the relevant prices. D. A fee charged by every bank.

Answer C. A describes nominal units; B invents certainty; C connects money with prices; D confuses a comparison with a charge.

### Q2 — A balance rises 1% while the relevant basket rises 3%. What can happen?

A. Dollar balance rises while purchasing power falls. B. Every price must fall. C. The balance becomes zero. D. The basket's contents must double.

Answer A. A follows the different growth rates; B–D do not follow from the record.

### Q3 — Which input must be known to interpret a fee?

A. Only its advertising colour. B. The number of followers promoting it. C. A friend's unrelated profit. D. Its amount or rate, calculation base and timing.

Answer D. A–C do not establish a charge; D supplies the terms needed for comparison.

### Q4 — A calculator uses 4% growth each year. What does that establish?

A. A promise by the market. B. A conditional output if that model's assumptions hold. C. A guaranteed minimum gain. D. The current inflation rate.

Answer B. A and C turn an input into a promise; B states the model's role; D confuses growth and price inflation.

### Q5 — A $300 target rises 4%. What is the new target?

A. $312. B. $304. C. $288. D. $1,200.

Answer A. A adds $12; B treats 4% as $4; C subtracts the increase; D multiplies by 4 rather than 1.04.

### Q6 — A specific item costs more this month. What can you conclude?

A. Every Canadian purchase rose by the same percentage. B. A riskier investment will cover the difference. C. This goal's price should be updated; the single price is not the whole CPI. D. No saving plan can ever work.

Answer C. A overgeneralizes; B invents a return; C identifies the useful local implication; D overstates one change.

## 7. Apply independently

### Riley's materials target

Riley is a fictional learner saving for required course materials. The original all-in quotation was **$240**. A new confirmed quotation increases that price by **5%** and is valid until payment in **two weeks**. Riley has **$248 already saved** for it.

A confirmed $10 of unallocated money will be available in one week. It can be assigned to this target without affecting other obligations. Alternatively, a suitable used set is confirmed available for **$245**, all-in, until the same deadline. Both sets meet the course requirement; the used set has cosmetic wear. No interest or market gain is assumed.

Enter **New quoted price ($)** and **Gap compared with $248 saved ($)**. Choose **add money for the new set** or **buy the confirmed used set**. Write **My updated plan for Riley**, 3–4 sentences explaining the calculation, timing, remaining money and one tradeoff.

**Complete model, new set:** “The updated price is $240 × 1.05 = $252. With $248 saved, Riley is $4 short. I would allocate $4 of the confirmed $10 next week, reaching $252 before the two-week deadline and leaving $6 of that new money unallocated. This keeps the new-set choice but gives up $7 compared with the $245 used option.”

**Alternative model:** “The new quotation is $252, $4 above current savings. I would choose the confirmed suitable used set for $245, leaving $3 of the $248 saved and keeping next week's $10 available. The tradeoff is accepting cosmetic wear; the case confirms it still meets the requirement.”

Neither plan needs an invented investment gain. Compare and revise your chosen plan.
