## 2. Meet the situation and read the documents

### Kai's starting evidence

Kai is a fictional 19-year-old who has completed Grade 12. During eight community workshop sessions, Kai checked part labels against an inventory list, explained completed steps to visitors and asked the workshop leader before unfamiliar work. Kai has not had a paid repair-shop job. Kai likes practical work and explaining it, wants to develop technical knowledge and values a predictable short weekly shift.

A **work offer record** describes what a particular employer is offering. A **venture record** organizes a person's proposed sales and expenses. The documents below are original fiction, with invented prices and wages; they are not current offers or legal guidance.

**Document K1 — Cedar Cycle shop helper offer**

One four-hour Saturday helper shift at $18 per hour, before payroll deductions. The shop schedules customers, supplies tools and materials, handles payments and directs the work. Kai would check inventory, explain the service-booking process and ask the supervisor about unfamiliar requests. Training for those duties is provided. Kai is not being hired as an independent repair technician. This record confirms one offered shift only; later hours are unknown.

**Document K2 — Fictional one-week service scenario**

For the scenario, five service jobs are completed under appropriate workshop supervision and all five customers pay $30. Replacement parts cost $75 in total; transport and other supplies cost $25 in total. Kai spends six hours on the work and administration. The record does not price equipment wear, insurance, permits, taxes or payment for Kai's time. It supplies no evidence of next week's demand. The activity is a paper case, not permission to operate a real service.

**Document K3 — Kai's initial claim**

“Five customers paid $30, so I made $150 profit. If I like repairs, running my own business must be the best route.”

**Reading check:** Which responsibility belongs to the shop in K1? Select one and use **Check my reading**.

A. Making Kai's entire career decision. B. Scheduling customers and handling payments. C. Guaranteeing every later week's hours. D. Proving Kai is already an independent technician.

Feedback: **B** is explicitly assigned to the shop. **A** is not an employer's promise. **C** exceeds the single offered shift. **D** contradicts the role description.

## 3. Learn the method

### Compare the way of working as well as the task

An **employee** works within an employer's direction and arrangements. A **self-employed person** is responsible for obtaining work from clients rather than being on an employer's payroll. **Entrepreneurship** involves developing an opportunity and taking on the uncertainty of offering a product or service. A venture can be small; size alone does not remove its responsibilities.

The same interest can appear in either route. Kai may enjoy bicycles while preferring the shop's scheduling and supervision. Or Kai may want to learn about pricing and customer needs as well as technical work. Neither preference makes a person more capable or more ambitious. The useful question is which responsibilities and supports fit the current goal.

**Dated reference record:** ALIS's *Choose the Way You Work*, read September 28, 2026, distinguishes employee arrangements from self-employment and discusses finding clients, unpaid business-development time and learning business skills. This lesson's two records provide the details to compare. [Optional original](https://alis.alberta.ca/look-for-work/work-options/choose-the-way-you-work/).

### Follow the money through the record

**Revenue** is money from sales before expenses. An **expense** is a cost of providing the product or service. Subtracting the listed expenses gives an amount remaining under the supplied assumptions. If some costs are not recorded, do not label that remainder complete profit or take-home pay.

Use the flow **number of paid sales × price → revenue → subtract listed costs → remainder before unlisted costs/time**. It keeps a sales headline from hiding the resources used to earn it. A fixed cost stays the same in the particular scenario; a per-item cost repeats for every item. Fewer sales do not necessarily reduce every expense.

Wages before payroll deductions are also different from take-home pay. Four hours at $18 produces a gross wage calculation, not proof of the amount deposited. Comparing that wage directly with a venture's incomplete remainder as if both were final income would mislead. Explain the categories and missing information before making a recommendation.

### Test an opportunity without pretending demand is certain

A person liking an idea is not the same as paying the proposed price. One week's sales do not establish reliable future demand. Consider who needs the service, what alternatives they have and what conditions they will accept. Customer feedback can justify changing the size, timing or scope of an offer. It does not justify removing a necessary safety step or pretending a material cost has disappeared.

For this course, testing means using supplied scenario cards and calculations. A next learning step can be making a more complete cost record or revising an explanation. No real purchase, customer research or unsupervised work is required.

### Prepare truthful evidence for an employment route

A résumé summarizes relevant skills, education and experience in an organized record. A basic structure is a name/contact heading, a short relevant summary, skills supported by examples, dated experience entries and education/training. Real applications may need contact information; a public practice page should not display private addresses, identification numbers or other people's information.

Using only K1 and Kai's starting evidence, this compact fictional résumé section would be truthful:

**Kai — practice candidate; private contact details omitted**

**Summary:** Interested in supervised bicycle-shop helper work and developing technical knowledge.

**Relevant experience:** Community workshop participant, eight sessions. Checked part labels against an inventory list; explained completed steps to visitors; asked the leader before unfamiliar work.

**Education:** Grade 12 completed in this fictional case.

This describes experience without changing it into a paid job. A **cover letter** is a message to a particular employer explaining why selected evidence fits the opening. For K1, a truthful sentence would be: “My workshop experience checking labels against a list relates to your inventory duty.” A **portfolio** contains selected examples with explanations, rather than every file a person owns. An **online employment profile** presents relevant background on a digital service; it is not a reason to post private documents publicly. No résumé, message or profile is sent in this lesson.

For example, a portfolio could contain a blank sample inventory checklist that Kai created to demonstrate the checking method, accompanied by a note explaining the task and Kai's contribution. A visitor's identifiable repair form would be a poor public sample: it could reveal someone else's details and would not by itself explain Kai's work. Select the minimum relevant evidence and remove private information. A cover letter then points the employer to the useful example; it does not need to retell every item in the résumé.

**Optional hint:** Before subtracting, label each amount “sales,” “listed cost,” “gross wage” or “not supplied.” That label often reveals a misleading comparison.

## 4. Follow a complete example

### Correct Kai's claim and compare the routes

The claim starts with a valid multiplication: five paid jobs × $30 = $150 revenue. The error is naming that entire amount profit. The record lists $75 parts and $25 other costs. Subtracting both leaves $50. Time and other potential costs have not been valued, so I keep that boundary in the sentence.

The shop offer provides a different calculation: four hours × $18 = $72 gross wages for the one offered shift. That is not a complete after-deduction amount. It also comes with different responsibilities: the shop handles booking, equipment and customer payments, while Kai works under its direction.

**Completed comparison:** “K2 records $150 revenue, $100 listed costs and $50 remaining before unlisted costs or payment for Kai's six hours. It does not prove $150 profit or recurring weekly sales. K1 offers $72 gross wages for one supervised four-hour shift, with the shop handling customers, tools and payments. I would investigate the shop helper route first because Kai wants technical learning and a predictable short shift. The service idea could remain a learning project for understanding costs and customer needs. Before treating it as an earning plan, its missing costs and repeat demand would need evidence.”

“Employment means no responsibility” would also be wrong. Kai still needs accuracy, truthful communication and appropriate requests for help. The distinction is which responsibilities each record assigns, not whether one route requires effort.

## 5. Practise with support

### Fewer customers change the same venture

**New scenario card K4:** For a second fictional week, only three customers agree to the $30 price and all three pay after the work. Parts cost $15 per job. The week's transport/supplies charge remains $25. Kai's time and the other unlisted costs are still excluded. The paper scenario still assumes appropriate supervision; no learner performs repairs.

Select which cost repeats per job: **the $25 weekly charge / the $15 parts charge / every cost becomes zero after one sale**. Use **Check the cost type**. The second choice is per job. The first is fixed for this week. The third has no support.

Enter **Three-job revenue ($)**, **Listed costs ($)** and **Amount remaining ($)**. Use **Check my calculation**. Then write **What changed in the opportunity** and **A safe next learning step**.

**Optional hint:** Keep $25 once; multiply $15 by the number of jobs. A smaller revenue total does not automatically mean every cost shrinks equally.

**Calculation feedback:** The supported results are $90 revenue, $70 listed costs and $20 remaining. A $40 remainder likely omits part of the weekly charge; a $45 cost total omits it entirely. For any other mismatch, show “3 × $30 − (3 × $15) − $25” and identify the first field that differs. Feedback never treats the incomplete remainder as final profit.

**Compare with the opportunity model:** “Revenue falls to $90 while costs are $45 parts plus $25, leaving $20 before the excluded costs and time. Fewer paid jobs reduce this week's remainder, so the first week's results did not establish a stable income. I would use the supplied cards to make a complete cost checklist and compare a smaller service schedule before making a claim about viability. I would not remove necessary work or ask real customers for money to finish this lesson.”

Use **Return to my practice** to revise both calculations and explanation.

## 6. Check your understanding

### Q1 — What is revenue?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-06:q1`.

A. Money from sales before expenses. B. Guaranteed take-home pay. C. Every future week's profit. D. The cost of materials.

Answer: A. A defines sales revenue. B skips deductions/costs. C invents future results. D describes an expense.

### Q2 — A venture has $90 sales and $70 listed costs, with time unpriced. What is supported?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-06:q2`.

A. $90 profit. B. No work was needed. C. $20 remains before unlisted costs and time. D. $20 is guaranteed personal income every week.

Answer: C. A ignores expenses. B contradicts the scenario. C preserves the arithmetic and boundary. D invents completeness and repetition.

### Q3 — What distinguishes the shop route in K1?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-06:q3`.

A. Kai has no duties. B. It guarantees a lifelong job. C. It turns workshop experience into expert status. D. The employer supplies defined systems and direction for the offered shift.

Answer: D. A ignores duties. B exceeds the record. C invents expertise. D matches the supplied arrangement.

### Q4 — Three people say an idea sounds interesting. What does that establish?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-06:q4`.

A. They will all pay the proposed price. B. They expressed interest; paid demand remains unconfirmed. C. Every cost is covered. D. The business can operate safely under any conditions.

Answer: B. A treats an opinion as a purchase. B keeps the evidence limited. C requires cost and sales records. D requires separate evidence.

### Q5 — Which résumé statement is truthful for Kai?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-06:q5`.

A. Guaranteed every repair independently. B. Managed a repair shop for years. C. Was the employer's head technician. D. Checked inventory labels during eight community workshop sessions.

Answer: D. D matches the starting evidence. B invents employment and duration. C invents a role. A conflicts with the supervised/ask-for-help boundary.

### Q6 — What is an appropriate in-course next step for investigating the venture?

Implementation key: `practice:ce1-06:q6`.

A. Buy tools immediately. B. Use supplied records to identify unlisted costs and test a revised paper scenario. C. Collect strangers' contact details. D. Promise customers an unverified deadline.

Answer: B. A adds unrequested real spending. C adds unnecessary personal-data collection. B develops the reasoning with available evidence. D creates an unsupported promise.

## 7. Apply independently

### Samir's handmade-bookmark interest

Samir is a fictional learner who enjoys making textile bookmarks and explaining choices to other people. Samir wants to keep learning the craft, has no paid retail experience and prefers a predictable weekly commitment. A school craft display provides evidence: Samir labelled materials, explained two colour options to visitors and checked stock before promising that an item was available.

**New employment record S1 — Thread & Paper shop:** One offered four-hour helper shift at $17 per hour before deductions. The employer schedules the shift, supplies stock, sets prices and handles payments. The helper labels stock, explains available options and asks the supervisor about unfamiliar requests. Later hours are not promised.

**New venture record S2 — Fictional four-sale trial:** Four bookmarks sell for $20 each. Materials cost $8 per item, selling costs are $3 per item, and five hours are spent making, presenting and recording the sales. Equipment wear, taxes and payment for Samir's time are not calculated. Future buyers are unknown. These are supplied fictional sales, not an instruction to sell.

Create **Venture amount remaining ($)** and **Samir's route comparison**. Show revenue and both cost calculations; compare the employment and venture responsibilities; state what the amounts do and do not mean; recommend a next learning step tied to Samir's goals. Add **Truthful employment-profile snippet**: one or two sentences using only the craft-display evidence, without private contact details or claims of paid retail experience. About 180–260 words across writing fields is a guide.

**Compare with a supported response**

“The venture produces 4 × $20 = $80 revenue. Materials are $32 and selling costs $12, leaving $36 before equipment wear, tax or payment for five hours. The shop offers 4 × $17 = $68 gross wages for one shift; that is not a supplied take-home amount. In the shop, the employer handles stock, prices and payments, while Samir follows its processes. The venture also requires finding buyers, costing, selling and record keeping. I would investigate the shop helper route first because Samir values a predictable commitment and can develop customer explanations. I would keep the venture as a paper learning exercise and make a fuller cost/time record before claiming recurring profit. Neither route promises future work.”

**Supported profile snippet:** “Interested in supervised craft-retail work. At a school display, I labelled materials, explained two colour options and checked stock before promising availability.”

A venture-first learning preference is defensible if the answer retains the uncertainty and proposes an in-course, evidence-building step. The task does not require a real venture or account. Compare and revise your fields.
