Pause, verify, then act — narrated visual draft
Status: draft for factual, caption, visual, and teacher review.
Voice: macOS Samantha system voice (synthetic narration).
All stated scenarios and dollar values are fictional unless a slide explicitly labels a general safety process.

Slide 1: Two messages, two requests
Visual description: Read the action—not just the logo. The slide displays: A  “Move your money to our safe account. Send the code.”; B  “A new tax notice is available.”. Key visual: What does each contact want you to do?
Narration: Two fictional contacts arrive. A bank-looking text says a transfer is at risk and asks the person to move money to a “safe account” and reply with a one-time code. A second notice says a tax message is waiting and tells the person to sign in through the usual government route. A familiar logo or official-sounding name does not identify the sender. Start by underlining the action each message requests and decide whether it is reversible or sensitive.

Slide 2: Spot pressure and sensitive requests
Visual description: A cue raises concern; verify before acting. The slide displays: Urgency or threat; Transfer to a “safe” account; One-time code or password; Remote access or unusual payment. Key visual: Cue ≠ proof • pause before an irreversible action.
Narration: Scams often create urgency, secrecy, threats, or an unexpected reward. Requests to transfer money to a “safe account,” buy gift cards, use cryptocurrency, install remote-access software, or share a password or one-time code are high-risk. Poor spelling can be a clue, but polished writing does not prove a message is real. A cue is evidence to slow down, not a complete identity test. Do not click, reply, transfer, or share secrets while pressured.

Slide 3: Verify through a separate route
Visual description: Do not use the contact path supplied by the suspicious message. The slide displays: PAUSE; Find the official service independently; Ask if the request is real; Act only through a verified route. Key visual: Known app • saved statement • official website typed independently
Narration: For a bank concern, open the known app or use contact details from a bank card or statement. Do not call a number included only in the unexpected text, and do not trust caller ID by itself. For a web notice, navigate to the known official site yourself rather than following a message link. If uncertain, talk to a trusted adult and ask the institution through an independently found route. A search result or advertisement may also be misleading; use the known official domain.

Slide 4: CRA contact needs a precise rule
Visual description: CRA may contact people; caller ID is not proof. The slide displays: CRA may call, write, or send notifications; Caller ID can be spoofed; Never share passwords or security codes; Ask, end, verify independently if unsure. Key visual: Use the CRA contact-verification tool or Canada.ca details.
Narration: Do not memorize the false rule “CRA never calls.” The Canada Revenue Agency may contact people by phone and other channels. A caller ID that displays a government name still does not prove who is calling. If a person is unsure, they can ask for the caller’s name, office, and callback number, end the call, and check using CRA’s official contact-verification tool or contact details found independently on Canada.ca. CRA may request some information for a legitimate identity check or missing tax information, but never share passwords or one-time security codes with an unexpected caller.

Slide 5: Pause: classify before you respond
Visual description: Name the action, cue, and independent check. The slide displays: Message A: requested action?; Message B: safe route?; What remains uncertain?. Key visual: Pause and write one sentence for each contact.
Narration: For the two fictional contacts, identify the requested action, one warning cue, and the verification route. Pause before choosing.
Pause: 7 seconds of silent think time follows the spoken question before the worked explanation.

Slide 6: Worked answer: safe without guessing
Visual description: Verify the contact; do not make an irreversible move. The slide displays: A  Do not transfer or share the code; contact the bank independently.; B  Navigate to Canada.ca yourself and check for the notice.; Unknown sender identity? Verify before acting.. Key visual: Appearance is a clue—not authentication.
Narration: For message A, the safe-account transfer and code request are high-risk. Do not transfer or reply; contact the bank independently. For message B, the notification may be legitimate, but this fictional card is not proof. Navigate to Canada.ca yourself and check the account through the official route. If a caller claims to be CRA, remember that CRA can call, but caller ID can be spoofed. Ask for callback details, end the call, and verify independently if unsure. The lesson is neither “trust it” nor “all contacts are fake.” It is pause, verify, then act. If a learner already clicked or shared information, the response changes: stop interacting, secure the affected account from a trusted device, and promptly contact the relevant institution through an independently found route. Preserve the message and time, but do not reopen its link to collect evidence. If money was lost, contact local police promptly and report through current Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre guidance. Reporting does not guarantee recovery. A second person offering guaranteed reimbursement for an upfront fee is another reason to pause and verify.
