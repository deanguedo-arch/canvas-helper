Stop, identify, and control a hazard — narrated visual draft
Status: draft for factual, caption, visual, and teacher review.
Voice: macOS Samantha system voice (synthetic narration).
All stated scenarios and dollar values are fictional unless a slide explicitly labels a general safety process.

Slide 1: The first shift
Visual description: A request is not the same as safe information. The slide displays: UNLABELLED LIQUID; POOR VENTILATION; SUPERVISOR BUSY. Key visual: What is unknown?
Narration: A first shift is a time to ask, not guess. Devin is asked to use a cleaning liquid beside a storage area with poor ventilation. There is no readable label, and the supervisor is helping another worker. A coworker says, “It is probably the usual cleaner.” That description is not enough to identify the substance or its safe use. Pause the task. We will separate what Devin observed from what still needs confirmation.

Slide 2: Name the hazard and the gap
Visual description: Describe evidence before deciding what the substance is. The slide displays: OBSERVED: container has no readable label; CONDITION: air feels stale; ventilation uncertain; UNKNOWN: contents, exposure risk, required controls. Key visual: Observed fact ≠ confirmed identity
Narration: A hazard is a source or condition that could cause harm. Here, the unlabeled container creates uncertainty; the ventilation condition may affect exposure. Devin should not infer the chemical from its colour, smell, or a coworker’s guess. The missing information includes the product identity, its instructions, possible exposure routes, and the site procedure. This is a case about a missing safety control, not about diagnosing a chemical or deciding whether a law was broken.

Slide 3: Use the three worker rights
Visual description: Know • Participate • Refuse dangerous work The slide displays: KNOW: ask for hazard information and training; PARTICIPATE: report the concern and suggest a control; REFUSE: follow the applicable process if danger remains. Key visual: Rights guide a safe process
Narration: Canadian workplace safety guidance describes three worker rights: to know about hazards, to participate in health and safety decisions, and to refuse work that may endanger someone. Devin can request the product label, the relevant safety information, instruction, and supervision. Devin can report the concern and take part in choosing a safer method. If the hazard remains, the worker follows the applicable refusal process; it is not a dare or an invitation to confront someone alone. Details belong to the current Alberta process and workplace.

Slide 4: Choose an immediate control
Visual description: Reduce exposure before collecting more detail. The slide displays: 1  STOP the task; 2  KEEP CLEAR if safe; 3  TELL the supervisor; 4  WAIT for trained direction. Key visual: Never smell • mix • pour • test
Narration: The safe immediate move is to stop before handling the container. If it is safe, Devin should keep other people away from the area and alert the supervisor or designated trained person. Devin should not smell the liquid, mix it with another cleaner, pour it down a drain, or carry it to find a label. Those actions can create exposure or a reaction. If there is immediate serious danger, move to safety and follow the site emergency procedure.

Slide 5: Pause: make the decision
Visual description: Name the hazard, missing evidence, and next safe action. The slide displays: What is observed?; What must be confirmed?; What should Devin do now?. Key visual: Pause the video and write a 1–2 sentence response.
Narration: Before Devin acts: name the hazard, the missing information, and the safest next step. Pause here and decide.
Pause: 7 seconds of silent think time follows the spoken question before the worked explanation.

Slide 6: Worked response and follow-up
Visual description: State facts, ask for controls, and close the loop. The slide displays: DO NOT handle the unknown liquid; Ask for identity, information, training, and procedure; Record the concern through the site process. Key visual: A good response is specific—not dramatic.
Narration: A strong response says: the container has no readable label, ventilation is uncertain, and Devin has not received the safe procedure. Devin pauses, keeps clear if safe, alerts the supervisor, and asks for the identity, hazard information, training, and approved control before using it. The misconception is that a familiar-looking product or coworker’s confidence makes handling safe. It does not. After the hazard is addressed, Devin records the outcome and follows up. For an actual workplace, check current Alberta guidance and site instructions; this example does not decide a legal case. Before closing, Devin checks who will identify the liquid and what training is needed before anyone resumes the task. If no trained person is available, the area remains controlled and the concern is escalated through the site process. The follow-up is not “clean it up later”; it is to confirm that a responsible person has identified the product, selected controls, and explained how to work safely. This makes the handoff concrete and prevents the unknown hazard from becoming the next shift’s problem.
