ADH and water balance
How does the body reduce water loss when its fluids become too concentrated?
Learning goal
Trace ADH production, release and kidney action, then predict urine changes and distinguish causes of diabetes insipidus.
Before you begin
Water can move by osmosis. Reabsorption returns material from kidney tubules to the blood rather than sending it out as urine.

Follow the water before following the hormone
After losing water in sweat, a person with normally responding kidneys usually produces a smaller volume of urine. Why would conserving water make the urine more concentrated while helping the blood become less concentrated? The answer depends on keeping two fluid spaces separate.
Blood plasma
is the liquid part of blood.
Forming urine
is the fluid inside kidney tubules that is being processed before it leaves the body. A nephron is a working unit of the kidney; its tubules receive filtered fluid, and a
collecting duct
carries fluid onward toward the urine outlet. The duct wall separates that fluid from surrounding tissue and nearby blood vessels. They are not one open space.
Water
reabsorption
means water moving from the forming urine, across the tubule or duct wall and surrounding tissue, back toward the blood. Reabsorbed water stays in the body instead of leaving in urine. This changes how much water is left with the dissolved substances in each space. Concentration compares the amount of dissolved substance with the amount of fluid; a smaller urine volume can therefore be more concentrated without containing a larger total amount of waste.
Antidiuretic hormone
, abbreviated
ADH
and also called vasopressin, helps regulate this movement. Neurons in the
hypothalamus
make ADH. The hormone moves along their axons to the
posterior pituitary
, where it is stored and released into blood. Blood carries ADH to responsive kidney cells. The release site supplies a message; the kidneys perform the water-conserving response.

Connect water loss to a kidney response
Suppose water is lost without an equivalent loss of dissolved substances. Body fluids, including plasma, become more concentrated.
Osmoreceptors
are cells sensitive to changes in this osmotic concentration. Osmoreceptors in the
hypothalamus
help detect the change and support increased ADH release from the
posterior pituitary
. Detection, hormone production and release are related steps, but they are not interchangeable names for the same event.
ADH travels in blood and binds suitable receptors on collecting-duct cells. Signalling inside those cells increases their water permeability: water can cross their membranes more easily. For the predictions here, assume a normally functioning kidney with its usual concentration difference between the duct fluid and surrounding tissue.
Osmosis
is net water movement across a selectively permeable membrane toward the side with a lower availability of free water, commonly the side with a higher effective solute concentration. ADH does not push water or turn it into something else; it changes how readily the water can follow the existing osmotic gradient.
More water is then reabsorbed toward blood. Less water remains in the duct to become urine, so urine volume falls and urine becomes more concentrated. The blood conserves water that would otherwise have been lost. Read this chain aloud without skipping the kidney mechanism:
higher body-fluid concentration → osmoreceptor signal → increased ADH release → greater collecting-duct water permeability → increased water reabsorption → smaller, more concentrated urine volume.
Notice the condition attached to that prediction: the kidneys must respond normally. A hormone arriving in blood does not guarantee that its target can carry out the response. We will use that distinction to explain a failure of water conservation.
Use the return effect to identify
negative feedback
Conserving water opposes the original rise in body-fluid concentration. As that disturbance is reduced, the osmoreceptor drive for extra ADH release becomes smaller. This is negative feedback: the response weakens the change that started it. It does not mean that ADH harms the body, or that all ADH secretion permanently stops.
Follow the return line in the overview diagram. It represents the effect of corrected concentration or volume on the release stimulus. It is not a second stream carrying ADH backward from the kidney to the brain. The concentration-focused explanation in this lesson is sufficient for the required water-balance questions; the textbook also shows a pressure-related branch.
The opposite starting condition helps test the reasoning. If a person takes in water and their body fluids become less concentrated, the drive for ADH release decreases. Collecting ducts become less water-permeable, water
reabsorption
decreases, and a larger volume of more dilute urine can leave the body. Losing more water in this situation helps oppose the dilution. Both directions are negative feedback because each response counteracts its own starting disturbance.
Keep three statements separate:
blood concentration
describes the fluid being regulated;
water reabsorption
describes movement back toward blood;
urine concentration
describes what remains for excretion. They need not change in the same direction. During successful conservation, blood is protected against becoming too concentrated while the smaller urine volume becomes more concentrated.
Use “increased” and “decreased” as comparisons with the earlier condition. The diagram does not claim that a low-ADH kidney reabsorbs no water or that a high-ADH kidney makes no urine.
Locate the failed step before predicting a correction
Diabetes insipidus
involves impaired water conservation and can cause unusually large volumes of dilute urine. Two mechanisms are important here. In
central diabetes insipidus
, too little ADH is available because its production or release is impaired. The kidney may still be able to respond, but it receives insufficient signal. In
nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
, the kidneys respond inadequately to ADH. The signal can be present while the target response is impaired.
These mechanisms can produce a similar observation: too much water stays in the forming urine and is lost from the body. This explains an apparent contradiction. A person can lose large amounts of
dilute urine
while the water remaining in the body becomes insufficient and the body fluids become
too concentrated
. “Dilute” describes the outgoing urine; it does not prove that the person has plenty of water left.
Replacing a missing
hormone
signal can help a deficiency case when the receiving kidney cells work. Replacing the signal alone does not repair a kidney-response defect. This is a comparison of biological mechanisms, not a diagnosis or a treatment instruction. A case must state or supply evidence about the failed step; urine volume alone cannot identify it.
Reading clarification:
the ADH-treatment statement on textbook p.441 describes inadequate ADH production. Do not extend it to every form of diabetes insipidus. The deficiency and resistance mechanisms require different reasoning, as explained by the
NIDDK overview of diabetes insipidus
.
Diabetes insipidus and diabetes mellitus share part of a name and can both involve increased urination, but they are different regulatory problems. Diabetes mellitus concerns blood-glucose regulation; the cases here concern ADH and water conservation. Do not choose the hormone from the word “diabetes” alone.
For any water-balance explanation, now ask:
What happened to body-fluid concentration? What signal was released? Could the kidney respond? Where did the water go? How did that affect the original disturbance?

Worked examples
Predict both fluid spaces
Question and givens.
A classroom case states that a student has lost water in sweat, plasma has become more concentrated, and the ADH pathway and kidneys respond normally. Predict the change in urine and explain the feedback. These are supplied conditions, not measurements you must infer from a photograph or symptom.
Identify the disturbance.
There is less water relative to dissolved substances in plasma. Name the rising concentration, rather than using “exercise” as though it were itself the regulated variable.
Locate detection and release.
Hypothalamic osmoreceptors detect the concentration change. More ADH, made in hypothalamic neurons, is released from the posterior pituitary into blood.
Explain the target mechanism.
ADH reaches responsive collecting-duct cells and increases water permeability. With the normal osmotic gradient, more water crosses from forming urine toward blood.
Predict the outgoing fluid.
With less water left in the duct, urine volume decreases and urine concentration increases. Saying “the blood is concentrated, so the urine must be dilute” would skip the kidney response and give the wrong prediction for these givens.
Close the loop.
Conserving water opposes further concentration of body fluids. As the disturbance is corrected, the stimulus for extra ADH release falls. The response is negative feedback.
Conclusion.
The hormone does not add new water to the body. It reduces water loss, so replenishing lost water and conserving it are different processes.
Change one crucial condition: the kidney cannot respond
New givens.
Keep the concentrated body fluids, but now the case states that ADH is available and the collecting-duct response is impaired. Predict whether increasing the signal alone guarantees conservation.
Keep what still works.
The signal is available; this is not evidence of inadequate hormone production.
Locate the missing link.
The duct does not increase water permeability normally in response to ADH. The target step, not simply delivery, is defective.
Trace the consequence.
Too much water can remain in urine and leave the body. Urine can stay large in volume and dilute while the remaining body fluids become too concentrated.
Evaluate the proposed correction.
Extra signal alone does not guarantee repair of the response machinery. Contrast a stated ADH-deficiency case with functioning kidneys: supplying the missing signal could then enable water conservation.
Check your reasoning.
If your two explanations predict the same normal kidney response despite the changed given, return to the permeability step. The difference between a missing signal and a missing response is the reason the prediction changes.
