Aldosterone, sodium and water

How can conserving sodium help conserve water without being the same mechanism as ADH?

Learning goal

Explain aldosterone’s kidney effects, connect them to volume and pressure, and distinguish its control from cortisol control.

Before you begin

ADH changes kidney water permeability. Sodium reabsorption and water permeability are connected but different processes.

Start with the substance returned to blood

Two hormones can help conserve body fluid while changing different processes in the kidney. To understand aldosterone, begin with sodium ions rather than answering every kidney question with “saves water.” The immediate movement explains how the later fluid effect becomes possible.

is a released by the , the outer region of each adrenal gland. It belongs to the group, involved in regulation of ions and body fluid. Sodium ions are written Na+, and potassium ions K+; the plus signs indicate electrical charge, not an increase in their amounts.

A nephron is a kidney's working unit. Fluid travels through its tubules and onward through a before leaving as urine. Forming urine and blood are separated fluid spaces. Sodium returns sodium from tubule fluid through the tissue toward blood, reducing the amount available to leave the body in urine.

Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption in responsive cells in the later nephron and collecting system. It also increases potassium secretion into the forming urine. In this kidney context, secretion means movement toward the tubule fluid for potential excretion; it is not the adrenal gland releasing a hormone. Sodium and potassium are being handled in different directions.

Read the overview from the control signals on the left through cortical release to the kidney response. The Na+ statement means more sodium returned toward blood. The K+ statement means more potassium moved into forming urine. The final box is a consequence of salt and water handling, not proof that aldosterone directly opens every water pathway.

Retaining sodium helps retain water when water can follow by osmosis, net water movement across a water-permeable boundary in response to differences in effective solute concentration. Water availability, gradients and permeability still matter, including the permeability changes controlled by ADH. Conserving sodium does not mean water can cross an impermeable wall.

Retention of salt and water can support extracellular fluid volume, including , the amount of blood in the circulation. Greater maintained circulating volume can help support blood pressure. Pressure also depends on the heart and blood vessels; the lesson's chain describes a contribution, not its only controller.

Follow the kidney signal to the

If blood flow or pressure reaching the kidneys falls, the kidneys can release renin, an enzyme that begins a signalling sequence. An enzyme helps a chemical reaction occur; renin is not and is not made by the pituitary. The sequence leads to formation of , which stimulates aldosterone release from the adrenal cortex.

The pathway is called the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, or RAAS. For this lesson, follow the functional links: reduced kidney blood supply → renin-mediated pathway → angiotensin II → aldosterone → increased → supported salt and water retention. You do not need to invent missing molecular intermediates to explain the stated control relationship.

As circulating volume and kidney blood supply recover, the original drive for renin release decreases. That reduces this source of further aldosterone stimulation. Feedback therefore opposes the starting disturbance; it does not require all normal aldosterone secretion to stop.

Blood potassium is another major signal. Increased potassium can stimulate aldosterone release directly from the cortex. Aldosterone-supported potassium secretion by the kidney then helps remove potassium in urine. This branch responds to an ion level, so an aldosterone increase does not by itself prove that blood pressure was low.

ACTH can have effects on aldosterone secretion, but it is not its principal sustained controller. The figure's ACTH caution keeps that distinction visible.

Compare the immediate action before the shared result

and ADH can both contribute to fluid conservation, but they have different sources and immediate kidney actions. ADH means antidiuretic hormone. It is made in hypothalamic neurons, stored and released from the posterior pituitary, and increases water permeability in responsive collecting-duct cells. That makes it easier for water to leave forming urine when the appropriate osmotic gradient is present.

By contrast, aldosterone changes sodium and potassium handling. Increased conserves sodium; water retention is linked to that solute handling but still depends on water being able to move. The distinction is between changing the route available to water and changing how an important ion is conserved.

In both cases, finish the explanation by following water movement and relating the result to the starting condition.

Do not use “both save water” as a substitute for these mechanisms. It names a possible shared consequence but hides the sources, controls and first changes at the kidney. Their effects can work together without being interchangeable.

Distinguish a damaged gland from a missing upstream signal

The makes more than one . Damage there can impair production and, in some cases, production. Addison's disease is primary adrenal insufficiency: the problem is in the adrenal glands themselves. The effects depend on which hormone outputs are reduced and by how much.

Follow two separate consequences when a case states that both are low. Reduced cortisol can impair the metabolic support that helps make glucose and other fuels available. Reduced aldosterone means less support for kidney sodium conservation; sodium and water can be lost more readily, contributing to reduced circulating volume and low blood pressure. Reduced potassium secretion can also disturb potassium regulation.

The common damaged region does not make the two explanations identical. For each hormone, name its normal action and then explain the consequence of losing that action. A list saying “low hormones cause weakness” does not locate the affected mechanisms.

A pituitary problem is different from destruction of the cortex. Inadequate ACTH can reduce cortisol stimulation, but it does not automatically remove aldosterone output, whose principal controls include angiotensin II and potassium. Keep the stated failure location in your reasoning rather than assigning every adrenal-related problem the same pattern.

These are biological case comparisons, not diagnoses from symptoms or treatment instructions. The textbook's historical medical examples are not necessary to decide the pathway questions. Worked examples

Connect a kidney response to support for blood pressure

Question and givens. A classroom case states that kidney blood supply has fallen, the renin–angiotensin pathway responds normally, the adrenal cortex can release aldosterone, and kidney targets and water movement respond normally. Explain how this pathway can help conserve volume.

  1. Identify the starting disturbance. Reduced kidney blood supply can increase renin release. Renin begins a pathway leading to angiotensin II; it is not the final adrenal hormone.
  2. Locate the hormone source. Angiotensin II stimulates the adrenal cortex to release aldosterone into blood.
  3. State the immediate kidney action. Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption from forming urine toward blood and increases potassium secretion into the forming urine. These are opposite directions for different ions.
  4. Explain the water connection. Sodium conservation supports water retention when water is available and can follow the osmotic gradient. The given normal water response matters; aldosterone is not simply another name for ADH.
  5. Connect volume and pressure. Retained salt and water help maintain extracellular and circulating volume, which can support blood pressure. This is one contribution alongside heart and blood-vessel function.
  6. Close the return effect. As kidney blood supply improves, the drive for extra renin decreases, reducing this stimulus for further aldosterone release.

Conclusion. Source → sodium handling → water retention → volume/pressure is the important chain. Do not jump directly from the word “aldosterone” to “pressure rises” without explaining the intervening movement.

Change the observation: distinguish permeability from ion transport

New givens. Two separate model cases are described. Case A directly increases the water permeability of collecting ducts. Case B increases return of sodium from tubule fluid toward blood. Identify the most relevant hormone in each and explain why the answer is not identical.

  1. Use Case A's immediate change. Greater water permeability identifies the ADH mechanism. ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary.
  2. Use Case B's immediate change. Increased sodium return identifies aldosterone action from the adrenal cortex. It concerns ion reabsorption, with a linked effect on water conservation.
  3. Compare the shared outcome. Both can support body-fluid retention, but matching an outcome does not erase different sources and kidney actions.
  4. Keep the condition on water. A change in sodium handling alone does not prove normal water movement if a different given says that permeability is impaired.

Check your explanation. If you identified a hormone only from “less urine” or “more water retained,” look for the described first change at the kidney. An outcome can have more than one contributing pathway.
