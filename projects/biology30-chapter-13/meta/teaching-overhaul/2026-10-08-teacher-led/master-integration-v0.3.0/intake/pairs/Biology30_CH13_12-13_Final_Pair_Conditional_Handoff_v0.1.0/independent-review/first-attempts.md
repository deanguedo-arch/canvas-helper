### 6. Written explanation 1: compare meal and between-meal responses
First answer: After a carbohydrate-containing meal, rising blood glucose stimulates pancreatic beta cells to release more insulin. Responsive muscle and fat increase glucose uptake. The liver increases glucose use and glycogen storage and reduces glucose output, helping blood glucose fall toward its usual range. Between meals, falling blood glucose stimulates pancreatic alpha cells to release more glucagon. Glucagon promotes liver glycogen breakdown and glucose release and supports new glucose production, so blood glucose rises toward its usual range. Glycogen stays inside cells; glucose can enter blood. The hormones have antagonistic overall effects, but both loops are negative feedback: each opposes its own starting disturbance, reducing the stimulus for extra secretion as the disturbance is corrected.
Step support: meal trigger, beta source, responsive uptake and liver changes are quoted in item 1; lower-glucose alpha/glucagon and liver breakdown are quoted in item 2; new production is quoted in item 5. Compartments: “glycogen stays inside cells; glucose released from the store can enter blood.” Feedback: “As the original rise in glucose is corrected, the stimulus for extra insulin release decreases” plus item 3. Antagonism: “their overall effects on blood glucose oppose one another.” Imported knowledge: none.

### 7. Written explanation 2: mellitus versus insipidus
First answer: Diabetes mellitus concerns inadequate insulin production or action and elevated blood glucose. Type 1 involves immune-mediated beta-cell loss and severe insulin deficiency; type 2 involves reduced target responsiveness, with secretion potentially insufficient to compensate. If filtered glucose exceeds kidney reabsorption capacity, glucose stays in forming urine, reducing water reabsorption and increasing water loss. Diabetes insipidus instead involves inadequate ADH availability or kidney resistance to it, so the water-conserving response is impaired without requiring high glucose. ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released by the posterior pituitary; it normally increases collecting-duct water permeability. Similar thirst or urine losses therefore do not establish the same hormone problem.
Step support: “immune-mediated loss of pancreatic beta cells causes severe insulin deficiency”; “target tissues respond less effectively to insulin and secretion may be insufficient to compensate”; “If the filtered amount exceeds what can be reabsorbed, glucose remains in forming urine”; “The additional dissolved glucose reduces water reabsorption and increases water loss in urine”; “inadequate ADH availability or kidney resistance impairs the water-conserving response without requiring high blood glucose”; “ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary; it normally increases collecting-duct water permeability.” Last sentence is an application of the distinct supplied mechanisms. Imported knowledge: none.

### 12. Optional transfer writing: failed cortex and intact upstream glands
First answer: ACTH should increase. Cortisol normally inhibits upstream stimulation; low cortisol from the unresponsive adrenal cortex weakens that inhibition. Because the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary are functioning, the CRH → ACTH route can provide stronger stimulation, but the defective cortex may still produce too little cortisol. The rapid adrenal-medulla route is different: sympathetic neurons directly stimulate the medulla to release epinephrine and norepinephrine. It does not require the CRH → ACTH → cortex chain. Cardiac effects support transport and liver glycogen breakdown helps supply fuel.
Step support: “Hypothalamic CRH → anterior-pituitary ACTH → adrenal cortex → cortisol”; “Cortisol supports fuel availability and inhibits upstream stimulation”; “A failed cortex may remain low-output despite stronger stimulation from working control glands”; “Sympathetic neurons directly stimulate the adrenal medulla to release epinephrine/norepinephrine”; “Cardiac effects support transport, and liver glycogen breakdown supports fuel supply.” Predicting increased ACTH is an explicit application of the first three statements to the supplied defect, not an imported pathway. ### 20. Written explanation 1: water loss, meal and immediate threat
First answer: Water loss can concentrate body fluids, stimulating more ADH release from the posterior pituitary; ADH is produced in the hypothalamus. In responsive kidney collecting ducts, greater water permeability returns more water toward blood, leaving less, more concentrated urine. Correcting the disturbance reduces the stimulus, giving negative feedback. After a carbohydrate-containing meal, rising glucose stimulates pancreatic beta cells to release insulin. Responsive muscle/fat uptake and liver glucose use/storage, with reduced liver output, help lower blood glucose. This opposes the rise and reduces extra secretion, also negative feedback. An immediate threat can activate sympathetic neurons, which directly stimulate the adrenal medulla to release epinephrine and norepinephrine. Cardiac effects support transport, and liver glycogen breakdown increases fuel supply. This is neural control of endocrine output, rather than the CRH–ACTH–cortisol route.

### 21. Written explanation 2: correct three absolute claims
First answer: ACTH is released by the anterior pituitary and stimulates the cortisol-producing adrenal-cortex pathway; it does not control every adrenal hormone. Aldosterone is also cortical, but its principal controls are angiotensin II and potassium. The adrenal medulla is directly stimulated by sympathetic neurons to release epinephrine and norepinephrine. A cell responds to a hormone only if it has a suitable receptor and working response machinery; circulating past a cell is not sufficient. Low thyroxine can lead to increased TSH when the pituitary control system is intact, as in primary thyroid underproduction. If the pituitary cannot release enough TSH, thyroxine can instead be low without an appropriate TSH rise. A stronger signal cannot guarantee recovery if the target gland itself cannot respond.
Step support: ACTH/cortisol route in item 12; aldosterone controller sentence in item 13; medulla route in item 16; “A target cell needs a suitable receptor and functioning response machinery. Blood carrying a hormone past a cell does not guarantee a response”; thyroid conditional and upstream failure statements in items 9/15; worked example “Do not assume the failed target gland can respond just because stimulation rises.” Imported knowledge: none.
