Insulin, glucagon and blood glucose
How does the pancreas respond differently after a meal and between meals?
Learning goal
Explain insulin and glucagon as antagonistic regulators, distinguish diabetes types, and interpret glucose patterns without overdiagnosing.
Before you begin
Glucose is a circulating fuel; glycogen is a storage polymer. The liver can change the amount of glucose entering the blood.

Separate the circulating fuel, its store and its signals
A carbohydrate-containing meal can add glucose to blood, while tissues continue using glucose between meals. The pancreas helps coordinate different responses to a rise and a fall. The aim is a suitable circulating level, not keeping the same
hormone
response at maximum strength all day.
Clusters of endocrine cells in the pancreas are called
pancreatic islets
. Their beta cells release
insulin
and their alpha cells release
glucagon
into blood. These are different cell types producing different chemical messages. The pancreas also has exocrine cells that deliver digestive substances through ducts, but the blood-glucose pathways here concern its endocrine role.
Glucose
is a small sugar used as fuel.
Glycogen
is a storage polymer, a molecule built from many linked glucose units, stored especially in liver and muscle cells. A
hormone
is a signal, so glucagon belongs in that category rather than in the fuel or storage category. Similar spelling does not make glucose, glycogen and glucagon interchangeable.
For each loop, identify the starting blood-glucose change, the pancreatic cell and its hormone, the target action, and the effect on the original change. That is the difference between explaining regulation and memorizing a pair of names.
Follow the meal response into a target cell
When blood glucose rises after a carbohydrate-containing meal, pancreatic beta cells release more
insulin
. Insulin binds suitable receptors on responsive cells and changes their activity. In muscle and fat tissue, it can increase glucose uptake. In the liver, it promotes glucose use and storage as
glycogen
while reducing glucose output. These coordinated actions help lower the circulating level toward its usual range.

The liver stores and releases glucose
View larger
After a meal, pancreatic beta cells release insulin, promoting glucose use and storage, including glycogen formation in liver cells. Between meals, pancreatic alpha cells release glucagon, promoting liver glycogen breakdown and glucose release into blood. Glycogen stays inside cells; the released glucose can enter blood. This is only one part of blood-glucose regulation. Insulin also acts on other responsive tissues, including muscle and fat, and glucagon also supports new glucose production by the liver. Muscle glycogen mainly supports the muscle's own fuel needs. Both regulatory responses oppose their initiating glucose disturbance. Hexagons and branching are schematic symbols, not complete chemical structures or a step-by-step biochemical pathway; omitted metabolic intermediates are not shown. Not to scale.

Insulin and glucagon are antagonistic hormones because their overall effects on
blood glucose
oppose one another. That does not mean their individual target mechanisms are identical reversals. The illustration shows one liver contribution, not every action in every tissue.
As the original rise in glucose is corrected, the stimulus for extra insulin release decreases. That is
negative feedback
: the effect opposes the initiating rise. Negative feedback does not require insulin to disappear completely between meals.
Do not treat insulin as a universal door without which every cell is unable to obtain glucose. Different tissues use different uptake mechanisms. For this pathway, name the responsive muscle/fat uptake and liver storage/output effects rather than turning a useful classroom summary into an absolute rule about all cells.
Correct a fall by supplying glucose from the liver
When blood glucose falls between meals, pancreatic alpha cells release more
glucagon
. Its important target here is the liver. Glucagon promotes liver
glycogen
breakdown and glucose release into blood; it also supports new glucose production. Opening a stored supply and making new glucose are distinct ways the liver can contribute.
The response raises blood glucose, opposing the original fall and reducing the stimulus for extra glucagon release. It is therefore
negative feedback
even though the value rises. Decide feedback type by comparing the response with the
starting disturbance
, not by whether the last arrow points up.
Insulin
emphasizes uptake/use/storage when glucose is higher; glucagon emphasizes liver glucose supply when glucose is lower. Their relative activities vary with conditions. They are not necessarily two switches of which one must be completely off.
Read the liver panel precisely:
glycogen stays inside cells; glucose released from the store can enter blood. Muscle glycogen mainly supports the muscle's own fuel needs. The teacher diagram that groups liver and muscle stores together should not be read as equal direct glucagon-driven sources of blood glucose. Also, textbook Figure13.25's fat-to-glucose shortcut is not the mechanism shown here: name liver glycogen breakdown or new liver glucose production.

Reading clarification:
p.459 says type2 can develop into type1 when insulin is needed. Requiring insulin does not change type2 into autoimmune type1. Likewise, the older labels “juvenile” and “adult-onset” are not reliable diagnostic rules. Compare the mechanism, not age or one treatment label.
Too much circulating glucose is called
hyperglycemia
; too little is
hypoglycemia
. These describe a measured condition, not its cause. A single high value or curve does not identify the diabetes type. The
hormone
supply
and the
target response
are different parts of the explanation.
Why can high blood glucose cause water loss? Glucose is filtered into kidney-tubule fluid. If the filtered amount exceeds what can be reabsorbed, glucose remains in forming urine. The additional dissolved glucose reduces water reabsorption and increases water loss in urine. Thirst and dehydration can follow. This is different from
diabetes insipidus
: inadequate ADH availability or kidney resistance impairs the water-conserving response without requiring high blood glucose. ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary; it normally increases collecting-duct water permeability.
With severe insulin deficiency, increased fat use can lead the liver to produce excessive ketones. Ketones can serve as fuels, but excessive accumulation can cause dangerous acid–base disturbance. Do not treat all ketones as inherently toxic or claim that every person with type2 has the same severe insulin deficiency. These are mechanism explanations, not instructions for diagnosing or treating someone.
Worked examples and data support
Trace both corrective directions
Given:
the pancreas and target tissues respond normally. Compare a rise in blood glucose after a meal with a fall between meals.
Name the meal disturbance.
Glucose rises. Beta cells respond with more insulin.
Explain the target action.
Responsive tissues increase uptake/use; the liver promotes storage and reduces glucose output. Blood glucose is pushed back against the rise.
Change the starting condition.
Between meals glucose falls. Alpha cells respond with more glucagon, promoting liver glycogen breakdown and new glucose production.
Compare direction with disturbance.
Liver glucose supply opposes the fall. Both loops are negative feedback; one corrects upward from a low value and the other downward from a high value.
Conclusion.
State the cell, hormone and liver action on each side. “Insulin and glucagon are opposites” alone omits the biological work.
