Calcium regulation: PTH and calcitonin

How can bone serve as both a support structure and a calcium reservoir?

Learning goal

Before you begin

Calcium is needed for processes such as muscle contraction and neural signalling. Blood calcium and calcium stored in bone are different pools.

One element in different places

Bones help support the body, yet some of their calcium can also become available to the blood. How can moving calcium out of a supporting structure help maintain an internal condition? Begin by naming the two places involved, rather than assuming that more calcium anywhere means more calcium everywhere.

Much of the body's calcium is stored in the mineral of bones and teeth. A much smaller circulating pool is important for functions such as nerve signalling, muscle contraction and blood clotting. In this lesson, blood calcium means the calcium level in that circulating pool. The symbol Ca2+ represents a calcium ion with two positive charges. It does not mean two calcium atoms.

Bone is living tissue that is continually renewed. Cells can deposit mineral in bone or release mineral from it. Movement of calcium from bone toward blood can help raise blood calcium; movement into bone removes calcium from the circulating pool. These are changes in distribution, not creation or destruction of the element.

The thyroid and parathyroid glands have similar names and are close together in the neck, but they are distinct glands. The thyroid produces . The small parathyroid glands, usually on the back of the thyroid, produce , abbreviated PTH. Their location is a useful starting point, but the response depends on which gland detects a change and what its hormone makes target tissues do.

For the main pathway, follow a fall in blood calcium. Ask three questions: What signals the need for correction? Which routes can supply or conserve calcium? How does the resulting change affect further hormone release?

PTH coordinates several responses to the same disturbance

When falls, calcium-sensitive cells in the parathyroid glands increase PTH release. The hormone enters blood and reaches responsive tissues. It helps raise the regulated level through several coordinated effects, rather than sending calcium along a single route from one organ to the next.

At bone: PTH promotes processes that mobilize calcium from bone mineral into blood. Mobilize means make a stored substance available. The hormone does not contain the replacement calcium and does not itself dissolve bone; cells in the tissue carry out the response.

At the kidneys: some calcium is present in fluid inside kidney tubules. Increased calcium returns more of it through the tubule wall toward blood, leaving less to be lost in urine. Conservation helps defend blood calcium even though it does not add a new dietary supply.

Through active vitamin D: PTH promotes activation of vitamin D in the kidneys. This active form, called calcitriol, increases calcium absorption across the intestine from its contents into the body. PTH's intestinal effect is therefore indirect: an additional signalling step links the PTH response to increased intestinal absorption. The pathway is PTH → kidney activation of vitamin D → active vitamin D → greater intestinal calcium absorption.

Read the branching overview first. Each branch contributes to the same outcome, higher blood calcium. The branches are not a sequence in which a calcium ion must visit bone, kidney and intestine in that order. Then use the three tissue panels to locate the source of calcium in each route.

In the middle panel, it runs from tubule fluid through the wall toward blood: previously filtered calcium is conserved. In the right panel, it runs from intestinal contents through the intestinal wall toward blood: calcium from food is absorbed, with active vitamin D increasing this process.

The blue circles represent calcium ions and the arrows represent net movement. They are not hormone molecules, measured flow rates or drawings of molecular pumps. The illustration simplifies cell structure and spacing; its kidney panel does not identify a particular nephron segment. It isolates calcium movement rather than showing every mineral regulated by PTH.

As the combined responses raise blood calcium toward a suitable range, the original low-calcium stimulus becomes smaller and the drive for extra PTH release decreases. That return effect makes the response negative feedback. The red return line in the overview represents this reduced stimulus; it is not calcium physically travelling backward along a drawn vessel.

Opposing effects do not require identical control systems

When is higher, the thyroid can release more . Its effects can lower the circulating calcium level. One mechanism is reduced bone breakdown: less stored calcium is released into blood, helping calcium remain in bone. It can also reduce kidney calcium , allowing more calcium to leave in urine.

The textbook describes calcitonin as encouraging calcium uptake or storage in bone. Keep the net direction in view: calcium is retained in the skeletal pool rather than supplied to blood. Do not imagine calcitonin physically carrying calcium into bone or simply running every PTH action backward.

PTH tends to raise the circulating level, whereas calcitonin can lower it. Hormones with opposing effects on a shared variable are called . Here the named variable is blood calcium, not total body calcium or bone size. If you say only that “the hormones do opposite things,” the reader still cannot tell which compartment is being regulated.

The comparison has an important limit. PTH and active vitamin D have major roles in normal calcium regulation; calcitonin has a relatively minor role in adult humans. The pathways also use different mechanisms. PTH's kidney–vitamin D–intestine branch does not justify drawing an equal reversed intestinal branch for calcitonin. Opposite overall effects do not prove equal strength, identical targets or perfectly mirrored control.

Use the two-hormone comparison to explain direction, then use the actual pathway to explain mechanism. A symmetric pair of arrows can be a memory aid, but it cannot replace the more specific biological account.

Name the movement and close the feedback loop

Absorption brings calcium from food in the intestine across the gut wall into the body. returns calcium from kidney-tubule fluid toward blood after it has entered that fluid. Release from bone makes calcium in a stored mineral pool available to blood. The prefix “re-” is a useful clue to the kidney route: material is being returned rather than newly absorbed from a meal.

These routes can produce the same direction of change in while doing different jobs. Intestinal absorption supplies dietary calcium. Renal reabsorption conserves calcium that could otherwise leave in urine. Bone mobilization draws on a reserve. A statement about one route is not evidence that the other two have stopped or that calcium has been created.

To write a complete explanation, connect low blood calcium → increased PTH from parathyroid glands → named target effects → increased blood calcium → reduced stimulus for extra PTH. For the intestinal branch, include active vitamin D. A name and an upward arrow alone do not explain how the correction occurs.

The result depends on functioning target tissues and available calcium. In a simplified case where the intestine cannot increase absorption, the bone and kidney branches can still be considered separately. You should not assume the overall correction will be normal, because one contribution has been limited. This is the same distinction between a signal and an effective target response that helps us interpret other endocrine pathways.

Next, we will change the kind of trigger. Instead of tracking a low circulating mineral level, we will follow an action-ready response in which a neural signal causes an endocrine gland to release hormones.

Worked examples

Explain how several routes oppose one change

Question and givens. A classroom case states that blood calcium has fallen, the parathyroid glands and target tissues function normally, and dietary calcium is available in the intestine. Explain how PTH can help correct the fall. Do not assume a medical cause for the initial change.

  1. Name the regulated pool. The starting change is a fall in blood calcium. The case does not say that every bone has lost calcium or that total body calcium has fallen by the same amount.
  2. Identify the source and signal. The parathyroid glands respond by releasing more PTH into blood. They are different from the nearby thyroid gland.
  3. Trace the bone contribution. PTH promotes calcium mobilization from bone mineral into blood. That is access to a reserve.
  4. Trace kidney conservation. Increased calcium reabsorption returns more calcium from tubule fluid toward blood. Less is left to leave in urine.
  5. Include the indirect branch. PTH promotes kidney activation of vitamin D. Active vitamin D increases intestinal absorption of available dietary calcium. Omitting active vitamin D would hide the reason this is an indirect PTH effect.
  6. Close the loop. The combined effects help blood calcium rise. The original low-calcium stimulus is reduced, so the drive for extra PTH release decreases. The response opposes the initiating fall.

Conclusion. The three routes differ in where their calcium comes from, but each helps defend the same circulating pool. Negative feedback is shown by the effect on the starting disturbance, not by declaring that the hormone is “good” or “bad.”

Change one given: the vitamin D branch is impaired

New givens. In a simplified model, PTH is released and the bone and renal calcium-conservation responses work, but the kidney cannot increase active vitamin D production. Which predicted contribution is weakened?

  1. Locate the changed step. The limitation is vitamin D activation, not PTH release.
  2. Follow its consequence. The expected extra active-vitaminD signal to the intestine is missing, so the normal increase in intestinal calcium absorption through this branch is weakened.
  3. Preserve the unaffected givens. The stated bone and renal calcium-reabsorption responses can still contribute. Do not erase those branches merely because one kidney function is impaired.
  4. Limit the conclusion. We cannot assume full restoration of blood calcium without knowing whether the remaining contributions are sufficient. The case teaches a pathway distinction, not a diagnosis or treatment choice.

Check your explanation. If you called the intestinal movement “reabsorption from urine,” return to the figure and identify the fluid on the upper side of each tissue wall. Naming the starting compartment determines the correct process.
