## Lesson 06 first answers and complete step mapping

1. Main question (line 3), opening question (line 22), and first worked case (line 108): The body reduces further water loss through increased ADH release. Water loss without equivalent solute loss increases plasma concentration (E06-3). Hypothalamic osmoreceptors detect this and promote extra posterior-pituitary release of ADH made in hypothalamic neurons (E06-2/3). Blood carries the hormone to responsive duct cells; suitable receptor signalling makes their walls more water-permeable (E06-4). With the stated normal osmotic gradient, more water moves from forming urine toward blood (E06-1/4; F06). Less water remains to leave as urine, so volume decreases and concentration increases (E06-1/5). Conserving that water opposes the plasma-concentration disturbance, reducing the drive for extra release as correction occurs (E06-6). This conserves existing water, not new water replacement (E06-9). No imported knowledge needed.
2. Contrast worked case (line 123): No, extra ADH alone does not guarantee conservation. The signal is already available; the stated defective link is the target collecting-duct response (E06-10), so normal permeability/reabsorption cannot be assumed (E06-4/5). Too much water can remain in forming urine, causing large dilute urine while the body loses water (E06-8/10). A hormone-deficiency case differs because functioning recipient cells could respond when the missing signal is supplied (E06-8). No imported knowledge needed.
3. Guided selection 1 (line 137): Increased water reabsorption (E06-4/5; F06).
4. Guided selection 2 (line 142): “Its volume falls and it becomes more concentrated.” Water has left the forming urine toward blood, leaving less outgoing fluid (E06-1/5; F06).
5. Stop question (line 149): No. More ADH normally increases duct water permeability and water reabsorption, leaving less, more concentrated urine (E06-4/5; F06). “Normally” includes responsive kidneys and the usual gradient (E06-4/5). More hormone does not mean more urine or zero urine (E06-7).
6. Required selection 1 (line 158): “Less urine with a higher concentration” (E06-4/5; F06).
7. Required selection 2 (line 165): “Impaired kidney response rather than inadequate ADH availability.” The former is nephrogenic, the latter central (E06-8).
8. Required writing 1 (line 174): Hypothalamic osmoreceptors detect overly concentrated body fluids (E06-3). ADH made in hypothalamic neurons is released in increased amounts by the posterior pituitary into blood (E06-2/3). In normally responding collecting ducts, ADH-receptor signalling increases water permeability; water follows the existing osmotic gradient from forming urine toward blood (E06-1/4). More reabsorption leaves smaller-volume, more concentrated urine (E06-5; F06). Water conservation counteracts the original rise in body-fluid concentration, so the extra release stimulus decreases as the disturbance is reduced. This is negative feedback and does not permanently shut off secretion (E06-6). No imported knowledge needed.
9. Required writing 2, higher-demand comparison (line 178): If too little ADH is available because its production or release is deficient but the kidney can respond, replacing the signal can allow receptor signalling, greater duct permeability and more water reabsorption (E06-2/4/8). If the kidney responds inadequately to ADH, the failed target link remains even with available or extra signal, so replacement alone does not fully solve the defect (E06-8/10). Large dilute urine alone cannot tell us which link failed (E06-8). No imported knowledge needed.
10. Local optional (line 184): “Dilute” refers to the fluid leaving as urine, not the water available to the body (E06-1/8). ADH deficiency or an inadequate kidney response can leave too much water in forming urine rather than reabsorbing it (E06-8). Losing this water makes outgoing urine large in volume and dilute, while insufficient remaining body water makes body fluids too concentrated (E06-8). No imported knowledge needed.
11. General self-question sequence (line 103): For the normal loss case, concentration rises, ADH is released, responsive kidneys reabsorb more water toward blood, and reduced loss opposes the disturbance (E06-1 through E06-6). For the supplied resistance case, the kidney-response answer changes to no/inadequate, so water loss can persist (E06-8/10).

## Lesson 07 visible evidence ledger

- E07-1, lines 23–31: thyroid location is given; it releases thyroxine/T4 into blood. Responsive cells need suitable receptors and internal machinery. “Metabolism means the chemical reactions occurring in cells.” Respiration transfers fuel energy into usable forms with some released as heat; “Greater thyroid-hormone action can increase energy use and heat production in many tissues.” “The hormone is a signal ... not a fuel.” Thyroid hormones also have growth/development roles. “hGH and thyroid hormones act through different signalling systems and have different roles.” “Much T4 is converted to the more active hormone T3.”
- E07-2, lines 33–50: hypothalamus releases TRH via the connecting portal blood system to the anterior pituitary, stimulating TSH release. “TSH enters the general bloodstream and stimulates the thyroid to produce and secrete thyroid hormones, including thyroxine.” “Hypothalamus makes TRH; anterior pituitary makes TSH; thyroid makes T4.” “TSH is a tropic hormone because its target is another endocrine gland.” The three hormones are different molecules.
- E07-3, lines 57–64: green arrows show forward stimulation; red T-bars show inhibition. Thyroid-hormone feedback “reduces upstream TRH and TSH release in a normally functioning pathway.” The return line is not a blood vessel or conversion of T4 into TSH. With increased thyroid hormones, stronger feedback tends to reduce TRH/TSH and further thyroid stimulation; with reduced hormones, inhibition weakens and permits TRH/TSH to rise “provided the hypothalamus and pituitary can respond.” Thyroid recovery also requires responsive thyroid. “The response opposes the starting change”; feedback does not permanently switch glands off.
- E07-4, lines 68–91: worked rise/fall cases supply intact function in all three regions. The fall case notes TSH can rise while thyroid hormone falls. If the pituitary cannot provide sufficient TSH or the thyroid cannot respond, the corresponding step cannot be assumed.
- E07-5, lines 108–118: “The thyroid needs iodine to make thyroxine.” T4 has four iodine atoms. “A strong stimulating signal cannot substitute for a missing raw material.” Iodine limitation can therefore maintain low output. “Reduced thyroid output weakens negative feedback. If the hypothalamus and pituitary still work, TSH can remain elevated. Continued TSH stimulation promotes thyroid growth, so the gland can enlarge into a goitre while still failing to produce enough hormone.” Neither all goitres nor all low-thyroid-hormone cases have this cause.
- E07-6, lines 125–131: the worked iodine-limited case expressly gives inadequate iodine, low T4 and intact pituitary control; high TSH is permitted by weaker feedback, extra TSH does not supply iodine, sustained stimulation can enlarge the gland.
- E07-7, lines 135–160: hypothyroidism is insufficient thyroid-hormone supply/action, with possible slow metabolic activity, tiredness/cold; hyperthyroidism involves excess production with possible greater energy use/heat intolerance. Symptoms do not identify the failed gland by themselves. The comparison table gives primary thyroid failure with responding hypothalamus/pituitary → low thyroid hormone, high TSH; inadequate pituitary TSH with a thyroid that could respond → low thyroid hormone, TSH “low or not elevated enough for the low thyroid hormone.” An ordinary-looking TSH may be inappropriate in the setting of low thyroid hormones.
- E07-8, lines 162–164: worked A/B comparison explicitly names each defect. Low output in A weakens inhibition and allows high TSH, but the thyroid remains defective; inadequate TSH in B leaves the capable thyroid under-stimulated. These are pathway explanations, not complete diagnoses from two numbers. For deficiency since birth, another growth-related signal does not automatically restore the missing thyroid actions.
- F07 companion pixels: hypothalamus, anterior pituitary, thyroid, and body tissues are arranged top-to-bottom; green arrows between them are labelled TRH, TSH, and Thyroxine (T4). A red line branches at the thyroid-hormone output level, runs up the left, and ends in T-bars at both hypothalamus and anterior pituitary. Red text says feedback reduces TRH and TSH release; the key distinguishes green stimulation and red T-bar inhibition. The tissue caption names metabolism, growth and development. The image states it is simplified/not to scale. It supports E07-1/2/3 and does not imply quantitative levels or anatomical spacing.

## Lesson 07 first answers and complete step mapping

1. Main/hook question (lines 3, 22): Low thyroid hormone plus high TSH is consistent with the stated primary-thyroid failure case when upstream control works; low thyroid hormone plus inadequate TSH suggests the stated pituitary-control failure instead (E07-7/8). Both hormones are needed because identical low final output can result from a defective thyroid or inadequate stimulation (E07-2/7/8). These are model assumptions, not a complete diagnosis (E07-8).
2. First worked case (line 68): With functioning hypothalamus/pituitary/thyroid, elevated thyroid-hormone output increases inhibitory feedback, so TRH and TSH tend to fall (E07-3/4; F07). Less TSH reduces thyroid stimulation and restrains further output, opposing the initial rise (E07-2/3). No imported knowledge needed.
3. Reverse worked case (line 81): Falling thyroid hormone weakens inhibition (E07-3/4). With intact control glands, TRH and TSH can rise; more TSH stimulates a responsive thyroid to raise its output, opposing the fall (E07-2/3/4). No imported knowledge needed.
4. Guided selection 1 (line 95): TSH, because TRH stimulates the anterior pituitary and pituitary TSH stimulates the thyroid (E07-2; F07).
5. Guided selection 2 (line 101): “It rises as thyroxine feedback weakens,” given the expressly intact hypothalamus and pituitary (E07-3/4).
6. Worked iodine case (lines 123–131) and stop question (line 167): Iodine is needed to synthesize T4, so inadequate iodine can limit output even with a strong stimulus (E07-5). Low thyroid output weakens feedback; intact upstream glands permit elevated TSH (E07-3/5/6). Continued TSH promotes growth and enlargement, while the missing raw material still limits production (E07-5/6). Large size therefore does not establish adequate hormone output (E07-5). No imported knowledge needed.
7. Worked A/B failure comparison (line 162): A has low thyroid hormone and high TSH, assuming hypothalamus/pituitary can respond: reduced inhibition increases stimulation but cannot fix the defective thyroid (E07-7/8). B has low thyroid hormone with low or inappropriately non-elevated TSH: the failed pituitary cannot supply the expected stimulation even though the thyroid could respond (E07-7/8). No imported knowledge needed.
8. Optional-video viewing prompt (line 171), answered without viewing: hypothalamus, anterior pituitary, thyroid; thyroid hormones, represented by T4, supply the upstream inhibitory feedback (E07-2/3; F07). Visible text fully supports this answer. Video contents/accuracy/access are not verified.
9. Required selection 1 (line 185): “Low thyroid hormone and high TSH” (E07-7/8). The question explicitly keeps pituitary control intact.
10. Required selection 2 (line 192): “Low thyroid output allows persistent TSH stimulation.” Iodine limitation reduces output; weaker feedback permits elevated TSH, which promotes growth (E07-5/6).
11. Required writing 1 (line 201): Hypothalamic TRH travels through the portal blood system to stimulate anterior-pituitary TSH release. TSH travels in general blood to stimulate thyroid hormone synthesis/release, including T4; circulating thyroid hormones act in responsive tissues (E07-1/2; F07). Rising thyroid hormones strengthen inhibition of hypothalamus/pituitary, lowering TRH and TSH and hence thyroid drive. Falling hormone weakens inhibition and allows greater stimulation if all relevant glands function. Because these responses oppose the initial change, the pathway is negative feedback; it reduces stimulation rather than permanently switching the glands off (E07-3/4; F07). No imported knowledge needed.
12. Required writing 2, higher-demand comparison (line 205): In primary thyroid failure, the thyroid cannot make enough hormone but I assume hypothalamic/pituitary responses are intact. Low output weakens feedback, so TSH is high, but extra stimulation does not restore the failed thyroid (E07-3/7/8). In the pituitary-failure case, I assume the thyroid can respond but pituitary TSH supply is inadequate. TSH is low or inappropriately not elevated despite low thyroid hormone, and the thyroid remains under-stimulated (E07-2/7/8). Low final output alone does not locate the defect, and neither pattern is a complete clinical diagnosis (E07-8). No imported knowledge needed.
13. Local optional (line 211): The case names a thyroid-hormone deficiency present since birth. Thyroid hormones have their own metabolic and developmental actions; hGH and thyroid hormones have distinct signalling systems and roles (E07-1/8). A growth-related signal cannot automatically replace the missing thyroid-hormone signal or restore all of its actions. The shared observation of poor growth does not identify the same cause (E07-1/8). No imported knowledge needed. “Congenital” is made accessible by the visible phrase “deficient since birth” (line 164).
