### 10-W1 — required comparison writing
**First answer:** In the rapid route, sympathetic neurons stimulate the inner adrenal medulla directly. The medulla then releases epinephrine and norepinephrine into blood. In the cortisol route, the hypothalamus releases CRH, which stimulates the anterior pituitary to release ACTH into blood. ACTH stimulates the adrenal cortex to release cortisol. The cortisol route is a hormonal relay through three sources; the medulla's incoming trigger is a nerve signal. Both routes can operate during the same challenge.
**Visible derivation:** (1) Neural trigger, medulla and final hormones: “Contrast the rapid route: sympathetic neurons → adrenal medulla → epinephrine and norepinephrine in blood.” (2) Inner region and incoming signal: “The medulla is the inner adrenal region, and its incoming trigger here is neural.” (3) Cortisol relay: paragraph beginning “The hypothalamus, a brain region…” and source-pair diagram. (4) Overlap: “The two routes need not take turns.” No claim about an exact delay is needed.
**Imported knowledge:** None necessary. **First-pass status:** Pass.

### 10-W2 — required glucose and feedback writing
**First answer:** Cortisol can promote protein breakdown, making amino acids available in blood. The liver can use some of those amino acids to synthesize new glucose, called gluconeogenesis, so glucose becomes available to circulation. This is different from breaking down stored glycogen. As cortisol rises in a normally functioning pathway, it inhibits CRH release from the hypothalamus and ACTH release from the anterior pituitary. Less ACTH means less stimulation of the adrenal cortex to release further cortisol. The feedback restrains output rather than requiring secretion to stop completely.
**Visible derivation:** (1) Protein → amino acids → liver glucose → circulation: the paragraph ending “The important sequence is protein breakdown → available amino acids → liver synthesis of glucose → glucose available to circulation.” (2) Glycogen comparison: next paragraph. (3) Feedback direction and consequence: first paragraph of “Trace the return message…” (4) Nonzero regulation: “This is regulation of the amount of hormone supplied, not a permanent on/off switch” and the following reading clarification. The first worked example also rehearses both mechanisms.
**Imported knowledge:** None necessary. **First-pass status:** Pass.

### 11-W1 — required pressure chain writing
**First answer:** Aldosterone is released by the adrenal cortex. It increases sodium reabsorption from forming urine toward the blood and increases potassium secretion into forming urine. Conserving sodium supports water retention when water is available, a suitable osmotic gradient exists and water can cross the boundary. Retained salt and water help maintain extracellular fluid and circulating blood volume, which can support blood pressure. This contribution does not replace the roles of the heart, blood vessels or ADH-controlled water permeability.
**Visible derivation:** (1) Source: “Aldosterone is a steroid hormone released by the adrenal cortex…” (2) Direction of sodium: the nephron paragraph defines reabsorption as tubule fluid → blood; next paragraph teaches both ions. (3) Water causality and conditions: “Retaining sodium helps retain water when water can follow by osmosis…” and “Water availability, gradients and permeability still matter…” (4) Volume/pressure: next paragraph defines blood volume and connects retained salt/water to it. (5) Other determinants: “Pressure also depends on the heart and blood vessels…” The first worked example models the same chain.
**Imported knowledge:** None necessary. **First-pass status:** Pass.

### 11-W2 — required ADH/aldosterone comparison writing
**First answer:** ADH is made in hypothalamic neurons and released from the posterior pituitary. It increases water permeability in responsive collecting-duct cells, allowing water to leave forming urine when the osmotic gradient permits. Aldosterone is made and released by the adrenal cortex. It increases sodium reabsorption toward blood and potassium secretion into forming urine. Its sodium conservation can support water retention, but water still needs an available path and gradient. ADH responds to fluid-concentration and volume/pressure signals; aldosterone is principally controlled by angiotensin II and blood potassium. “Both save water” gives a possible shared result but misses their different sources, controls and immediate actions.
**Visible derivation:** (1) ADH synthesis/release/action/gradient: first paragraph of “Compare the immediate action before the shared result.” (2) Aldosterone source and action: early explanation and the table's “Where is it made?”, “Where is it released into blood?” and “Immediate kidney action emphasized” rows. (3) Aldosterone water conditions: next paragraph and early osmosis paragraph. (4) Controls: table's “Main control emphasized” row plus RAAS/potassium teaching. (5) Why incomplete: “Do not use ‘both save water’ as a substitute for these mechanisms. It names a possible shared consequence but hides the sources, controls and first changes at the kidney.”
**Imported knowledge:** None necessary. **First-pass status:** Pass.

### 11-O — optional written practice

**Imported knowledge:** None necessary. **First-pass status:** Pass.
