Follow a hormonal relay through three sources
The adrenal medulla can respond directly to a sympathetic nerve signal, but a different route regulates
cortisol
from the
adrenal cortex
. Both routes can be active during one challenge. To keep them distinct, track the source of each message rather than deciding from the word “stress” alone.
The
hypothalamus
, a brain region involved in coordinating internal responses, releases corticotropin-releasing
hormone
, abbreviated
CRH
. CRH reaches the
anterior pituitary
through the connecting portal blood system. It stimulates pituitary cells to release adrenocorticotropic hormone, or
ACTH
, into the general bloodstream. ACTH then reaches the adrenal cortex, the outer part of each adrenal gland, and stimulates cortisol production and release.
This is the
hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal
pathway, often shortened to
HPA
. An axis is a functional control relationship, not one continuous tube. CRH, ACTH and cortisol are separate chemical messages from separate sources; a molecule does not change its name as it passes from one gland to the next.
Read the diagram from left to right. Pair each source with its output: hypothalamus/CRH, anterior pituitary/ACTH, adrenal cortex/cortisol. The final box represents responsive body tissues, where fuel use and immune activity can change. The red return line has a different meaning, which we will explain after the target effects.

Contrast the rapid route:
sympathetic neurons → adrenal medulla → epinephrine and norepinephrine in blood
. The medulla is the inner adrenal region, and its incoming trigger here is neural. The cortisol route uses a releasing hormone and a pituitary tropic hormone before the final cortical hormone. A tropic hormone acts on another endocrine gland; ACTH is the example in this pathway.
The two routes need not take turns. A stressful event can activate sympathetic/adrenal-medullary activity and HPA activity together, with different dominant controls and time courses. “Longer-lasting” describes a useful comparison, not a rule that cortisol waits until every epinephrine effect ends.
Distinguish making glucose from releasing a stored supply
Cortisol
belongs to the
glucocorticoid
group, adrenal-cortex steroid hormones involved in metabolism. Metabolism is the set of chemical reactions that makes and uses substances in cells. Cortisol can help increase the availability of glucose and other fuels by changing those reactions.
One route begins with proteins. Proteins are built from smaller units called
amino acids
. Cortisol can promote protein breakdown in tissues, making amino acids available in blood. The liver can use some amino acids as starting material for
new glucose production
. This process is called
gluconeogenesis
. The important sequence is protein breakdown → available amino acids → liver synthesis of glucose → glucose available to circulation.
Compare that with
glycogen breakdown
. Glycogen is a store of linked glucose units. Breaking it down releases a previously stored supply; making glucose from amino-acid starting materials is a different process. If a question states that the liver is using amino acids to make glucose, do not explain it merely as opening a glycogen store.
Cortisol also influences fat metabolism and how tissues use fuels. Stored fat is not a bag of glucose. Fat breakdown releases fatty acids and glycerol; fatty acids can serve as fuels, and glycerol can contribute to glucose production. Do not replace these different routes with the statement that all fat is converted directly into glucose.
Reading clarification:
textbook p.454 says fat-cell breakdown releases glucose. For the mechanism in this lesson, distinguish fat breakdown products from the liver's new glucose production. The protein–amino acid–liver example gives a complete, checkable route without that shortcut.
As a
steroid hormone
made from cholesterol, cortisol can act through receptors inside responsive cells. These interactions can change gene activity and the proteins a cell produces, influencing its metabolism. The hormone is a signal that changes cellular work; it does not itself become glucose or supply the energy contained in a fuel molecule.
Trace the return message as well as the forward pathway
As
cortisol
increases in a normally functioning pathway, its feedback tends to reduce
CRH
release from the
hypothalamus
and
ACTH
release from the
anterior pituitary
. With less ACTH stimulation, the cortex receives less drive for further cortisol secretion. This is
negative feedback
: the pathway's output restrains upstream stimulation.
Now follow the red return line in the figure. It starts at the cortisol-output part of the pathway and ends at both control sources. Its short T-shaped endings mean inhibition, not a second blood vessel or cortisol being changed back into ACTH. Forward stimulation and return inhibition are two different relationships.
If cortisol falls, there is less feedback inhibition. Functioning control glands can increase CRH and ACTH, which can stimulate the responsive cortex. The response depends on those conditions. If the cortex is damaged, increased ACTH may fail to restore cortisol output. If the pituitary cannot release enough ACTH, low cortisol cannot guarantee that the stimulating signal will rise normally.
This is regulation of the amount of
hormone
supplied, not a permanent on/off switch.
Reading clarification:
the “stops the release” wording on textbook p.454 is a simplified account of reduced stimulation. Negative feedback does not require all normal cortisol secretion to become zero.
To explain a result, say which signal first changed, which functioning source responds, how its output changes and what that does to the next stage. “Cortisol is high, so feedback happens” leaves out the causal steps.
Separate a useful response from continuing excess
Cortisol
also influences immune activity and has
anti-inflammatory
effects: it can reduce inflammation. Inflammation is part of the body's response to tissue injury or infection, involving changes in blood vessels and immune-cell activity. Restraining that response is a different effect from supplying glucose.
Source clarification:
a teacher slide describes cortisol as increasing inflammation. For the comparison here, use its anti-inflammatory action, consistent with textbook p.454 and the scientific account. Do not carry the slide's opposite direction into your explanation.
Physiological effects depend on the level, duration and situation. A regulated response can help make fuel available during a challenge. Persistently excessive cortisol can interfere with normal glucose regulation and immune function. These statements do not make cortisol universally helpful or harmful, and they do not let a symptom alone establish somebody's hormone level.
Cortisol and aldosterone both come from the
adrenal cortex
, but sharing a region does not give them the same job or principal control. Cortisol is a glucocorticoid emphasized here for metabolic and immune effects. Aldosterone is a mineralocorticoid involved in kidney ion handling and fluid balance. The next topic follows that separate route.
For now, keep two explanations ready:
how cortisol changes fuel availability
and
how cortisol feedback reduces its own upstream stimulation
. A complete response connects both without confusing the metabolic target effect with the feedback signal.
Worked examples
Connect a metabolic effect with its control loop
Question and givens.
A classroom case states that HPA activity increases during a challenge, all three control structures and target tissues respond normally, and cortisol increases. Explain one way glucose can become more available and predict what cortisol feedback tends to do to CRH and ACTH.
Identify the forward route.
Hypothalamic CRH stimulates anterior-pituitary ACTH, which stimulates cortisol release from the adrenal cortex. These are three source–signal pairs.
Choose a specific target mechanism.
Cortisol can promote protein breakdown, making amino acids available. The liver can use some amino acids to make new glucose. Naming the starting material and liver step explains more than saying “energy increases.”
Check the process name.
This is new glucose production, not simply release of glucose units already stored as glycogen. Cortisol has not turned into glucose.
Trace the return effect.
Increased cortisol tends to inhibit CRH and ACTH release in the functioning control pathway. Less ACTH restrains further cortical stimulation.
Keep effect and control separate.
The metabolic effect helps make fuel available; the feedback effect limits upstream drive. Both are actions within the same regulated system.
Conclusion.
A useful answer traces the liver's work and then closes the hormonal control loop. It does not need an unsupported direct fat-to-glucose shortcut or a claim that normal cortisol falls to zero.
Change one given: the cortex cannot restore its output
New givens.
Cortisol is low because the adrenal cortex is impaired, while the hypothalamus and pituitary respond normally. Predict ACTH and explain why it may not solve the shortage.
Follow low cortisol backward.
The control glands receive less inhibitory feedback.
Predict the functioning response.
ACTH can rise as upstream stimulation increases.
Carry the target limitation forward.
The impaired cortex may still make too little cortisol despite the extra ACTH signal. A stronger message does not guarantee a working target.
Do not overgeneralize.
This prediction depends on an intact pituitary. A pituitary that cannot supply ACTH is a different case.
Check your reasoning.
If you predicted low ACTH only because cortisol is low, retrace the inhibitory return line. The final hormone and its stimulating hormone do not have to change in the same direction.
