Start with the variable—not the name of the hormone

After a meal, glucose from digestion enters the bloodstream. Blood glucose concentration rises above its earlier level, but it does not normally keep increasing without limit. What brings it back toward a useful range? Before naming the feedback type, identify the regulated variable: in this case it is the concentration of glucose in the blood, not “the pancreas” or “insulin.” A gland and a hormone are part of the response, not the measurement being corrected.

When blood glucose rises, pancreatic beta cells detect that change and increase insulin release. Insulin travels in blood and binds receptors on responsive cells. Muscle and adipose tissues can increase glucose uptake; the liver also responds by promoting glucose storage as glycogen and limiting glucose output. These effects help the concentration in blood fall toward its usual range. As the initial rise is corrected, the stimulus for the extra insulin secretion becomes smaller. This is : the response reduces the disturbance that triggered it. It does not mean that insulin normally disappears completely between meals.

Read the figure from left to right. The left box names the initial rise in glucose. The middle box shows insulin increasing as a response. The right box shows responsive tissues taking up and storing more glucose, which changes the original variable. Now follow the red return line: it represents the reduced drive for extra insulin release as glucose comes down. It is not a second vessel or insulin travelling backwards through the body.

A complete feedback answer tells the sequence and the direction: initial rise → detection and hormonal response → effect that lowers that same variable → less stimulus for the response. If you simply say “insulin is present so it is negative feedback,” you have not identified the causal relation. The word negative tells us the effect opposes the starting change; it does not mean the response is unhealthy.

A correction can move a variable upward and still be negative

What if blood glucose goes the other way? Between meals or during continued activity, blood glucose can fall. Pancreatic alpha cells respond by releasing glucagon, another hormone carried in blood. Glucagon acts especially on the liver, encouraging stored glycogen to be broken down so that glucose can enter the bloodstream. The concentration rises toward its usual range, reducing the stimulus for extra glucagon release. The direction of the response is upward, but the direction relative to the starting disturbance is corrective: it opposes the fall. That is also negative feedback.

Notice that insulin and glucagon are not just two words to memorize. The trigger determines which branch is emphasized: when glucose has risen, insulin-supported processes bring it down; when glucose has fallen, glucagon-supported liver processes bring it up. The body does not need both hormones to stop being secreted; their relative activities vary with conditions. The maintained range matters more than whether the last arrow points up or down.

Think of a thermostat as a comparison. If room temperature drops below the setting, heating raises it; if temperature climbs too high, heating stops. The heater's upward effect on temperature is not “positive feedback” simply because the number rises. The important question is whether the response opposes the original deviation. In the body, the same reasoning applies to feedback through nerves, hormones or both.

To classify any feedback loop, use this method: (1) name the monitored condition; (2) state the direction in which it first changed; (3) identify the response and its effect on that same condition; (4) decide whether the effect weakens or reinforces the original change. The last step determines the feedback type.

When reinforcement is useful: childbirth

Not every physiological process tries to hold its initiating condition near a steady level. During labour, the stretching of the cervix can trigger a process that makes that stretch stronger. The response does not oppose the original change; it reinforces it. This is . “Positive” does not mean medically good, and “negative” does not mean medically bad. These terms name the direction of influence on the initial change.

Follow the chain carefully. As the baby's head presses on the cervix, stretch receptors send nerve impulses toward the brain. The hypothalamus coordinates release of oxytocin from the posterior pituitary into blood. Oxytocin reaches responsive uterine muscle, increasing the strength of contractions. Stronger contractions can push the baby farther against the cervix, increasing the stretch. That additional stretch promotes further oxytocin release. Each circuit adds to the stimulus for another circuit.

Draw the loop in words: more cervical stretch → more oxytocin release → stronger uterine contractions → more cervical stretch. Notice that the hormone travels in blood, but the first stretch is detected by nervous-system receptors. This is another example of the two communication systems working together. It is not the same as prolactin making milk, and oxytocin's milk-ejection role is a separate target effect from uterine contraction.

There must be an endpoint. After the baby is delivered, the mechanical condition that supported the loop changes: the pressure and stretch caused by the baby's passage diminish. The reinforcing cycle therefore does not continue indefinitely. A positive feedback example is incomplete if it names only “oxytocin makes contractions stronger” without both the return to more stretch and the event that ends the loop.

Contrast the loops: negative feedback pushes a regulated variable back against a disturbance; positive feedback builds on an initiating event until some limiting or terminating event ends the process. Neither label tells you whether an individual hormone is “positive” or “negative” all by itself.

Feedback can act across several glands

So far one gland has responded to a change in a variable. Some regulatory pathways have several levels. The hypothalamus can release a signal that acts on the anterior pituitary. The anterior pituitary can then release a , meaning a hormone whose target is another endocrine gland. That target gland releases a further hormone, which acts on body tissues. The target-gland hormone can also influence earlier stages of the pathway.

Draw the general sequence before memorizing any specific gland: hypothalamus → releasing hormone → anterior pituitary → stimulating (tropic) hormone → target endocrine gland → target-gland hormone → target tissue. In a negative-feedback version of this pathway, enough target-gland hormone reduces the signals from the hypothalamus and pituitary. This is . The later gland's output provides information to the earlier regulatory centres about the state of the pathway.

Here is why one blood test is not always enough to locate a problem. If a stimulating hormone is high, several possibilities remain: the target gland may not be making enough of its own hormone, or the upstream control could be unusually active, among other explanations. You need to know the level of the target-gland hormone and how the other steps are functioning before making a causal claim. A measurement is evidence about one step, not an automatic diagnosis of the whole pathway.

Try this transfer before opening the explanation. In a simplified two-gland model, the stimulating hormone is high but the target-gland hormone is low. What part of the pathway might the brain be trying to compensate for? Can those two observations alone prove exactly which gland has failed?

A poorly responding target gland is one plausible explanation, but the pair of measurements does not by itself prove the cause. A complete answer gives the direction of feedback and identifies what remains uncertain.

The common question across these examples is not “Which hormone is positive?” It is what changed first, what response followed, what happened to the original condition, and what information returned to the source? With that method in place, you are ready to study the hypothalamus and pituitary themselves.

Worked example

A falling temperature: classify the loop without relying on a hormone name

Question. A student waits outside on a cold day. Their core temperature begins to fall slightly. Temperature sensors signal a regulating centre, and the student begins shivering. Heat made by muscle activity raises body temperature toward its earlier level. Is this positive or negative feedback? Explain your decision using the variable, original change, response and effect.

  1. Name the variable being monitored. Core body temperature. “The student is cold” describes an experience, but it is not as precise as the condition being regulated.
  2. State the direction of the starting change. Temperature falls. Record that downward direction before looking at the response.
  3. Trace the response and its mechanism. Neural regulation increases involuntary muscle activity, or shivering. Muscle contractions use energy and release heat, which helps warm the body. In this example the corrective output is primarily nervous-system driven, but the same feedback logic also governs endocrine pathways.
  4. Compare the effect with the starting change. The response raises temperature, opposing the original fall. Therefore the feedback is negative, despite the upward direction of the response.
  5. Check whether the loop would continue unchanged. As temperature returns toward a suitable range, the drive for the extra heat-producing response decreases. That reduction fits a correcting loop, not an escalating one.

Biological conclusion. A response that increases a measurement can be negative feedback if the measurement initially decreased. The name comes from the relationship between initial disturbance and effect, not from the sign of the last arrow or whether a hormone is involved.
