Why does a circulating hormone change only certain cells?

Imagine two different tissues supplied by the same bloodstream. A passes both. One tissue changes its activity; the other does not. The difference is not necessarily that the blood failed to reach the second tissue. The puzzle is how a cell recognizes a particular chemical message and turns that recognition into a response.

Hormones move through circulation, but their effects depend on specificity. A cell is a target for a particular hormone when it has a suitable and the machinery needed to respond. A receptor is not simply a hole for the hormone to enter. It is a protein that recognizes and binds a signalling molecule. The familiar key-and-lock comparison is useful for the fit, but the essential next step is that binding changes what the receptor or the cell does.

Think of it as three linked events: the hormone arrives → a suitable receptor recognizes and binds it → activity inside the target cell changes. A hormone may bind on the outside of a cell and trigger events within it, or it may act through a receptor located inside the cell. Either way, receptor presence alone is not the whole story: the cell needs functioning internal pathways that can carry out the response.

That explains three different observations. A cell without a matching receptor may not recognize the hormone at all. A cell with a receptor but a damaged internal pathway may recognize the hormone yet respond poorly. A cell with functioning receptors and pathways may still respond weakly if its gland releases too little hormone. These are different potential failure points, and they must not be treated as identical.

For now, do not memorize a gland list. Instead, ask where the receptor is, how the hormone reaches it, and what happens immediately after binding. The structure of the cell membrane will help answer the first two questions.

How a water-soluble message crosses the membrane problem

Look first at the plasma membrane. Its centre is built mainly from the water-repelling tails of a lipid bilayer. This creates a barrier to many water-soluble substances. A therefore does not normally pass freely through the membrane to find a receptor inside. Instead, it can bind to a receptor exposed on the cell surface. That receptor passes the message inward, even while the hormone remains outside.

Read the left half of the figure. The signal approaches from above the membrane and meets a receptor that spans the membrane. The downward arrow below that receptor is an internal signalling event, not a claim that the hormone molecule itself crossed the membrane. Compare the right half afterward, when we discuss internal receptors.

Take the hormone epinephrine as an example. In a responsive liver cell, the sequence is: epinephrine binds a surface receptor → receptor activity changes → internal messenger molecules activate enzymes → stored glycogen is broken down → more glucose becomes available for release into blood. The receptor is the contact point, but enzymes inside the cell produce the metabolic effect. This is why naming only “epinephrine binds” does not explain the response.

Now follow the middle steps more slowly. One activated receptor can influence several molecules in a signalling pathway. Those molecules can activate additional proteins, and enzymes can process many substrate molecules. This is : a comparatively small initial signal can lead to a much larger cellular effect. We do not need a made-up fixed multiplication factor; the important reasoning is that one binding event can initiate multiple downstream events. The pathway's parts and activity determine how much amplification occurs.

Insulin and human growth hormone also act mainly through receptors at cell surfaces, though their internal pathways are not identical to epinephrine's. In muscle and adipose tissue, for example, insulin signalling helps increase glucose uptake, partly by bringing glucose transport proteins to the surface. Do not turn that into “every body cell needs insulin to absorb glucose.” Different tissues regulate their uptake differently. The general rule is hormone recognition + appropriate signalling machinery → a specific response.

Inside the cell: steroid hormones and an important exception

A interacts differently with a cell membrane. Because steroid hormones are lipid soluble, they can pass through the lipid bilayer and reach receptors within responsive cells. molecules are made from cholesterol. Examples you will meet later include cortisol, aldosterone and reproductive steroid hormones. Their receptors may be located in the cytoplasm or nucleus, depending on the hormone and receptor.

Look at the right half of the figure. The signal moves across the membrane to a receptor inside the cell. Once the hormone binds, the hormone–receptor system can influence activity in the nucleus, including which genes are transcribed. Altered gene activity can change which proteins a cell produces and, therefore, how it functions. Draw the causal chain this way: steroid enters → binds intracellular receptor → alters gene activity → changes protein production or other cellular functions. The diagram is conceptual; it does not mean every steroid uses an identical step or that all changes take the same amount of time.

One trap is to confuse what a hormone is made from with how it behaves around membranes. Thyroid hormones, including thyroxine (T4), are derived from the amino acid tyrosine and contain iodine. They are not steroids. Nevertheless, they are lipophilic and act mainly through intracellular, especially nuclear, receptors. Their uptake into cells uses specific transport proteins; do not assume they cross the plasma membrane just by the simple diffusion shown for the steroid model. Much T4 is converted to the more active T3 before it acts at nuclear receptors. This is enough of the exception to understand receptor location; thyroid regulation itself comes later.

What stops a signal, and what if the response fails?

A hormone should not keep producing the same response forever merely because it was released once. Hormones can be metabolized or removed from circulation, and the amount released can change as the monitored condition changes. Target cells also regulate receptor and signalling activity. Together, these processes help determine the signal's duration. Later you will see how feedback reduces further hormone release when the response has done its job.

When evaluating a cell that is not responding, separate the source problem from the receiver problem. If a gland secretes too little hormone, even healthy target cells may get too little signal. If the amount circulating is adequate but receptors are absent or defective, the message may arrive without a normal response. If receptors work but an internal enzyme or signal relay fails, binding may happen without the expected effect. A blood hormone measurement alone cannot always tell you which step is failing.

Here is a small reasoning check. Two groups of responsive cells receive the same hormone concentration. In group A, receptors bind the hormone and activate an internal messenger. In group B, receptors bind but the messenger pathway is disabled. Would more hormone guarantee the same response in B?

Compare your reasoning

No. The failure is after receptor binding. More arriving signal does not automatically repair a missing internal step. A complete answer names the shared concentration, states that binding occurred, locates the failed relay and explains why the target effect is reduced.

The opening puzzle is now answerable: broad delivery does not create a universal response. A target must recognize the hormone and perform the necessary intracellular steps. Once we understand both delivery and action, the next question becomes how glands know when to increase or decrease hormone release.

Worked example

Follow a signal through a liver cell—and locate a failure

Question. After a sudden fright, a liver cell is exposed to epinephrine in the blood. The cell has a functioning epinephrine receptor at its surface and stored glycogen. Normally it releases more glucose after stimulation. In an experimental version of this cell, the internal enzyme-relay pathway is blocked. Predict what happens and explain where the two versions differ.

  1. Identify the physical barrier. Epinephrine is water soluble. It does not need to enter the liver cell through the lipid bilayer to send a message; its relevant receptor is on the surface.
  2. Trace recognition. Epinephrine arrives in the bloodstream and binds a matching surface receptor on both the normal and experimental cells. Delivery and binding have occurred in both cases.
  3. Trace the normal cellular relay. Receptor activation starts a series of internal signals. Those activate enzymes that help convert stored glycogen into glucose. An enzyme can act on many substrate molecules, so the initial binding event can be amplified into a substantial metabolic response.
  4. Identify the blocked step. In the experimental cell, receptor binding still occurs, but the internal relay cannot activate the glycogen-processing steps normally. Glucose release is therefore expected to be reduced despite hormone being present.
  5. Check a tempting alternative. Calling this “low epinephrine secretion” would contradict the information given: the same hormone arrives and binds both cells. The evidence points to a response-pathway problem, not a delivery problem.

Interpretation. The hormone changes the cell by activating its signalling machinery. If that machinery fails, a functioning receptor is not sufficient by itself. The same diagnostic reasoning—source, receptor, internal response—will help us interpret other hormone systems later.
