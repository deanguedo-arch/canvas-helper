Hormones and their target cells

How can a small amount of hormone change a cell’s activity?

Learning goal

Explain receptor specificity and compare membrane-receptor signalling with intracellular hormone action.

Before you begin

A target cell is defined by its ability to recognize and respond to a particular hormone, not only by receiving blood.

Lesson vocabulary

An increase in the scale of a cellular response through a chain of signalling steps.

Why does a hormone affect some cells but not others?

A must bind to a suitable receptor before it can produce its usual effect. Think of a key fitting a particular lock: the fit helps explain why the message is recognized by some cells and not others.

Binding also has to start a change inside the cell. If the receptor is missing, the cell cannot respond normally to that hormone. Sending more hormone past the cell does not replace a missing receptor.

Water-soluble hormones bind at the cell surface

The cell membrane contains a lipid, or fatty, layer. Water-soluble hormones generally cannot pass through that layer directly. They bind to receptors on the cell surface, which pass the message to processes inside the cell.

Insulin, human growth and epinephrine are examples. The hormone does not have to enter the cell to change its activity. A series of reactions inside the cell can amplify the message, meaning that a small initial signal can produce a larger response.

Lipid-soluble hormones bind inside the cell

Steroid hormones are made from cholesterol. Examples include cortisol, aldosterone and the sex hormones. They can enter target cells and bind to receptors inside them. The –receptor complex can influence which genes are used and which proteins the cell makes.

Compare the receptor locations first: water-soluble hormones usually bind at the surface; steroid hormones bind inside the cell. These different mechanisms help explain differences in how responses develop.

Thyroxine is also lipid soluble and acts through receptors inside cells, but it is not a steroid. It is made from an amino-acid starting material. Chemical origin and solubility describe different properties of a hormone.

Hormone signals must also come to an end

Hormones are broken down and cleared from the blood. Changes in release and target-cell response also help control how long a signal lasts.

Keep two problems separate. A gland may release too little hormone, so its target receives too little signal. Alternatively, enough hormone may be present but the target may not respond properly. Later, you will use this distinction to explain different kinds of hormone imbalance.

Worked example

Predict where the receptor is

A hormone is described as water soluble and unable to pass directly through the membrane’s lipid layer.

  1. The hormone is unlikely to reach an internal receptor by passing directly through the membrane.
  2. It can bind to a receptor at the cell surface.
  3. The receptor passes a signal to processes inside the cell. The hormone itself need not enter.
