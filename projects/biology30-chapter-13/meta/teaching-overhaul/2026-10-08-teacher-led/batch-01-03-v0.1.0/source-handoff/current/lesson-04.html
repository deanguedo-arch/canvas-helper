The hypothalamus helps control the pituitary

The pituitary is a small gland below the at the base of the brain. Several pituitary hormones act on other endocrine glands, which is why the pituitary is often called the master gland. The hypothalamus helps regulate its activity.

The pituitary has two lobes: the at the front and the at the back. Their names sound similar, but they handle hormones differently. Learn these two routes before learning the individual hormones.

The anterior pituitary makes its own hormones

The sends releasing and inhibiting hormones through a small connecting blood-vessel network called a . These signals can increase or decrease production and release by anterior-pituitary cells.

The then releases its own hormones into the general bloodstream. These include hGH, , TSH, , and . A acts on another . For example, TSH stimulates the thyroid, and ACTH stimulates cortisol release from the adrenal cortex.

Not every pituitary hormone has the same target. Prolactin acts on mammary glands, while hGH affects growth and metabolism, including through growth factors released by the liver.

The posterior pituitary stores and releases hormones

ADH and are made by neurons in the . They travel along the neurons’ axons to nerve endings in the . They are stored there and released into the blood when appropriate signals arrive.

Keep the two verbs separate: made in the hypothalamus; released from the posterior pituitary. A question asking where ADH is produced has a different answer from one asking where stored ADH enters the blood.

Worked example

Answer the verb in the question

Two questions ask where ADH is made and where it is released into blood.

  1. “Made” asks where the molecule is produced: neurons in the hypothalamus.
  2. ADH travels along axons to the posterior pituitary, where it is stored.
  3. “Released” asks where the stored hormone enters the blood: the posterior pituitary.
