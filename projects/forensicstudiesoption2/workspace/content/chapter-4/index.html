Body Fluid Evidence

Body Fluid Evidence

This module will explore the significance of body fluid evidence found at crime scenes. The two main types of body fluids that may be found at a crime scene or upon the bodies of the individuals involved with a particular crime are blood and semen. The lessons in this module will:

  • outline some of the basic features of blood.
  • explore how latent blood residue can be enhanced and how blood spatter patterns can be interpreted.
  • focus upon the information that semen collected from a sexual assault yields.
  • examine a historical crime and a fictional crime that both involve body fluid evidence.

The body fluid that is most often found at crime scene involving injuries (ie. traffic accidents) or violence (ie. fighting, homicide) is blood. Blood evidence can help to identify an individual. Even the pattern of the blood residue (called blood spatter) can yield a great deal of information about a crime.

Semen is ejaculated by a male during sexual intercourse and this type of evidence is often collected after a sexual assault has occurred. Sometimes semen is collected from the victim’s clothing or from on or within the victim’s body. In addition, semen may be found on or near the object(s) where the sexual assault occurred (ie. bed sheets, ground/floor).

By the end of Module 4 you should be able to:

  • Outline the four basic blood types displayed by humans and identify the significance of a secretor in human blood types.
  • Explain the steps involved in the identification of an unknown blood sample through the process of blood sample testing.
  • Describe the relevance of the basic types of blood spatter evidence.
  • Outline how semen evidence is often obtained and how this evidence can help identify a culprit.
  • Analyze various microscopic samples of body fluids to differentiate between blood and semen.
  • Perform an experiment that identifies the four basic blood types and identify various unknown blood samples.

Blood Components

The Components of Blood

Red Blood Cells

Blood is the most common evidence found after a violent crime or a serious motor vehicle accident. It may be found at the crime scene, it may be found on the perpetrator, it may be found onthe victim, or it may be found in more than one of these locations.

An average human adult has about five litres of blood in the circulatory system. Blood is a complex fluid that has four components, which from largest to smallest quantity are as follows:

  1. Plasma - fluid portion of blood; comprised mainly of water, dissolved nutrients, minerals and gases; makes up 55% of blood
  2. Red Blood Cells - specialized cells designed to attach to oxygen and carbon dioxide; also known as erythrocytes
  3. White Blood Cells - specialized cells that focus upon the destruction of foreign antigens; have a nucleus and thus are the cells obtained for DNA analysis, a useful tool in forensic science
  4. Thrombocytes - specialized cells involved in the clotting of blood; prevent excessive bleeding
Blood Flow during Stress

During most violent crimes and motor vehicle accidents, the body of every individual involved is in a state of stress. The stressed body responds by increasing the heart rate. This leads to increased blood flow or blood pressure. Therefore, if any blood vessels are damaged when an individual is under stress, a significant amount of blood is released. At a suspected crime scene, forensic investigators check for both blood as well as materials that may have been used to clean up any blood.

Red Blood Cell Count and Elevation

A healthy male has about 5.4 million red blood cells per cubic millimetre of blood; a female has 4.8 million per cubic millimetre of blood. An average adult has an average of 25 trillion red blood cells! After about 120 days, a red blood cell becomes weak and nonfunctional. These old red blood cells are broken down by the spleen, liver, and bone marrow.

Under low oxygen conditions at high elevations, the human body produces a greater number of red blood cells. Therefore, it is possible for forensic scientists to estimate the elevation of the location at which an individual was simply by looking at their red blood cell count. For example, Calgary is at a higher elevation (1033 m) than Edmonton (689 m). As a result, an individual who has been in Calgary longer than 5 days has a higher red blood cell count than an individual from Edmonton. Thus, a forensic scientist with a microscope may use this difference to discern the approximate geographic location for an individual.

Determining the Organism that Blood Comes from

A light microscope may be used to determine if a blood sample from a crime scene is human and not animal. A red blood cell of birds, reptiles, and lower vertebrates (i.e., birds, frogs, toads) have a nucleus (see figure A). Mature red blood cells in humans and other mammals are anucleated -- they have no nucleus (see figure B). Having anucleated red blood cells is considered an advanced evolutionarily trait because these red blood cells can carry more oxygen than nucleated cells due to a higher concentration of hemoglobin.

Therefore, if a microscopic blood sample has a-nucleated red blood cells, the blood came from either a human or from another mammal (such as dogs, cats. etc.). After the possibility of the blood coming from a mammal has been ruled out, further tests can be done to try to individualize the sample (i.e., DNA print analysis).

Blood Typing

"During the past two decades, the individualization of dried blood has become one of the most exciting and promising research areas in forensic science."
-Saferstein, Richard. Criminalistics: An Introduction to Forensic Science

 


During the early 1900s, the discovery was made that the red blood cells in one human are not necessarily the same as the red blood cells in another human. Three types of protein-markers (also called agglutinogens) were discovered on the surface of red blood cells. The variable used to identify each of the three protein markers was A, B, and Rh+. Your blood type is determined by the protein-marker(s) found on your red blood cells; the protein markers you are born with are inherited from the genes from your parents. If you ever require a blood transfusion, the compatibility of the blood given is very important.

You can have either the A and B protein markers independently (type A or type B) or in combination (type AB) or, interestingly, you may have neither (type O). In addition to the A and B protein markers, the Rh+ protein marker must be taken into account. If the Rh+ protein marker is present, you are considered to be (+), and if you do not have the Rh+ protein marker you are considered to be (-).

For forensic scientists, determining the blood type of a sample recovered from a crime scene helps to narrow the field of suspects by either implicating suspects with the same blood type or eliminating any suspects who do not have the blood type identified.

Certain percentages of any given population have certain blood types. For example, in North America, 42% have blood type A, about 46% have blood type O, 9% have blood type B, and blood type AB is the least common at 3%. Blood type identification does not by itself prove that a blood sample belongs to a particular suspect because others may also have the same blood type. However, it helps to shorten the list of suspects. To further prove that a blood sample came from a particular individual, a DNA analysis of the blood sample is required.

Chemical substances called 'anti-serums' determine blood type. The three types of anti-serums are anti-serum A, anti-serum B, anti-serum Rh+. Each identifies one of the three protein-markers that exist on the surface of red blood cells (A, B, Rh+). To determine the blood type of a sample anti-serum is added and if an agglutination reaction occurs (precipitate forms causing cloudiness) a positive test for the that protein-marker has occurred.

Steps in Determing the Blood Type of an Unknown Sample

Example 1:

  1. Three separate small samples of the blood being tested are created.
  2. A drop of anti-serum A is added and mixed into sample #1.
  3. A drop of anti-serum B is added and mixed into sample #2.
  4. A drop of anti-serum Rh+ is added and mixed into sample #3.
  5. Each sample is mixed gently for 1 to 3 minutes.
  6. Cloudiness is observed in sample #1. The formation of a precipitate in sample #1 indicates a positive agglutination reaction has occurred.
  7. No reaction has been observed in sample #2 and #3.

Conclusion: The unknown blood sample is type A-.

Example 2:

  1. Three separate small samples of the blood being tested are created.
  2. A drop of anti-serum A is added and mixed into sample #1.
  3. A drop of anti-serum B is added and mixed into sample #2.
  4. A drop of anti-serum Rh+ is added and mixed into sample #3.
  5. Each sample is mixed gently for 1 to 3 minutes.
  6. Cloudiness is observed in all three samples. The formation of a precipitate in samples #1, #2 and #3 indicates a positive agglutination reaction has occurred in all three samples.

Conclusion: The unknown blood sample is type AB+.

Blood Enzymes

A number of unique enzymes and proteins in human blood can help to further identify the source of blood evidence. Just as each blood type is found in a certain percentage of the population, so too are these unique blood enzymes and proteins. Finding one or more of these enzymes or proteins using various chemical tests helps forensic experts to prove further the identity of an individual. One drawback to this technique is that these enzymes and proteins begin to decompose after they are released from the body. Therefore, the fresher the blood, the greater likelihood that these enzymes or proteins will be found.

Blood Evidence - Phenolphthalein

Identification of Blood Evidence

The first step that a forensic investigator must take in a crime scene where blood evidence is suspected is confirming that the evidence found is blood. Some liquids when spilled and left to dry can be mistaken for blood such as paint, stain, juices, food coloring, or hair dyes. Forensic scientists use various chemical tests to confirm that the residue left behind at a crime scene is blood. In this lesson two popular chemical blood identification tests will be examined - phenolphthalein and luminal.

The Phenolphthalein Test

Trying to clean up every trace of blood after a violent crime is very difficult especially if there is great deal of blood. Often criminals think that they have sufficiently cleaned up all blood left behind, but end up missing trace amounts of blood spatter. Forensic investigators are aware of this and as a result one of the first things they will often do at a secured crime scene is bring in powerful lighting equipment to illuminate the area.

The bright lighting helps investigators find any small suspicious stains possibly of blood that may be left at a crime scene. Dried blood will appear a deep burgundy or brownish-black in color. If an investigator does find a suspicious stain - no matter how small - a photograph is taken followed by a chemical test which is performed to confirm that it is blood and not something else.

The chemical formula for phenolphthalein is C20H14O4, it is an acid-base indicator. Phenolphthalein is normally colourless in a solution but when a hydrogen atom is removed from the phenolphthalein molecule, the negatively charged ion turns the solution colour pink. In this way, phenolphthalein is colourless in a neutral or acidic solution but pink in a basic solution.

Hemoglobin is a large protein in all red blood cells that carries oxygen and carbon dioxide throughout the blood stream. In addition to carrying gases, hemoglobin breaks down toxic hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) that may build up in the blood stream. The hydrogen peroxide is broken down into two hydroxide ions, 2(OH-). The excess hydroxide ions make the blood basic.

A simple chemical test that is used by many forensic scientists is the phenolphthalein test. During the phenolphthalein test each hydroxide ion will react with a phenolphthalein molecule producing water (H2O) and a negatively charged phenolphthalein ion. The resulting solution turns a bright pink colour. These chemical reactions are outlined below:

If an unknown stain at a crime scene is suspected to be blood a swab dipped in phenolphthalein is placed upon the stain and observations are then made. If the swab turns a bright pink color the stain is of blood, if the swab remains colorless then the stain is not blood. This chemical test is very popular for two reasons:

  1. it is fast - results are immediate.
  2. it is very sensitive - it can detect blood at 1 part to 5 million. In other words, if there is one drop of blood in a container of one million drops of water, phenolphthalein will react quickly producing a bright pink color.

One drawback to the phenolphthalein test is that it gives a positive test for any type of blood no matter the source. It does not distinguish between humans, other mammals, birds, reptiles or lower invertebrates. Further analysis (ie. microscopic or DNA) needs to be done upon the blood in question to identify its source.

Phenolphthalein: Confirming that it IS blood

The science of chemistry will be the focus of this section as Forensic scientists use various chemical tests to confirm the presence of blood.  There are a variety of chemical tests that can be used to identify blood, but we will only examine the two most popular methods in this lesson.

Trying to clean up every trace of blood after a violent crime can be next to impossible - especially if there was a great deal of blood.  Often criminals will think that they have sufficiently cleaned up a room, but end up missing minute amounts of blood spatter.  Forensic experts are aware of this and as a result one of the first things they will often do at a crime scene is to simply bring in a powerful light.  This powerful light then can be used by investigators to find any small traces of dried blood that may be left at a crime scene.  Dried blood will appear a deep burgundy or a brownish-black in color.  If an investigator does find a suspicious stain - no matter how small - a photograph is taken followed by a chemical test to confirm that it is indeed blood and not something else.

The Kastle-Meyer test is used to detect blood.  This test uses phenolphthalein, which turns deep pink when exposed to hemoglobin, which is found in all red blood cells.  If an unknown stain at a crime scene is suspected to be blood, a Forensic Investigator will take a Q-tip dipped in a phenolphthalein reagent, place it upon the stain in question and then look for a bright pink color to occur.  This will indicate that the substance is indeed blood.  This chemical test is very popular for two reasons: it is fast and sensitive - it can detect blood at 1 part to 5 million.  In other words, if there is one drop of blood in a container of 4, 999, 999 drops of water, phenolphthalein will react quickly to produce a bright pink color.

Blood Evidence - The Luminol Test

The Luminol Test

At a crime scene, large pools of blood are easy to spot, but what if all the blood has been cleaned up by the assailant?  It too can be detected using a powerful chemiluminescent compound called LUMINOL (3-aminophthalhydazide). Luminol reacts with a portion of the hemoglobin of red blood cells called 'heme'. When luminol comes into contact with ‘heme’ it reacts producing a unique product - a greenish-blue light. Luminol is very sensitive; it can detect blood at 1 part to 300 000.

Investigators use luminol at crime scenes where there is no visible blood whatsoever. They spray it over a wide area in near-total darkness so that any reaction is obvious. Officers then photograph/videotape the result, using a flash technique that picks up the glow while making the area around it visible on film.

There are several reasons to use luminol.  Luminol is non-destructive, meaning that after spraying it at a crime scene other chemical tests such as the phenolphthalein test can still be performed to confirm that it is blood. Another reason is that luminol works well even after a long period of time and after the blood has aged and decomposed. As the red blood cell ages and decomposes its exterior cell membrane breaks down and exposes the hemoglobin. Thus, the exposed hemoglobin can more easily come into contact with luminol which increases the likelihood of a reaction. In fact, if it is suspected that blood has recently been cleaned up forensic investigators will spray the area with hydrochloric acid (HCl) solution before applying luminol.

An advantage to luminol is that it can point the way to further evidence or to how events may may have unfolded. For example, if luminol sprayed onto a carpet produces a glow, officers might peel back the rug to reveal floorboards still soaked with blood. Or perhaps the glow will disclose the direction in which a criminal walked or dragged a body, or whether the victim was shot from behind or above. 

Some disadvantages to luminol include the fact that it cannot distinguish between human blood and animal blood because both contain hemoglobin. Also, luminol can react in the presence of similar substances such as metals like copper, nickel, paints containing metals, rust, cleaning products like highly concentrated bleach, iodine, and even weird things like horseradish, watermelon and some citrus fruits. However, luminol's reaction to each of these differs in intensity (ie. metals cause an immediate glow which quickly fades, while blood glows longer). A forensic expert can tell the difference and then perform more conclusive tests to determine whether the blood is in fact of human origin.

So, anyone thinking that cleaning up after a bloody crime scene will erase evidence  of their crime is mistaken...blood is one stain that cannot be "shouted out"!

Blood Splatter Evidence

Blood Splatter Evidence

Blood is one of the most visible and useful (yet disturbing) types of physical evidence that can be left at a crime scene. Varying amounts of blood are typically left behind by the victim and/or the suspect at the scene of a violent crime.


The pattern that blood leaves behind on a surface can be interpreted, helping investigators to learn more about the crime itself. Blood that is left behind at a crime scene and later interpreted by forensics experts is called blood spatter  or blood stain evidence.

The two best ways of interpreting blood spatter/stain evidence is through direct analysis of the blood at the scene of the crime and/or through careful study of crime scene photographs. It takes experience and extensive training to become an expert in blood spatter/stain analysis, however this type of knowledge can prove to be very useful in criminal investigations.

Information that can be determined from blood spatter evidence includes:

  • origin(s) of the blood.
  • type and direction of impact that produced the blood stain/spatter.
  • position of victim(s) and/or suspect(s) involved.
  • movement and direction of victim and/or assailant after the initial assault. 


Surface Texture

Blood will 'free-fall' when gravitational forces cause blood to separate and fall from its source. The gravitational force must exceed the surface tension of the blood in order for it to 'free-fall'.  (Surface tension is caused by the molecular cohesive forces within a compound).  Examples of situations where blood free-falls includes open wounds, blood-soaked clothing, or dripping from weapon surface. 

When a free-falling droplet of blood strikes a horizontal surface it will create a CIRCULAR blood stain. However, how circular it appears depends upon the surface - the smoother the horizontal surface the more circular the bloodstain surface will be (eg. glass vs. cement).  In general, the harder and less porous the surface, the less the blood will spatter.

Control experiments on identical (or similar) surfaces must be conducted to determine distance of spatter, direction etc.

In general, the greater the distance that blood free-falls, the greater the size of the circular blood stain pattern and the more cast-off residue (spatter) evident.


Direction of Travel

The blood's direction of travel after impact can be determined based on the stain's shape.  The pointed end of the bloodstain will always face its direction of travel.  In the top bloodstain pattern on the right, we can determined that the droplets of blood were moving from left to right. 

Angle of Impact

When blood impacts a surface, the degree of circular distortion (i.e. how un-circular the blood is) is an indicator of the trajectory of the blood before it hit the object.  

As the angle of the surface decreases, the less circular and greater in length the blood stain will be.  The image below shows examples of this effect.

Origin of the Blood

When a force acts upon an exposed source of blood, it is broken up into many small droplets due to the energy applied to it. The greater the impact/force, the smaller the size of the blood droplets and the greater the number of blood droplets left behind. Therefore, getting hit several times by a human fist (a medium-velocity impact) will leave behind fewer but larger blood stains as opposed to being struck by a bullet (a high-velocity impact).

The site of origin of the blood can often be determined by drawing straight lines through the long axis of several individual bloodstains.  The intersection (or point of convergence) of the lines represents the point from which the blood originated.  The image on the right demonstrates this.

Other Body Fluid Evidence

Sexual Assault Crimes

One of the most disturbing crimes that can occur involves a person being forced to engage in sexual activity against their will. The majority of all sex-related crimes (ie. rape/sexual assault, molestation, pedophilia and other indecent acts) are committed by men. Thus, forensic investigators look for semen left behind by a suspect during the investigation of sex-related crimes. Semen can prove to be especially useful if the male suspect involved is a secretor.

What is a Secretor?

There are body fluids and tissues other than blood which may be left at a crime scene that can prove useful to forensic investigators.  These include: muscle tissue, skin, saliva, semen, sweat, tears and vaginal secretions.

Approximately 80% of the North Americans are known as secretors. In a person who is a secretor the antigens, antibodies, proteins, and enzymes in their blood are also present in their other body fluids and body tissues. Recall that red blood cells have protein-markers on their surface which determine a person's blood type.  Therefore, the same anti-serum test that is used for the blood to identify type A, B, AB, or O blood types can be used on a secretor’s body tissues yielding the same results. An anti-serum test is useful because secretors have the same protein-marker within the cells in their body fluids/tissues as they do in their red blood cells.

If it is determined that a suspect is a secretor then their body fluids can be very useful to help confirm their identity. If the suspect is not a secretor then investigators must find other evidence to help prove guilt.

Disturbing Statistics about Sexual Assault

Sexual assault is one of the most underreported crimes (Benoit et al. 2015; Brennan and Taylor-Butts 2008; Kaufman 2008; Luce et al. 2010). Research has attributed this to a wide range of reasons, including the shame, guilt and stigma of sexual victimization (Johnson 2012; Sable et al. 2006), the normalization of inappropriate or unwanted sexual behaviour, and the perception that sexual violence does not warrant reporting (Benoit et al. 2015).  Of all sexual assault incidents perpetrated by someone other than a spouse, only one in twenty was reported to the police, compared to one in three incidents of other types of crime measured by the General Social Survey on Victimization.

Overall, sexual assault offenders were most often men, acting alone and under the age of 35. Just over half of victims knew the person who sexually assaulted them. Most often, offenders were a friend, acquaintance or neighbour, then a stranger.

According to the General Social Survey on Canadians’ Safety (Victimization), there were 22 incidents of sexual assault for every 1,000 Canadians aged 15 and older in 2014. This represented approximately 636,000 self-reported incidents of sexual assault.

Semen

Collection of Semen from a Crime Scene

When investigators arrive at the scene of a sex-related crime, the victim is taken to a hospital for an extensive physical examination by a physician and nurse with specialized training. During this examination, physical injuries upon the victim’s body are identified and a search for semen from the culprit is conducted. If the victim is a female a swab of the vulva (external genitalia), vagina (internal genitalia), pubic hair region, and rectum for traces of semen are done.

When a male suspect commits a sexual assault it should not be assumed that the victim is always a female. Sexual assaults are sometimes committed against males as well. If the victim is male then a swab of his rectum for traces of semen is done. However, male sexual assault victims rarely report this type of crime.

There are other places that investigators will also check for semen evidence other than inside the victim’s body - such as the victim’s clothing and at the crime scene itself. If the suspect attempted to clean up after the assault any towels or clothing in the vicinity of the crime may be taken as evidence.

Semen Identification Tests

After body fluid evidence that is suspected to be semen is collected it must be confirmed that the fluid is semen. Two tests that can be done to confirm that a substance is semen include:

Microscopic Analysis: Visual analysis of the suspected fluid under the microscope. Sperm cells are very unique in appearance as they are the only human body cells that have a long flagella or tail. Therefore, an investigator may simply confirm that the suspected substance is semen by looking for such cells. Sperm cells are very small though, so a magnification of 100x or higher is needed.

‘Fast Blue B’ test: A special protein called acid phosphatase is found only in secretions from the prostate gland. When a chemical called ‘Fast Blue B’ is combined with acid phosphatase it will change from a blue to a deep purple color. If it does not turn a deep purple color then the substance being tested is not semen. A drawback to this test is that it also turns a deep purple color when it is exposed to fungi and some fruit and vegetable juices. However, a trained forensic expert knows that unlike other substances semen reacts in 30 seconds or less.

In addition, a test using MUP (4-methyl umbelliferyl phosphate) can be used as MUP will fluoresce (glow) under UV light when it comes into contact with acid phosphatase.  This test is similar to using Luminol to detect blood.  This test can be used to search large areas such as bedsheets and carpets for seminal stains.

Sexual Assault (Rape) Kits

What is a Rape Kit?

Soon after a victim has reported a sexual assault, police take the victim to a local hospital for medical examination. A specially trained physician and/or nurse follow certain specialized procedures using commonly referred to as a ‘rape kit’. During a ‘rape kit’ examination, the nurse will collect as much biological material (that is, semen, blood, skin, head hair, pubic hair) as possible from the victim’s body.

The areas of the victim’s body that are focused upon during a rape kit examination include clothing, skin surface, fingernails, pubic hair, vulva (external genitalia), vagina (internal genitalia), and the rectum. This examination is obviously not a pleasant experience for the already traumatized victim; however, the evidence that can be obtained from a rape kit can help lead to the apprehension of the perpetrator responsible for this violent crime.

The biological materials obtained from a rape kit that are of particular interest to the forensic investigator are foreign materials left behind by the perpetrator of the sexual assault. After any foreign biological materials have been collected, the evidence is transferred to a DNA analysis laboratory for a DNA profile (also called a DNA fingerprint). In Canada, DNA analysis for all police departments is conducted by five RCMP laboratories across the country (Vancouver, Regina, Winnipeg, Ottawa, Halifax). After a DNA profile match is made, there is definitive proof that a particular individual was involved in the sexual assault.

Backlog in the Analysis of Rape Kits

Like many scientific procedures, completing a DNA analysis of a rape kit is expensive ($1000.00 per kit).  This cost factor, in addition to the vast number of rape kits that exist has resulted in a backlog in the testing of rape kits across North America.

The completion of a DNA analysis of a rape kit is time-consuming and expensive because it must be done by a highly trained forensic expert. Becoming a DNA analysis expert takes a minimum of six years of education; consequently, few of these skilled scientists are available. Only DNA from body fluid evidence from the most serious violent crimes (homicide, man slaughter, sexual assault) is analyzed because of the relative small numbers of DNA analysts. Due to the increased volume of specimens sent in for analysis, a backlog in the completion of DNA analysis of rape kits in Canada (and across the world) exists.

The DNA analysis backlog came to attention of the Canadian public after a judicial inquiry into the Paul Bernardo murder case in Ontario in 1996. The inquiry found that the DNA analysis of several rape kits took over two years to complete, during which time Paul Bernardo committed four more sexual assaults and two murders. Shortly after the DNA analysis was finally completed, Bernardo was arrested and charged with several counts of sexual assault and two counts of first-degree murder. This case clearly illustrates the detrimental impact that the slow response time for DNA analysis can have. The judicial recommendation that came from this inquiry was that all DNA analysis tests for the purpose of criminal investigations should be completed within 30 days.

This DNA analysis backlog issue was considered so serious that the Office of the Auditor General of Canada conducted an investigation of all six of the RCMP crime laboratories in 1999. Some information revealed after this investigation included:

  • The average turnaround time for DNA analysis by RCMP laboratories was 101 days.
  • The RCMP completed only 15 percent of its DNA cases within 30 days.
  • As of October 1999, the DNA analysis backlog was 633 cases.
  • Wide variations in workload contribute to the DNA analysis backlog. One laboratory had an average backlog of 28 cases per scientist, while another had 13 cases per scientist.

The comments about the DNA analysis backlog put forward in the Auditor General’s report were quite critical:

“DNA technology is a very useful tool for police investigations. However, delays in obtaining results slow police investigations and hamper the ability to eliminate suspects and make arrests quickly. Most important, delays in reporting DNA results endanger public safety because criminals have more opportunity to reoffend.”

In 2001, the RCMP used a private firm to analyze DNA crime scene samples to lower the DNA analysis backlog and speed up the completion of tests being submitted. However, the backlog continues. At the end of 2004, five years after the Auditor General’s report was released, a DNA analysis backlog still existed at the RCMP laboratories across Canada--a backlog of 648 DNA analysis tests. Experts and politicians suggest that lack of adequate funding by the federal and provincial governments and the low numbers of forensic DNA analysis experts are the main reasons for the backlog.

Cases Involving Body Fluid Evidence

Cases Involving Body Fluid Evidence

Body fluid evidence, most often found in the form of blood, is the most disturbing type of evidence found at a crime scene as it indicates that a violent crime has occurred. A great deal of information about the activities that occurred during the crime or about the perpetrator can be determined from body fluid evidence. Body fluid evidence can be considered to be one of the most valuable types of evidence that exists.

The following sections will focus on cases that involve blood evidence.

Historical Crime Case #1

Crime Case Study #1: Unconvincing Circumstantial Evidence

On the evening of May 21, 1939 the dead body of a widowed 64-year-old man named Walter Dinivan was found in his living room. The murderer had attempted to strangle Walter, but when this failed they killed him by crushing his skull with a hammer.

Investigation of the crime scene revealed that a large sum of money had been stolen from a small safe owned by the victim. In the victim's kitchen, an unknown fingerprint was found on a drinking glass. In the victim's living room, a brown paper bag was found on the floor crumpled up and soaked with the victim's blood. As well, numerous cigarette butts found in the living room were collected and analyzed as Walter Dinivan did not smoke. Tests upon the cigarette butts revealed that the smoker was type AB+.

When family and acquaintances of Walter Dinivan's were interviewed an individual of interest to investigators was Joseph Williams. Williams had told police that he was angry with Dinivan as he had refused to give him a small loan of money. Interestingly, a background check of Williams revealed that days after Walter Dinivan was murdered he paid off part of a debt he owed the bank. When Williams was asked by police for his fingerprints he refused and since there was no solid evidence against Joseph Williams he could not be forced to cooperate.

Having no other strong suspect, police decided to keep Joseph Williams under surveillance. One night when Williams went into a pub the police investigator who had asked him for his fingerprints went into the pub to speak to him. The police officer gave Williams the impression that he was there for a social call as he bought him a drink and some cigarettes. At the end of the evening, the investigating officer gathered the ashtray containing all of Williams's cigarette butts. Analysis of these butts indicated that Williams was type AB+.

John Williams was brought in for questioning in the hopes that the evidence that they had collected thus far would lead Williams to confess. Williams did not confess, however he did allow police to search his home. The only thing that investigators found of significance was a large stash of brown paper bags that were identical to the bag found in the victim's home. When Williams was confronted with this he angrily denied the allegation that he murdered Walter Dinivan again and he offered to give police his fingerprints. Williams right thumbprint matched the unknown print found in Dinivan's home.

John Williams was finally arrested and put on trial for the murder of Walter Dinivan. Williams pleaded non guilty and he had a strong defense lawyer who argued and convinced the jury all the evidence found was purely circumstantial. The jury found John Williams not guilty of the murder charge.

Sadly, shortly after the not guilty verdict was announced a drunken John Williams confessed to a local reporter he had been celebrating with that he had murdered Walter Dinivan. The reporter did not reveal this confession until after John Williams had died in 1951.

Historical Crime Case #2

Crime Case Study #2: Which Neighbour is a Killer?

On April 30th, 1984, police rushed to a local farm after getting a frenzied 911 call from Graham Backhouse. When they arrived he could barely stand, as he had several deep cuts on his face and left shoulder. Lying dead on the ground in front of Backhouse's back door was his neighbor, Colyn Bedale-Taylor. Colyn had died from two gunshots wounds to his chest and he had a large knife in his hand.

Graham said that Colyn had tried to murder him with the knife and that after he had fought him off he frantically ran down the hall to get his gun while bleeding from the initial attack. Colyn pursued Graham down the hall with his knife even after Graham told him he was getting his gun. Because Colyn kept coming after him with the knife, Graham told police he had no choice but to shoot him twice in self-defense. After he had shot and killed Colyn Graham he called police. Officers found blood spatter evidence on the floor of Graham's hallway.

Fictional Crime Case #1

Crime Case Study #3: The Death of Tammy's Parrot

 

Police get a hysterical phone call from an individual named Tammy Ingland who says her ex-husband tried to kill her during an argument. Tammy is well known to the police as the type of person who engages in 'attention seeking behavior' as she has a history of making prank calls to the police. However despite Tammy's long history, police officers must take this call seriously because she could be in danger.

When officers arrive at Tammy's apartment they discover Tammy lying on the floor. A large volume of blood is found smeared all over Tammy's hands, upper body and on many of the walls in her apartment. After looking through Tammy's apartment a police officer discovers a parrot stabbed to death under a sofa. When asked about this parrot, Tammy began sobbing saying that her ex-husband killed her parrot after he assaulted her.

Officers become suspicious of Tammy's story after close examination of her injuries by paramedics indicate that she had only a few small superficial cuts on her face and lower arms. Also, neighbours interviewed reveal that they had not seen Tammy's ex-husband around the apartment in over a month and that they did not hear any loud arguing from the apartment on the day in question.

Fictional Crime Case #2

Crime Case Study #4: How did Phil Beardman die?

Late one night a 911 emergency operator received a frantic phone call from a female named Susan stating that "...her boyfriend had fallen down the stairs and hurt himself". When paramedics arrived they found a 45-year-old advertising executive named Phil Beardman lying unconscious at the bottom of the stairs in his condo. Shortly, thereafter he is pronounced dead and the police are called to the scene. Police find a large pool of blood at the bottom of the stairs and blood spatter patterns on a wall near the top of the stairs.

Phil's sister reported talking to him on the phone around 4:00 pm that day. He told her that he was going to end his relationship with Susan that evening. Also, neighbours reported hearing loud arguing between a man and a woman that night.

When Susan was questioned, she explained that she and Phil had spent an uneventful night watching TV and made no mention of an argument. Susan said that Phil was going downstairs to get something to eat, she then heard Phil shout "Whoa!" and a few seconds later she heard a series of loud thuds as he fell down the stairs. She thought that he accidentally slipped and hit his head on the stair railing as he fell down the stairs. When police check the stair railing for blood they found nothing, only blood at the top of the stairs and beside his body at the bottom of the stairs (see photo below).

What is Blood?

As you are probably aware, many crime scenes involve blood.  Blood can be used to help solve crimes.  But what exactly is blood made of?

Blood is a complex fluid that consists of two main parts:

  1. Cellular Elements - this includes red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets
  2. Plasma (or serum) - this is the liquid portion of blood that contains dissolved substances.

We will examine each of these elements in detail to gain a better understanding of blood.

Red Blood Cells

Red blood cells are small, biconcave discs with no nucleus.  Red blood cells are also called Erythrocytes.  This comes from the Ancient Greek words erythro- (meaning red) and -cyt (meaning cell).  

In blood, there are about 5 million red blood cells per milliliter.  These red blood cells carry oxygen and carbon dioxide using the red pigment hemoglobin.

Each red blood cell lives for about 120 days before being removed by the liver or engulfed by white blood cells.

White Blood Cells

White blood cells are much larger than red blood cells.  They also have a nuclei (which may be fragmented as shown in the photo on the left).  White blood cells are also called Leukocytes.  This word comes from Ancient Greek Leuk- (meaning white) and -cyt (meaning cell).  

White blood cells function as part of the immune system.  They are used to destroy foreign cells of the body.  In this way, they help protect the body from infections.

There are many different types of white blood cells (e.g. Monocytes, macrophages, lymphocytes, T cells, B cells etc.) These cells all serve a unique function in our body's immune system.



Platelets

Platelets are tiny cellular fragments with no nuclei.  They are smaller than red blood cells.  Platelets are also called Thrombocytes.  This word comes from Ancient Greek Thrombos- (meaning lump or piece) and -cyt (meaning cell).

Platelets are formed in bone marrow, along with white blood cells and red blood cells.  Their primary purpose is to assist with blood clotting.  When a wound occurs to the body, platelets release a protein that causes blood to clot.  This helps reduce blood loss from the body.

Bone Marrow

 Bone marrow is located in the large bones of the body (especially the leg bones and the pelvis).  This type of tissue produces all three types of blood cells.

Red blood cells and platelets can't reproduce by themselves, so without bone marrow we would soon die.

Plasma

The composition of whole blood varies slightly, but in general blood consists of mostly plasma.  By placing blood in a centrifuge (a machine that separates blood parts) we can see the components that blood is made out of as well as their proportions.

Plasma contains many important proteins (e.g. antibodies, hormones, carrier molecules, etc.) that are essential to proper body functioning.

Plasma is made up of about 90% water, 7-9% protein and contains glucose and other nutrients, waste products, dissolved gasses etc.

Blood Types

Blood is a very important part of helping to solve crimes.  Investigators often want to know whose blood they have collected from a crime scene.  There are many ways that you can identify blood at a crime scene, but most involve using the immune system.

Blood typing was first developed by Karl Landsteiner in 1901.  He developed this because he was looking for a safer way to transfuse blood to patients.  Prior to this, blood transfusion was a very dangerous and difficult task and was often shunned by the medical community.

Landsteiner noticed that when blood from two incompatible individuals was mixed, an immune reaction was triggered, resulting in the red blood cells clumping together.  By knowing this, he was able to classify blood into certain types.  Individuals all have one type of blood and the donor and receiver of blood need to have matching blood types.  Because of his discovery, blood transfusions become much safer.

This also helps forensic investigators to identify blood matches.  Blood types can be used to help identify perpetrators of crimes.  Presently, DNA fingerprinting is the gold standard for identifying blood (this will be discussed in greater detail later in the course).

Identifying Blood Types

The ABO blood classification system classifies blood into different types: type A, B, AB and O.  The ABO system relies on using the proteins on the outside of red blood cells.  These proteins are called antigens.

There are only 2 types of antigens in the blood system: A and B.  Cells either have antigen A only, (Type A), antigen B only (Type B), antigen A and B (Type AB) or no antigens (Type O) for this system.

If a person does not normally produce an antigen, then their body will treat that antigen as an invader if it's introduced into their bloodstream.  The body will produce antibodies which attack this foreign antigen.  

These antibodies will clump (agglutinate) with your red blood cells and make it impossible for normal circulation to occur.  This is why you must receive a blood transfusion from someone with the same blood type as you.

In a lab, we can use these antibody reactions to determine what type of blood a person has.  It's possible to see the agglutination of blood cells under a microscope.

Blood Typing in a Lab

How are blood types identified once they're in the lab?

Scientists create serums containing Anti-A or Anti-B antibodies.  These serums are combined with the blood samples from the crime scene.

Blood type A will agglutinate when brought into contact with anti-A serum and blood type B will agglutinate when brought into contact with anti-B serum.  Blood type AB will agglutinate when in contact with both serums and blood type O will not agglutinate with either serum.

Using this simple test, we can be determine the blood type of the sample.

So unfortunately having a blood type is not enough to pin down a suspect.  Before DNA testing, there were 5 or 6 different blood typing systems that were used to further identify the blood.
