Forensic Genetics

Forensic Genetics

An image of DNA 

The use of the DNA print analysis in forensic science was introduced in the mid-1980s. In just twenty years, it has proven to be one of the most powerful forensic techniques in solving various types of serious crimes, in identifying unrecognizable human remains, and in paternity testing. This unit is about how the technique of DNA print analysis works, how it is being used to solve crime, and why it is considered to be one of the most valuable investigative tools available. 

The following lessons will allow you to:

  • examine DNA and two scientific techniques used to analyze it.
  • discuss the difference between nuclear DNA and mitochondrial DNA and how each of these molecules is used in forensic investigations.
  • focus upon how probability ratios are used to validate DNA matches and about the Canadian national DNA data bank.
  • examine how an actual crime case was solved using forensic genetics; it allows students to solve a fictional crime case using DNA profile data.

By the end of Module 7, you should be able to

  • outline the basic molecular structure and function of the DNA molecule and state the significance of VNTR patterns
  • compare the features of nuclear DNA and mitochondrial DNA
  • identify the steps involved in the creation of a DNA print and differentiate between RFLP and PCR DNA analysis techniques
  • explain how DNA evidence is supported by population frequency values
  • discuss the importance of the establishment of a comprehensive DNA data bank and propose the implications of having a national DNA data bank
  • perform an experiment that involves the identification and comparison of various sample nuclear DNA fingerprints
  • complete a case study that involves the evaluation and interpretation of DNA fingerprint population frequency values

The Cell

DNA - The Molecule behind Forensic Genetics

Inside Your Cells

Diagram of an animal cell. 

Every biological organism is composed of numerous microscopic cells. An average human is made up of over 300 trillion cells. Despite the vast number of cells that each organism contains, the contents of cells are amazingly similar from organism to organism. A dog looks much different than a cat and a human looks much different than a cow; however, each living creature contains the same type of organelles in their cells. For example, all cells contain a nucleus, mitochondria, lysosomes, ribosomes, and endoplasmic reticulum, and all of these cellular organelles are made up of proteins. That is why proteins are known as the "building blocks of life".

The nucleus of each cell contains chromosomes, but the number of chromosomes varies from organism to organism. The body cells of a horse contain 66 chromosomes, the body cells of a pig contain 38 chromosomes, and the body cells of a dog contain 84 chromosomes. A normal human body cell contain 46 chromosomes, except for sex cells (egg and sperm) which contain only half the number of chromosomes (that is, human egg and sperm contain 23 chromosomes each).

Chromosomes provide the "genetic blueprint" for the thousands of different proteins found in the body. The information inside the chromosomes provide the important directions to create these proteins. Without chromosomes, one's body would no longer be able to make proteins and he or she could not survive.

A photo of a map of 23 pairs of human chromosomes or 46 individual human chromosomes. 

To create this map, the chromosomes are removed from a single cell and are then photographed. The photographed chromosomes are organized into a map such as the one above, which is called a karyotype.

DNA

Description of DNA

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is found inside each of the 46 chromosomes within the nucleus of a normal human cell. DNA has a unique double helix molecular arrangement. It looks like a "twisted ladder". Although it is only a few microns wide, there is so much of it that when uncoiled it can stretch up to two metres! DNA consists of two matching strands fused together with hydrogen bonds. The precious interior of this "twisted ladder" is composed of four nucleic acid bases that are repeated over and over within the DNA strand. In fact, each DNA strand has some 3 billion repeating nucleic acid bases.

The names of the 4 nucleic acid bases within the chromosomes are adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G). Nucleic acid bases are found in pairs in the interior of the DNA strand. Because of the unique shapes of each of the nucleic acid bases, only certain bases will pair with each other.

  • Adenine will pair only with thymine (A - T).
  • Cytosine will pair only with guanine (C - G).

The distinct sequence of these nucleic acids creates amino acids. As you can see in the image above, a nucleic acid sequence of GCA corresponds to the alanine amino acid (Ala), the AGA sequence to the arginine amino acid (Arg), and so on. In fact, 20 different amino acids in the human body can be combined to create thousands of different proteins.

DNA left on cigarette butts inside the apartment of a mother and daughter murdered in 1993 was linked to a 44-year-old man in Wisconsin who was in prison for attempted murder. The man was charged with two counts of murder based this evidence.

- Sun-Sentinel Digest, Fort Lauderdale, Florida (February 6, 2005)

DNA Analysis

Forensic DNA Analysis

The sequencing of the four nucleic acid bases determines your genes and your genes determine what type of proteins your body creates. Every protein is created by a specific gene. Your body is made up of thousands of different proteins, and they determine your unique appearance and structure. Interestingly, only about 5% of your DNA creates the 30,000 genes that your body uses and 99% of this useful DNA is similar from person to person. Therefore, analysis of this type of DNA is NOT at all useful in forensic science. 

The function of 95% of your DNA is unknown and often referred to as junk DNA. Interestingly, this junk DNA is unique from person to person. In these sections of DNA, the nucleic acid base sequences will often repeat themselves throughout this DNA at a particular locus. For example:

T-A-G C-A-T G-G-G, T-A-G C-A-T G-G-G, T-A-G C-A-T G-G-G

Two distinctive DNA sequences that repeat themselves are used in forensic DNA profiling:

  1. Variable Number Tandem Repeats (VNTR) - 5 to 64 nucleic acid base pairs in length
  2. Short Tandem Repeats (STR) - 3 to 7 nucleic acid base pairs in length

VNTR and STR sections within DNA are the focus of a forensic DNA profile. These repetitive sequences are labeled with radioactive probes for experts to identify a match. In forensic DNA analysis, a total of five VNTR or STR patterns at five different loci have to be identical for it to be considered a match. A particular VNTR or STR pattern may be repeated 14 times at a specific location in your DNA, 20 times at the same site of your sister's DNA, and perhaps 52 times on your uncle's DNA. The odds of someone else having this exact same VNTR or STR pattern repeated the exact same number of times at five different sites in their DNA is highly improbable. Additionally, some VNTR or STR patterns are very rare.

The Wyoming Game and Fish Department (WGFD) use the analysis of animal DNA to help solve poaching crimes. The WGFD performed over 10 000 tests on 725 forensic samples as part of 95 investigations in 2004. The investigations helped the department obtain more than $224,339 in court-ordered fines.

RFLP vs PCR

DNA Print Analysis: RFLP vs. PCR

During the 1980s, Dr. Alec Jefferys founded two of the first DNA analysis labs in the world. One of these labs was in England; the other was situated in the United States. Dr. Jefferys called his private DNA analysis company Cellmark Diagnostics. Countless police agencies and criminal defense lawyers around the world have sought help from Cellmark Diagnostics. For example, they completed DNA analysis from the blood evidence related to the infamous O.J. Simpson murder trial.

When Dr. Alec Jeffreys was working at the University of Leichester in England, he developed the DNA analysis technique known as RFLP or Restrictive Fragment Length Polymorphism. In the RFLP method, the VNTR regions of DNA are extracted from a biological source such as blood, semen, or skin and are tagged with a radioactive probe that is exposed on X-ray film. This results in an illuminated pattern of VNTR gene bands that look somewhat like a "bar code" on a package of something bought in a store. The gene band patterns are then analyzed, compared, and matched by experts. This method requires numerous cells - from 5 000 to 50 000 cells.

A newer method of DNA profiling called Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) quickly replicates numerous copies of a single strand of DNA. Thus, the smaller STR regions are easily analyzed using this method. The replication of DNA is done using an instrument called a thermal cycler. Then, just as in the RFLP analysis method, gene bands are illuminated using radioactive probes, analyzed, compared, and matched. However, a major advantage to PCR analysis is that it yields results in a fraction of the time needed to complete the RFLP method. Also, PCR needs only about 50 cells rather than the thousands of cells required by RFLP.

Example of illuminated DNA patterns created during either the RFLP or PCR methods

In the U.S. state of Ohio, DNA analysis was used to identify a dog that killed a woman. DNA was collected from dog saliva found on the dead woman's clothing. The DNA matched one of three Rottweiler dogs found at a home where dog tracks ended from the scene of the attack.

Source: The Associated Press State & Local Wire (September 5, 2003).

RFLP Step-by-Step

Step-by-Step Description of DNA Analysis

To understand how a DNA print is created, focusing upon the simpler RFLP method is most effective. Some of the basic steps involved in the RFLP method include the following. Please follow the description with the diagram below.

  1. A sample of cells is collected (e.g., blood, hair, semen, skin, saliva).
  2. Using a powerful microscope, the nucleus or mitochondria from a cell is extracted using a micropipette. 
    (Nuclear DNA is normally used for criminal comparison while mitochondrial DNA is normally used for the identification of unrecognizable remains.  We will learn more about mitochondrial DNA in the next lesson).
  3. The chromosomes from this nucleus are isolated. Then, sections of DNA from within a chromosome are removed using a restrictive enzyme.
  4. A fragment of DNA is then placed within a device to be separated using gel electrophoresis. The DNA fragment is embedded within a thin layer of gel at one end of the device, the negative (-) pole, which has an overall negative charge. Opposite to the embedded DNA fragment is the positive (+) pole that has an overall positive charge. The positively-charged pole attracts the negatively-charged DNA fragment, and as a result the DNA slowly migrates and "stretches" through the gel to the positive pole.
  5. Once the DNA fragment has been stretched out, the DNA print is transferred onto a nylon membrane or X-ray film and preserved.
  6. Radioactive probes are applied to VNTR sections being identified in the DNA print. This makes distinct gene bands appear visible to the naked eye.
  7. Once the DNA print is complete, the analysis and comparison of the distinct gene band pattern occurs.

Mitochondrial DNA

The Function of the Mitochondria



Mitochondria, found in the cytoplasm of the cell, are the second largest organelles in the cell. They operate independently with little interaction with other organelles in the cell. Shaped like kidney beans, mitochondria are responsible for cellular respiration, the production of energy. Mitochondria contain enzymes that convert food material into adenosine triphosphate (ATP) that is used by all the cell organelles as an energy source. ATP is the energy molecule required for all cellular activities; every cell requires ATP to survive. In motile cells or cells that move (such as sperm cells), mitochondria are concentrated near moving structures (such as flagellum) because they require large amounts of energy to move.

The Discovery of Mitochondrial DNA

Previous to the early 1960s, it was thought that the DNA in a cell was only in the nucleus. However, in the early 1960s, mitochondria were discovered to have a small amount of DNA. This genetic material in the mitochondria became known as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). The structure of mtDNA is different from the DNA in the nucleus (nuclear DNA). It is seen in unorganized looping chains instead of within chromosomes.

A human ovum has more mitochondria than sperm cells have. This is because ova are much larger than sperm cells and because sperm cells need just enough energy to power them on their journey to an ovum. Immediately after a sperm penetrates an ovum (conception), the mitochondria in the sperm are destroyed leaving only the mother's mitochondria in the zygote. This is why 100% of the mtDNA in human cells comes from the mother. 

Your mtDNA is gained through maternal inheritance; therefore, your mtDNA is identical to your mother's mtDNA. All children who share the same biological mother have the same mtDNA. In fact, your mtDNA is identical to anyone from the maternal side or mother's side of your family.

Sample Family Pedigree Chart

In the above four generation pedigree, all the family members represented by a shaded circle or square have the same mtDNA. All the females pass their mtDNA to their children, but all the males who have children do not pass on their mtDNA. Therefore, the children do not have the same mtDNA as their fathers. In contrast, nuclear DNA is different in that half of the chromosomes in the nuclei are inherited from the mother and half of the chromosomes in the nuclei are inherited from the father.

The discovery of maternally inherited mtDNA has proven extremely useful in forensic investigations when unrecognizable human remains have been found. Comparison of the mtDNA from the remains with living maternal family members can confirm the identity of the deceased individual. Mitochondrial DNA analysis is completed in some criminal cases when no nuclear DNA is found in the biological evidence recovered.

Mitochondrial DNA was used to identify a direct descendent of the oldest skeletal remains found in Britain. The skeleton was that of a 23-year-old male who died from a blow to the head. The living descendent was a 42-year-old history teacher who lived just a short distance away from where the bones were first found in 1903. Mitochondrial DNA extracted from a tooth cavity in the 9,000 year old skeleton was used to link the two distant relatives.

mtDNA vs. nuclear DNA

Mitochondrial DNA vs. Nuclear DNA

Genetic material in the mitochondria of the cell is known as mtDNA, and the genetic material in the nucleus is known as nuclear DNA. A couple of major similarities and numerous differences between the mtDNA and nuclear DNA. Understanding the similarities and differences between these two types of genetic material helps explain why each is used in forensic investigations.

Some similarities between the DNA in the mitochondria and nucleus include the following:

  • Nuclear DNA and mtDNA are both comprised of the four basic nucleic bases of adenine, cytosine, guanine, and thymine.
  • Both nuclear DNA and mtDNA can be isolated and analyzed using the RFLP and PCR methods of DNA print analysis.

Some differences between the DNA in the mitochondria and nucleus include the following:

  • Nuclear DNA has a double-helix structure while mtDNA has a looping chain configuration.
  • Human cells have less mtDNA than nuclear DNA. Of the 3 000 000 000 nitrogen-base pairs of DNA, only 16 569 are mtDNA; the remaining DNA is found in the chromosomes of the nucleus.
  • Every cell has only one copy of nuclear DNA because every cell contains only one nucleus. However, every cell has numerous copies of mtDNA because every cell has more than one mitochondria.
  • Nuclear DNA can provide individual identification while mtDNA can identify a person only as being from a certain family.
  • Nuclear DNA can be obtained only from intact cell nuclei while mtDNA can be extracted from very badly damaged remains such as ancient bones or badly burnt bones.
  • Nuclear DNA is found in cells from blood, semen, and skin as well as follicle cells attached to hair roots that have been forcibly removed. Mitochondrial DNA can be found in hair shafts (no follicle attached), bones, teeth, urine, and saliva – all types of biological evidence that often do not have intact nuclei.

How Mitochondrial DNA is Used

When forensic cases arise with insufficient biological evidence for nuclear DNA analysis, mtDNA analysis may be used. Mitochondrial DNA analysis can be completed using small samples such a hair shaft 0.5 cm in length, fragments of bone, or even a single tooth. A match of the mtDNA pattern from one individual with the mtDNA of another individual can prove either their identity or that the two individuals are related.

Mitochondrial DNA print analysis is often used by forensic scientists to identify unrecognizable human remains from plane crashes, car crashes where the vehicle has burned, murder cases where the victim has been burned or buried for a long time, or bodies thought to be soldiers missing-in-action. Mitochondrial DNA print analysis is also used in cases where the maternity of adopted children or missing children is in question.

A criminal case where mtDNA print analyis may not be useful is one in which two or more maternal relatives (that is, two siblings) are suspects. Maternal family members have identical mtDNA. Due to the increasing demand of mtDNA forensic analysis by police agencies, the FBI in 2003 began operating four separate DNA profiling laboratories across the USA solely dedicated to analyzing mtDNA samples from crime scenes.

The first criminal case in history to be solved using mtDNA was the murder and sexual assault of a four year-old girl in Tennessee, U.S.A., in September, 1996. Forensic DNA analysis experts from the FBI performed mtDNA testing on foreign hairs recovered from the girl's throat and her bedsheets; they matched mtDNA samples from the defendant. A jury convicted the defendant and sentenced him to life in prison with no parole.

Source: American Prosecutors Research Institute

Mitochondrial DNA analysis was used to clear a man convicted of a 1981 murder in Oklahoma, U.S.A.. Analysis of the mtDNA in a hair shaft found in a gag stuffed in the victim's mouth proved that the hair did not belong to the convicted man as was originally thought.

Source: CNN (November 3, 2003)

Case Study - Unknown Soldiers

Case Study: Forensic Identification of Unknown Soldiers



Unquestionably, the largest ongoing scientific research program involving mtDNA analysis is occurring at the United States Defense Department's Joint POW/MIA Central Identification Laboratory in Rockville, Maryland, USA. The task of the forensic anthropologists and forensic odontologists working at this laboratory is to locate, exhume, and identify the missing human remains of American military members from Word War II, the Korean War, the Vietnam War, and the Gulf War. This is a huge task because approximately 45 000 soldiers are missing from these military conflicts. Since this program began in the 1990s, over 1 000 human remains have been identified and returned to their families.

The first step in the identification of recovered human remains of a military member is an examination by a forensic anthropologist. The amount of remains analyzed is usually dependent on how the soldier died. The skeletal remains of ground combat soldiers are more complete and better preserved than those remains of pilots whose aircraft crashed into the ground at high speeds. The remains are first laid out in anatomical order and any foreign debris is removed. After a forensic anthropologist examines the size, density, and other characteristics, he or she can often hypothesize the age, gender, and race and even identify some medical disorders of the deceased person.

After examination by the forensic anthropologist, any teeth found are examined and possibly identified by a forensic odontologist. This involves a computer database containing thousands of dental records from soldiers (excluding World War II soldiers). If dental identification cannot be completed, an mtDNA analysis done. The mtDNA taken from teeth, bones, or hair from the remains are analyzed, compared, and matched to samples from an mtDNA database. The database used contains mtDNA reference samples donated by maternal family members of missing military members of soldiers from World War II, the Korean War, or Vietnam War.

If the remains are from recent times (such as the Gulf War or the Iraqi War), the mtDNA is compared to entries in a large database of mtDNA samples from the soldiers themselves. Since the late-1980s, DNA samples have been taken from every military member for identification purposes. Currently, this database contains over 4 million nuclear DNA and mtDNA samples from military members and private citizens or contractors working for the US Defense Department in foreign countries. The existence of this DNA database will likely lead to no more remains from military members being classified as being "unknown".

mtDNA Identification and the Unknown Vietnam Soldier

Source image unavailable
Tomb of the Unknowns at Arlington National Cemetery. Photo courtesy of Sergeant A. Kowalyk (2006)

The Tomb of the Unknowns in Arlington, Virginia, U.S.A., is a popular tourist destination. The Tomb contains the remains of unknown American soldiers from World War I, World War II, the Korean War, and the Vietnam War and is guarded 24-hours-a-day every day of the year by specially trained military members. 

The power of mtDNA print analysis was demonstrated in 1998 when the remains of an unknown soldier from the Vietnam War were exhumed from the Tomb of the Unknowns. A forensic team matched mtDNA from the unknown remains with a donated mtDNA sample from one of the many families of missing relatives. Twenty-six years after he was shot down in Vietnam, the remains of Air Force Lieutenant Michael Blassie were identified. Testing of mtDNA was not available when Blassie was shot down in 1972 during a combat mission in Vietnam or in1984 when he was placed in the Tomb of the Unknowns.

Shortly after they were identified, Michael Blassie's remains were moved to a cemetery near his home in St. Louis, Missouri. The Vietnam crypt in the Tomb of the Unknowns is now empty.


In July of 1991, mtDNA analysis was used to identify nine bodies exhumed from a shallow grave in Russia. All of the skeletal remains were identified as members of the Romanov family, the last of the Russian royal family executed in 1918 by the Bolsheviks. Identification of the Romanov family was made after by comparing an mtDNA sample from a living Romanov descendent, Prince Philip, the Duke of Edinburgh (married currently to Queen Elizabeth) with the mtDNA from the bones of the nine skeletons.

Case Study: 9-11

Case Study: Identifying the Victims of 9-11

The largest single disaster that involved the use of mtDNA analysis to identify human remains was the tragedy of September 11, 2001.

Source image unavailable

The two burning towers of the World Trade Center after terrorists hijacked and flew two large commercial airplanes into them. Shortly after this photo was taken both towers collapsed.

Thousands of tissue fragments were collected from the rubble of the World Trade Center in New York City, New York, U.S.A., after it had been destroyed by terrorists. Using forensic mtDNA analysis, the New York Medical Examiner's Office was able to identify 1 592 out of the 2 749 victims from approximately 20 000 body parts and tissue fragments that were collected.

To link body parts or tissue fragments to individual victims, an mtDNA database was created. To create this mtDNA database, relatives of the victims were asked to donate blood samples from which their mtDNA was collected. Also, relatives were given the difficult task of trying to collect mtDNA samples from their missing family members on articles such as hairbrushes and toothbrushes.

Identification of large body parts such as skulls and hands were made rapidly using dental and fingerprint records. Identification of the smaller tissue fragments took longer because mtDNA analysis methods had to be used and, unfortunately, this did not always prove successful. Many of the victims' DNA within the tissue fragments were damaged by the heat of the explosions or humidity from the water used to douse the fires; other tissue fragments were simply too small.

In February 2005, the effort by the New York Medical Examiner's Office to identify the remaining tissue samples ended because the samples left were either too small or too badly damaged. Over 9 000 small unidentified pieces of bone and tissue were sealed and stored in the hope that in the future a technology will be developed to identify these remains.

Identifying Victims from the Pentagon

Less than an hour after the two towers of the World Trade Center were hit by two commercial airplanes, the Pentagon was hit by a hijacked Boeing 757 commercial airplane in a related terrorist attack. The Pentagon, located a short distance from Washington, D.C., in Arlington, Virginia, is the headquarters of the U.S Department of Defense. The crash caused part of the building to collapse, killing 125 Pentagon personal and all 64 passengers aboard the plane.

The Office of the Armed Forces Medical Examiner in Washington, D.C., was in charge of the identification of the victims from this tragedy. Over 2000 separate tissue fragments were analyzed and identified by a large team of military experts. The task of identifying the human remains from the Pentagon was relatively easy compared to the identification of remains from the World Trade Center. The grim process took a relatively short period of time--a little over two and one-half months. This was not because there were fewer people killed; rather, DNA samples for the majority of the victims were available on a database prior to the incident because the majority of victims were military personal from the Pentagon whose dental and DNA records were readily accessible.

Mitochondrial DNA analysis evidence helped to convict Scott Peterson of killing his 27-year-old wife and their unborn son in Modesto, California, U.S.A.. Mitochondrial DNA extracted from a human hair shaft found in a pair of pliers in Peterson's boat matched his wife's mtDNA. 

Source: CNN (November 3, 2003)

Population Frequency

DNA Profiling & Population Frequency

"I have good news and bad news," a defense attorney told his client. "First the bad news. The blood test came back, and your DNA is an exact match with the sample found on the victim's dress."

"Oh, no - I'm ruined! What's the good news?" cried the client.

"Your cholesterol is down to 140," said the defense attorney.

Making the conclusion that a DNA sample matches another DNA sample is not a quick or simple process. It is done only after careful and rigorous analysis of both DNA samples and after a comparison to the general population is made using probability ratios involving population frequency.

Validating a DNA Match Using Population Frequency Values

Human chromosomes have so much DNA that to match crime scene DNA samples to suspect DNA samples forensic experts must focus upon certain sections of DNA that are unique and distinctive. The unique segments of DNA used are known as the VNTR and STR fragments. To ensure further that the DNA analysis is accurate, VNTR or STR sections from five different loci are compared. Even so, there is still a chance that a certain VNTR sequence or STR sequence can be found randomly in more than one person. Forensic experts acknowledge this fact, and with each DNA match a related probability is included. The probability given is the frequency of this DNA sequence occurring in the general population; this is known as population frequency.

Population frequency values are presented with each DNA match because a data bank of all human DNA does not exist. If a sample of the DNA of every person in the world or even a country did exist, there would be no need to include a population frequency value with a match. Instead, the match would be undisputable. Some nations have begun DNA data banks for their respective countries containing DNA from convicted criminals.

The probability range for RFLP data is high - one in hundreds of thousands or even one in a million. This is because RFLP focuses upon the longer VNTR fragments that contain numerous nucleic acid bases. The more nucleic acid bases in a DNA sample, the less likely an identical match to this sequence will be found. However, now that the RFLP method has been in use for over 20 years, the occurance of laboratory interpretation errors is fairly high. Therefore, this method is being used less frequently in forensic investigations.

The PCR method of DNA analysis uses STR sequences. The probability range of a PCR analysis tends to be somewhat lower than RFLP analysis because of the smaller size of the STR DNA fragments used. The advantage of PCR analysis is that the occurence of laboratory interpretation errors is significantly less in comparison to RFLP analysis. However, any small contamination of the evidence collected or contamination of any of the lab equipment used leads to false results. Although the probability range is lower than RFLP analysis, PCR analysis has steadily become the preferred choice of DNA profiling in forensic investigations because of its ability to analyze small amounts of DNA evidence in a short period of time with less error.

In 1989, Allan Legere was serving a life sentence in prison for murder. He escaped while he was at the hospital being treated for an ear infection. In his seven months of freedom, he killed four people, sexually assaulted three women, and committed one act of arson. He became the first Canadian to be convicted using DNA evidence in 1991.

Probability Ratios

Interpreting DNA Match Probability Ratios

To understand better the meaning of the population frequency values presented with DNA profiles, consider these two examples.

Example #1: Weak DNA Match

DNA from unknown blood at a crime scene was matched to the DNA from the prime suspect. In addition to the forensic experts declaring a match, the DNA sample is compared to applicable population frequency data. The numerical chance that the DNA match could belong to someone other than the suspect was calculated to be 1 in 8 900

This means that one out of every 8 900 people could also match the DNA from the crime scene.

Example #2: Strong DNA Match

DNA from unknown blood at a crime scene was matched to the DNA from the prime suspect. In addition to the forensic experts declaring a match, the DNA sample is compared to applicable population frequency data. The numerical chance that the DNA match could belong to someone other than the suspect was calculated to be: 1 in 2 000 000

This means that one out of every two million people could also match the DNA from the crime scene.

Conclusion:

The probability ratio in Example #2 is a much stronger match than Example #1. Example #2 is a more convincing piece of DNA evidence because of the significantly lower chance that the DNA matches another person's. The reason that fewer people in the general population match the DNA in Example #2 is that the VNTR/STR sequence that was analyzed was an unusual pattern in human DNA.

DNA Matching

Forensic Determination of DNA Match

The STR and VNTR that are used in DNA profiling are distinct in two ways:

  1. the arrangement of the nucleic acid base pairs (for example, ATA - GTA - CAC)
  2. the number of times this distinct pattern repeats itself at a particular locus on a DNA strand

When investigators are trying to compare two samples of DNA, they look at the same locus in each sample (for example, chromosome 12 or chromosome 20).

For example, eight repeats of a particular STR are found at one locus of a suspect DNA sample, and eight repeats of this same STR are found at the same locus in a crime scene DNA sample. This could indicate that the two DNA samples came from the same person. However, you cannot make this assumption upon analysis of a single locus because there is a chance that someone else on Earth could have this same STR at the same locus. Therefore, in forensic investigations, a five-locus DNA profile is done in which VNTR/STR patterns from five different loci are examined. If a suspect's DNA shows the exact same VNTR/STR repeat patterns at all five loci as a crime scene sample, the probability that the DNA found at the scene came from someone other than the suspect is very small.

The inheritance of the VNTR/STR patterns at each locus is independent of any other locus. Therefore, the percentages must be multiplied to determine the probability of the DNA coming from someone other than the suspect. If an unusual VNTR/STR pattern has been found and analyzed in a crime scene sample and a suspect sample, the odds of another person with an identical VNTR/STR at five different loci can be staggering, perhaps 1 in 40 billion.

Determination of Population Frequency Values

To estimate the frequency of a particular DNA pattern, the number of occurrences of the pattern is counted in a random population sample. For example, if a particular VNTR/STR pattern occurred in 1 of 100 DNA samples, the estimated population frequency would be 1%.

Population frequency values used for forensic DNA typing are based on population genetic principles. Three steps occur in the calculation of these values:

  1. The number of alternate forms of a particular VNTR or STR section are calculated.
  2. The number of each of these VNTR or STR sections in a databank of DNA samples is recorded.
  3. The chance of having each of these different VNTR or STR sequences at five different loci is calculated mathematically to produce a probability ratio.

Although a databank might contain only 500 people, numerous different VNTR or STR patterns might result in an estimated population frequency of one in a billion.

The FBI has reported that in their DNA profile database, no two five-locus DNA profiles have ever matched.

In December 2005, after serving 24 years in prison, Robert Clark was proven innocent of a 1981 attack on an Atlanta woman. Clark was the 164th person in United States to be freed using post-conviction DNA analysis.

DNA & The Law

The Use of DNA Population Frequency Values in Court 


 

Probability ratios based on population frequency values have been known to confuse jurors and even some judges during criminal trials. This is because the mathematics behind these ratios is not easily understood. This confusion can lead to incorrect verdicts. Frequently in criminal trials involving DNA evidence, experts are brought in not only to explain the results but also to explain how the results were determined.

Defense lawyers are quite aware that DNA evidence can be difficult to understand; therefore, they do their best to play up this fact. Lawyers for the accused often try to convince jurors that the mere existence of a chance of a DNA sample matching someone else in the population is enough to "prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt".

To eliminate the doubt in DNA evidence that some may have, the public must be educated about what DNA is and how DNA profiles and matches are determined. Two effective ways to educate people about forensic DNA profiling are by increasing the incorporation of this scientific concept in school curriculums and by publicizing in the mass media criminal cases that use DNA evidence .

Canada's National DNA Bank



In July 2000, after years of lobbying by police and Crown prosecutors across Canada, a federal law was passed by Parliament that established a national criminal DNA data bank. The law was not passed without controversy; defense lawyers portrayed the law as an invasion of privacy.

Canada's national DNA data bank stores DNA collected from unsolved crimes committed across the country, and it stores DNA from individuals convicted of serious crimes. The crime(s) of which one must be for DNA to be taken include the following:

  • manslaughter
  • murder
  • aggravated assault
  • assault with a weapon
  • violent sex crimes
  • impaired driving causing death
  • robbery
  • possession of child pornography
  • break and enter
  • assaulting a police officer

After a convicted criminal's DNA record is entered into the national DNA data bank, it is compared to samples found at various unsolved crimes. If there is a DNA match, police are notified and the case is reopened.

Many police agencies believe that the establishment of a national DNA data bank is a breakthrough crime fighting tool. Some agencies believe that the law is not tough enough and want DNA samples obtained from every convicted criminal and not just the those who commit serious crimes. They think this would establish a comprehensive data bank that could provide better results.

The establishment of Canada's national DNA data bank has lead to some impressive results. Mroe than 84 000 DNA samples from convicted criminals have been entered into the databank since 2000. Another 24 020 samples have been taken and stored into the data bank from crime scenes across Canada. Matches between crime scene DNA and suspect DNA have been made in 3 966 cases while another 526 samples have been matched to other crime scenes.

Julian Fantino, the chief of police from Toronto, Ontario, has suggested that Canada is lagging behind other countries with regard to the forensic use of DNA as an investigative tool. Chief Fantino would like to see police take DNA samples from any person who has been arrested.

Source: "Do more with DNA: Fantino," The Toronto Star, October 19, 2004.

Christopher Hampton has been brought to justice after his daughter's DNA was put on the national database following a domestic incident with her partner. Police first collected DNA samples from St Stephen's Court in Bath, Somerset, where Melanie Road, 17, was found in a pool of blood at 5.30am on June 9 in 1984.  A DNA profile of the killer, extracted from samples of semen from the scene, was loaded onto the national DNA database in the 1990s.  There was no match from the database at that time as Hampton had not been arrested for any criminal offences.

Scientific advances meant that familial DNA links could be identified, a breakthrough that proved crucial in solving Melanie's murder. Scientific work continued and Hampton's DNA was found on four other areas of semen staining on Melanie's trousers and pants. His DNA also matched semen on swabs taken from Melanie's body during a post-mortem examination. It was also in blood spots found near Melanie's body.

Speaking outside court, Detective Chief Inspector Julie MacKay said: "The key to solving this case has been a combination of traditional police enquiries, advances in forensic science and the tenacity of a small group of officers and police staff.  Ms MacKay told reporters she believed Hampton would have been eventually caught if it had not been for his daughter's DNA being put on the national database. She praised the efforts of officers who worked on the 1984 investigation and said the senior investigating officer had died regretting Melanie's murder was unsolved.

"If in 1984 they hadn't swabbed every one of those 71 blood spots, if they hadn't meticulously examined everything that was available to them from Melanie's clothing and her body we would not have had that DNA available to us," she said.

Case Study - OJ Simpson

Case Study: The O.J. Simpson Double Murder Trial

Forensic DNA analysis had been available for over 10 years; however, the general public did not know much about it before 1994. The very high profile O.J. Simpson double murder trial brought this process to the attention of the world.

Background Information

On the afternoon of June 13, 1994, in the upscale suburb of Brentwood, Los Angeles, California, the slain bodies of Nicole Brown Simpson and Ron Goldman were found. The bodies, discovered by her neighbours, were lying in Ms Simpson's yard. The crime scene was a bloody mess as both victims had died from multiple stab wounds inflicted during a violent struggle between the victims and their attacker(s). When members of the Los Angeles Police Department (LAPD) arrived, they found one black leather glove soaked in blood and some bloody footprints, but no weapon or fingerprints.

Nicole was the ex-wife of famed NFL superstar/sportscaster/actor O.J. Simpson. She and O.J. had two young children -- a boy and a girl. Nicole and O.J.'s marriage had been turbulent. She had called the police to their home on at least two occasions after O.J. had become violent with her. After they divorced, they shared custody of their children and had frequent contact with each other.

Ron Goldman was an aspiring actor/model who worked as a waiter at a popular restaurant. Ron and Nicole were acquaintances, and there was some speculation that they were romantically involved; however, this has never been confirmed.

When police went to O.J. Simpson's estate to notify him of his ex-wife's death, he was not there. However, detectives discovered bloodstains on the outside of his Ford Bronco that was parked on the street. They also found a black leather glove, soaked in blood, laying behind his house. This gave the police enough reason to obtain a search warrant for O.J.'s home.

When Simpson's home was searched, two black socks with blood on them were recovered from his bedroom, and some small droplets of blood were found on his driveway leading up to his house.

After police eventually found O.J. Simpson and informed him of his ex-wife's death, they began questioning his alibi on the evening of June 13th. Mr. Simpson told police that he met Nicole and her family at their daughter's dance recital in the afternoon. He then went home and spent the early evening with his friend/house guest, Kato Kaelin. O.J. then said that he had been picked up from his estate late in the evening and was driven by limo to the airport, flying to Chicago at 11:45 p.m. When Kato Kaelin was questioned, he told police that he had been with O.J. from 7:00 p.m. until 9:45 p.m. Then O.J. said he was "tired" and Kato did not see him again until 11:00 p.m. From between 9:45 p.m. until 11:00 p.m. on June 13th, O.J.'s whereabouts were unknown.

Within a week of Nicole Brown Simpson and Ron Goldman's deaths, O.J. Simpson was charged with murder. The LAPD had many types of evidence against O.J. Simpson; however, one of the key pieces of evidence used by the prosecution was the DNA evidence found at the crime scene.

The DNA Evidence & Probability

The murder of Nicole Brown Simpson and Ron Goldman was violent, and as a result the exclusive source of DNA was blood. All the DNA evidence found was analyzed by two different agencies: a private laboratory, Cellmark Diagnostics Inc., and a government facility, the California Department of Justice.

Remember that some sections of DNA are more distinctive than others (that is, VNTR, STR). Therefore, a DNA match is supported by a probability ratio based upon population frequency values. The probability ratio predicts the numerical chance that the DNA match could belong to someone other than the individual specified.

Crime Case Studies Involving Forensic Genetics

Although DNA print analysis is a new branch of forensics, it has quickly become one of the most effective and powerful tools that can be used by forensic scientists. In crime scene investigations the analysis of nuclear DNA has proven useful in the identification of victims and the individualization of criminals. The analysis of mtDNA has been used successfully in the identification of unrecognizable human remains of combat soldiers and victims of crime, war, and horrific disasters.

The following lesson focuses upon two crime case studies that involve forensic genetics: one true crime story, The Punky Gustavson Story, and one fictional crime story, The Bandana Bank Robbery.

"Killers can seem smart when you can't figure out who they are."
- Pat Brown (Well-known Criminal Profiler and Author)

Crime Case Study #1: The Punky Gustavson Story

The Crime

Clifford Sleigh, 42, from Lodgepole, Alberta, went to Edmonton, Alberta, for a few days in 1992 to play in a softball tournament and visit his family. On a Friday night after drinking alcohol heavily, Clifford got into an argument and a fistfight with his brother-in-law. The next morning he got into a verbal spat with his wife. Frustrated and angry about both incidents, Clifford took off in his brother-in-law's car the morning of September 6th. During this time he bought some alcohol, got drunk, and pulled into a parking lot near a condominium complex close to where he had been staying. He spotted two young girls playing in a fenced yard nearby. After watching them for a time and not noticing any adults in the vicinity, he went to the yard and grabbed the little girl closest to the fence. Likely because she was so startled, the frightened young girl did not put up much resistance when she was abducted. Clifford Sleigh then drove the girl to an undisclosed location, sexually assaulted her, smothered her, and then dumped her lifeless body.

The Victim and the Initial Search

The little girl abducted, sexually assaulted, and killed by Clifford Sleigh was 6-year-old Corinne "Punky" Gustavson. Corinne was given the nickname of "Punky" by her family because her blond hair had the tendency to be spiky and unruly. Punky lived with her parents in a condominium in northeast Edmonton. Her father was home on the morning she was abducted; he had been watching the girls but went inside for a few minutes. After Punky was taken, the 5-year-old friend she had been playing with ran inside to tell her mother and Punky's father. They began searching and soon contacted the Edmonton Police Service (EPS). An intense search of the surrounding area by police and volunteers yielded nothing.

Two days after the abduction, Punky was found face down in a trucking yard on the outskirts of Edmonton. The 6-year-old was fully clothed, her jacket was twisted, her pants were on backwards, both legs were shoved through a single hole of her underwear, and she was wearing only socks on her feet. This case was to become the largest, longest, and most highly publicized criminal investigation in Edmonton's history.

Physical Evidence Found

An autopsy concluded that Punky died from severe blunt force genital trauma and smothering. Punky's clothing was sent to the RCMP's crime lab in Vancouver. In an attempt to find DNA belonging to the culprit, from semen or blood residue, swabs from the victim's anus and vagina were taken. Also, three small sections of Punky's panties were analyzed. In 1992, testing only small sections of clothing rather than the entire garment was a standard procedure. To isolate any semen or blood, filter paper was pressed against each panty section. Then, each piece of filter paper and the swabs were tested for blood using the phenolphthalein regent test (See module 3, lesson 2.) and for semen using the Fast Blue test (See module 3, lesson 3.). Unfortunately, these tests yielded no blood or semen.

During an examination of Punky's body, a single pubic hair was found on her ankle. During analysis of the hair, RCMP forensic experts thought that a small number of follicle cells may be found on the root tip. These follicle cells could yield DNA; however, there was not enough for them to test. At that time, the RCMP were using the RFLP method of DNA analysis, which requires 5 000 to 50 000 cells. The RCMP informed the EPS to test the follicle cells for DNA. The PCR testing method had to be used, but they would not begin using the PCR analysis method until the summer of 1993. 

Collection of Suspect DNA

One of the few pieces of physical evidence they had was the pubic hair. Over the course of this long investigation, the EPS collected DNA samples from over 400 suspects in the hopes that they could be compared to any DNA found in the pubic hair.

At this time, Canada"s DNA data bank had not yet been established (2000), so EPS investigators had to ask the suspects to donate voluntarily their DNA. The majority of suspects asked were sex offenders because these types of criminals tend to re-offend repeatedly until they are caught.

Clifford Sleigh was actually one of the suspects who volunteered his DNA because he was previously arrested and convicted for sexually assaulting two teenage girls. Sleigh was sentenced to 15 years in prison for both crimes. In addition to asking Sleigh for his DNA, investigators asked for his alibi for the day Punky was killed. Sleigh said he was with his family that day. When police asked Sleigh's wife and several of his in-laws, they said he was with them all day. 

Bad News and Good News 

The EPS expected PCR testing of any DNA from the pubic hair left by Punky's killer to be done by the RCMP in 1993. However, the RCMP postponed the initiation of their PCR testing facilities until 1995 and were not able to analyze the hair for DNA until 1997. Throughout this time, EPS investigators patiently collected and investigated thousands of tips from the public with no successful results.

Finally in 1997, RCMP forensic experts used the PCR analysis technique to analyze the hair. After waiting four years for this test, EPS investigators were told that insufficient follicle cells were available on the hair root tip.

After a long period of no solid leads in the investigation of Punky's murder, advances in DNA testing resulted in some good news. In 2000, the EPS sent physical evidence from the case to a forensic lab in the United States that was able to do DNA testing that the RCMP was unable to do. Instead of testing for semen using small sections of Punky"s panties, US forensic scientists tested the entire garment and found some semen. They extracted DNA from the semen, and with the help of the PCR analysis method, they created a complete DNA profile for Punky's killer by 2001. 

Matching the Killer's DNA Profile

When the DNA profile of Punky's killer was sent to Canada's National DNA Data Bank for comparison, no match was found because Clifford Sleigh's DNA was not registered. The EPS then decided to compare the murderer's DNA profile with the DNA samples they began collecting in 1993. The EPS database contained over 400 "raw" DNA samples that had not been turned into full DNA profiles. Clifford Sleigh's DNA should have been within the EPS database. Ironically, none of it was left. All the DNA that Clifford had volunteered had been used during previous forensic testing. Because none of Sleigh's DNA was in the EPS database, no match to the DNA from Punky's killer was found.

In September of 2001, Clifford Sleigh was serving prison time in a minimum security prison in Hobbema, Alberta. Sleigh and another prisoner escaped and fled to British Columbia. Sleigh was eventually caught and sent back to Alberta in September 2002. Before he was sent back to prison, a sample of his blood was taken, a DNA profile was made, and it was registered in the National DNA data bank. In January 2003, the EPS was told that Clifford Sleigh's DNA was identical to the DNA profile of Punky's killer. The chance of a complete DNA match such as this occurring randomly were 1 in 25 trillion.

The Confession and Trial

When told about the DNA evidence, Clifford Sleigh confessed and told investigators he was out to punish someone that day. That someone was an innocent six year-old girl. Sleigh pleaded guilty to kidnapping and sexually assault; however, he did not admit to intentionally killing Punky. He tried to plead guilty to the less serious charge of manslaughter. The prosecution did not accept this guilty plea and put him on trial for first-degree murder.

After eight days of testimony and eleven hours of deliberation, in May 2005 a jury found Clifford Sleigh guilty of kidnapping, aggravated sexual assault, and first-degree murder. He was given a life sentence with no parole for 25 years.

"We caught him. She can rest in peace now.''

- Karen Vallette (Punky Gustavson"s mother)

A Case of Wrongful Conviction

Crime Case Study: The Wrongfully Convicted

 

In addition to being able to help solve crimes, the emergence of DNA profiling has also been used to exonerate innocent people of crimes that they did not commit.  The following case describes just such an event.

The Crime

The crime took place in Red Springs, a town of fewer than 4000 people in the southern part of North Carolina.  On Sunday, 25 September 1983 at approximately 12:20 a.m., Ronnie Lee Buie noticed that his eleven-year-old daughter, Sabrina Buie, was missing from their home in Robeson County when he returned home from working the midnight shift at a nearby business. On 26 September 1983, James Shaw, a friend of Ronnie Lee Buie, found Sabrina Buie's nude body in a soybean field.  She had been raped and badly beaten.

The police picked up Henry Lee McCollum (19) and his half brother Leon Brown (15) were picked up by police in relation to the murder of Sabrina.  There was no physical evidence that tied the two African American youths to the crime, but a local teenager cast suspicion on Mr. McCollum and his half brother.  They had recently moved to the community from New Jersey and were considered outsiders.  In addition, the two youths were severely intellectually disabled, with IQ scores in the 50s.

The Confession

After five hours of questioning with no lawyer present and with his mother weeping in the hallway, not allowed to see him, Mr. McCollum told a story of how he and three other youths attacked and killed the girl. He then signed a confession statement written in longhand by the investigators.

Mr. Brown, after being told that his half brother had confessed and facing similar threats that he could be executed if he did not cooperate, also signed a confession.  The other two men mentioned in Mr. McCollum's confession were never prosecuted.

The Trial and Sentencing

The two young defendants were prosecuted by District Attorney Joe Freeman Britt.  Both men recanted their confessions at the trial, saying that they had been coerced. Despite this, both men were found guilty.

Initially, both defendants were convicted of first-degree murder and rape and received death sentences.  After new trials were ordered by the State Supreme Court, Mr. McCollum was again sentenced to death while Mr. Brown was convicted only of rape and his sentence was reduced to life.

In 1994, the United States Supreme Court turned down a request to review the case.  Justice Antonin Scalia described Mr. McCollum's crime as so heinous that it would be hard to argue against lethal injection.  But Justice Harry A. Blackmun noted that Mr. McCollum had the mental age of a 9-year-old and that "this factor alone persuades me that the death penalty in this case is unconstitutional."

DNA Evidence

Years later, lawyers from the Center for Death Penalty Litigation, working with private law firms, began pressing for DNA testing of the physical evidence in the case, which included a cigarette butt found near sticks used in the murder.

Recent DNA testing by an independent state agency of evidence gathered in the initial investigation found a match for the DNA on the cigarette butt - not to either of the imprisoned men, but to Roscoe Artis, who lived only a block from where the victim's body was found and who had a history of convictions for sexual assault.

Only weeks after her murder, in fact, Mr. Artis confessed to the rape and murder of an 18-year-old girl in Red Springs.  Mr. Artis received a death sentence, later reduced to life, for that crime and remains in prison.  Officials could not explain why, despite the remarkable similarities in the crimes, they kept their focus on Mr. McCollum and Mr. Brown even as the men proclaimed their innocence. 

Release

After three decades on death row, Henry Lee McCollum (50) and Leon Brown (46) were declared innocent and ordered to be released.  Following a hearing, Superior Court Judge Douglas B. Sasser determined that McCollum and Brown's sentences should be vacated "based on significant new evidence that they are in fact innocent" and that they should be released from prison.  The courtroom erupted into a standing ovation.  "We waited all these long years for this,"sid James McCollum, the father of the man released from death row. "Thank you, Jesus," he repeated.

The exoneration ended decades of legal and political battles over a case that became notorious in North Carolina and received nationwide discussion, vividly reflecting the country's fractured views of the death penalty.  The exoneration based on DNA evidence was another example of the way tainted convictions have unrabeled in recent years because of new technology and legal defense efforts like those of the Center for Death Penalty Litigation.

Regarding the confession that Mr. McCollum signed he said in an interview "I had never been under this much pressure, with a person hollering at me and threatening me.  I just made up a story and gave it to them so that they would let me go home."  After he signed his confession he asked "Can I go home now?" according to an account by his defense lawyers.

"I have never stopped believing that one day I'd be able to walk out that door," McCollum said in a videotaped interview with The News & Observer.

"A long time ago, I wanted to find me a good wife, I wanted to raise a family, I wanted to have my own business and everything," he said. "I never got a chance to realize those dreams.  Now I believe that God is going to bless me to get back out there."
