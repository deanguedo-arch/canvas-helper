How This Course Works

A repeatable learning process

  1. Read the brief and vocabulary. Identify the result you are working toward.
  2. Study the lessons and worked example. Revisit them when a decision becomes difficult.
  3. Try the practice decisions. Feedback explains the reasoning and you can retry.
  4. Create the required product in a document, presentation, drawing or recording. Use the response record to summarize decisions and identify your files.
  5. Check the rubric, revise, then choose Save to Portfolio. Print or download a backup when needed.

Plan your time

Each CTS code represents approximately 25 hours of access to instruction and practical learning. A suggested pattern is 6 hours of lessons and source investigation, 4 hours of examples and practice, 9 hours developing evidence, 4 hours testing and revising, and 2 hours evaluating and preparing submission. Adapt the pace with your teacher; there is no timer or automatic credit for hours.

Independent work and assessment

Use fictional customers and supplied data. You do not need to buy anything, travel, contact a business, recruit a partner or attend a live session. Written role responses and solo recordings let you practise applied skills. Some official outcomes require participation or performance evidence that a simulation alone may not demonstrate. Your teacher must validate those requirements before the affected module is released or awarded.

Project connections must refer to successfully completed eligible CTS courses, including one at the project’s level and in the same occupational area. Advanced projects connect to another intermediate or advanced course. Project and practicum codes cannot supply the connections. Teacher confirmation is separate from this page’s progress indicators.

Keep your work

TOU3040 · Learning module · Advanced


Develop an operations plan for a fictional seasonal lodge, connecting room inventory, housekeeping, maintenance, service and financial indicators.
Learning focus: coordinate a property as a system; use indicators without confusing them with profit; build a resilient daily operations plan.


1. Coordinate a property as a system2. Use indicators without confusing them with profit3. Build a resilient daily operations planPracticeYour work


Words to know
Available room-night
one room available for sale for one night.
Average daily rate
room revenue divided by rooms sold in the period.
RevPAR
room revenue divided by available rooms in the period.
Out-of-order room
inventory unavailable because it cannot be safely or appropriately sold.
1. Coordinate a property as a system
Accommodation operations must align the promise made to guests with the rooms and services that can actually be delivered. Reservations, front desk, housekeeping, maintenance and management share dependencies. A room is not ready merely because a guest has checked out; cleaning, inspection and maintenance status matter. Describe the handover points and who can change a room's status.


Compare a seasonal lodge with a hostel, campground or other accommodation model. Ownership, staffing, amenities, operating season and guest expectations can differ. Do not assume the operational solution for a large full-service hotel fits a small property. Identify the implications for scheduling, service coverage, supplies and contingency planning.
Next lesson
2. Use indicators without confusing them with profit
Supplied Harbour Lodge data for one night: 20 rooms are available for sale, 15 are sold, and room revenue is $2,250. Occupancy is 15 ÷ 20 = 75%; average daily rate is 2,250 ÷ 15 = $150; RevPAR is 2,250 ÷ 20 = $112.50. All are fictional room-only figures. None is net profit because expenses and other revenue are not included.


For tomorrow, two of the 20 rooms will be unavailable for maintenance and 16 reservations are confirmed. The remaining inventory is 18 rooms, leaving two unsold if all reservations use one room. Record the inventory definition so a comparison across dates remains meaningful. Never improve an indicator by quietly changing its denominator or selling an unavailable room.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Build a resilient daily operations plan
Scenario injects: a linen delivery is delayed, one guest requests an early arrival and a water leak closes a room. Prioritize safety and verified room status, then sequence housekeeping and communicate options within authority. A plan should identify who decides on alternative accommodation and what information the guest receives. It must not promise unapproved compensation or invent extra rooms.


Create a daily room-status board, staffing-role plan, supply checklist and incident handover using fictional data. Add water, energy and waste considerations with observable actions rather than unsupported sustainability claims. Evaluate a trade-off between service, cost and resource use. An actual property would need its own approved safety and operating procedures.

  3. When a leak closes a room, the response updates inventory and informs the authorized decision-maker before promising a relocation or early check-in.

TOU3060 · Learning module · Advanced


Create a destination-management proposal that balances visitor experience, resident priorities, business viability and care for a fictional place.
Learning focus: start with the place, not only promotion; compare interventions and indicators; recommend, monitor and adapt.


1. Start with the place, not only promotion2. Compare interventions and indicators3. Recommend, monitor and adaptPracticeYour work


Words to know
Destination management
coordinated decisions about a place's visitor experience and impacts.
Indicator
a defined measure connected with an objective.
Visitor dispersal
encouraging visits across times or places rather than concentrating demand.
Trade-off
a benefit gained while another objective or resource is affected.
1. Start with the place, not only promotion
Destination marketing attracts attention; destination management also considers infrastructure, services, resident experience and long-term impacts. A campaign can increase demand faster than a place can manage it. Identify stakeholders and decision authority before recommending growth. Local businesses, residents, governments and rights-holders may have different roles and priorities; consultation is not the same as assuming consent.


Fictional Harbour Reach receives 500 visitors on peak Saturdays and 100 on weekdays. Its waterfront route has congestion at one narrow section, the museum has spare weekday capacity, and residents report parking pressure. These are supplied scenario facts, not real survey findings. Frame a problem that connects evidence with a manageable objective.
Next lesson
2. Compare interventions and indicators
Consider three options: a larger peak-day advertising campaign, timed waterfront information and routing, or a weekday museum-and-town programme. Compare visitor benefit, resident effect, cost, feasibility and environmental implications. A demand-management option may improve experience without maximizing visitor count. State who would need to approve, operate and monitor each option.


Supplied planning costs are $2,000 for a campaign, $1,200 for route information and $1,500 for weekday programming. The fictional allowance is $2,500. Two full options cannot all fit by ignoring their combined cost. You may propose a clearly specified smaller version, but must explain what service or outcome is reduced. Do not invent external grants as guaranteed funding.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Recommend, monitor and adapt
Define indicators with a collection method and interpretation: observed queue length at specified times, resident feedback with response count, weekday museum entries or maintenance incidents. Visitor count alone does not measure resident wellbeing. Record a baseline and a review point; avoid claiming causal success when several conditions changed at once.


Create a proposal with stakeholder perspectives, option analysis, budget, implementation responsibilities, risk controls and evaluation. Use a fictional consultation script or document review rather than requiring business contacts. When cultural or Indigenous tourism is involved, identify the appropriate authority and need for permission; do not invent community endorsement. Revise the proposal after testing an adverse scenario.

  3. A monitoring plan counts queue length at comparable times and records weather or event differences, making the interpretation more credible.

Words to know
Load factor
passenger capacity used, expressed as a proportion for a defined measure.
Connection
a transfer between scheduled services.
Operating constraint
a condition limiting what a service can deliver.
Service recovery
a response to a disrupted or failed service.
1. Connect route design with traveller needs
Air transportation links airports, aircraft, schedules, ground services and distribution. An airline concept must identify its intended travellers, route purpose, frequency, service level and connections. A route line on a map does not establish demand or operational feasibility. Airport facilities, weather, staffing, regulation and aircraft performance require expert validation beyond this classroom concept.


Use a fictional regional service between Harbour Airport and Prairie Gateway, 600 kilometres apart. Proposed capacity is 60 seats per flight and four flights per week in each direction. Target travellers value a same-day connection at the gateway, but the supplied timetable leaves only 25 minutes between services. Treat that connection as a planning risk, not as guaranteed feasible.
Next lesson
2. Calculate capacity and describe the business assumptions
On one fictional flight, 45 of 60 seats are occupied: the seat load factor is 75%. At an average supplied fare of $180, passenger fare revenue is $8,100 before any taxes, fees, ancillary revenue or costs. A full aircraft is not automatically profitable; operating costs and yield matter. State whether the measure concerns one flight, a route or a distance-weighted network.


For a distance-weighted example, 45 passengers travelling 600 km create 27,000 revenue passenger-kilometres, while 60 seats over 600 km provide 36,000 available seat-kilometres. Their ratio is again 75% for this single segment. Do not mix passenger counts from one period with seat capacity from another. Use the calculations to explain assumptions, not to claim a certified business forecast.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Plan service and disruption communication
Scenario: weather delays departure by 90 minutes and threatens the gateway connection. A useful response verifies updated information, identifies affected travellers through the proper process, explains available options and assigns the next update. It does not guarantee compensation, a seat on another airline or a refund without checking applicable law and terms.


Create the original airline concept, airport-journey diagram and two disruption responses. Include access information, baggage/connection questions and official safety/security information sources. Use current Canadian Transportation Agency and official airport or security guidance for real rules; the fictional terms do not override passenger rights. Do not include actual passenger identifiers or imply the concept authorizes operating an airline.

TOU3090 · Surface Transportation
TOU3090 · Learning module · Advanced


Compare rail, coach, private vehicle and water-based travel, then design an original surface-transport plan with realistic timing, costs, terms and disruption alternatives.
Learning focus: compare the whole journey; use supplied comparable data; build a usable connection and fallback.


1. Compare the whole journey2. Use supplied comparable data3. Build a usable connection and fallbackPracticeYour work


Words to know
Total journey time
travel plus access, waiting, transfers and relevant buffers.
Intermodal journey
a trip using more than one transport mode.
Vehicle occupancy
number of people sharing a vehicle or its capacity.
Generalized cost
a comparison that considers money and other burdens such as time.
1. Compare the whole journey
Rail, coach, private vehicle, recreational vehicle and water-based services offer different experiences and constraints. Historical development can help explain networks, but current route availability must be verified. A fast main segment may still produce a slow door-to-door trip if terminals or transfers are distant. Compare the same origin, destination, date assumptions and traveller needs.


Describe booking terms, baggage provisions, access features, service frequency and connection risk. A cruise or ferry is not simply a floating hotel or a road substitute; the itinerary, boarding process and cancellation conditions matter. For vehicle rental or recreational vehicles, cost comparisons need relevant fees, fuel, parking and eligibility assumptions. No actual rental, purchase or travel is required.
Next lesson
2. Use supplied comparable data
Fictional one-way Harbour–Prairie options: coach costs $55 and takes four hours including the supplied terminal access; rail costs $75 and takes three hours plus 45 minutes of access and waiting; a shared car takes three hours and costs $90 total for fuel and parking. For two travellers splitting the car cost equally, the supplied cost is $45 each. Rental, insurance and other vehicle costs are excluded and must not be implied to be free.


A solo traveller comparing only these supplied figures sees coach at $55, rail at $75 and car at $90. Two travellers see a different per-person comparison. Explain how party size and omitted costs affect the result. Do not make universal environmental rankings from mode names alone; occupancy, energy source, distance and the method of comparison matter.
Previous lessonNext lesson
