Start with something familiar

The pedestrian steps off the curb

A vehicle is moving toward a crossing. Seeing a hazard, deciding what it means and moving a foot to the brake all take time. The vehicle continues moving during that interval even when the brakes are sound.

Collision statistics describe groups and conditions, not a prediction for one trip. A useful explanation traces the sequence: detect the hazard, respond, then slow the vehicle.

Separate reaction from braking

Reaction time begins when a hazard can be detected and ends when the driver begins an action. At steady speed, reaction distance = speed × reaction time. Braking distance begins only after braking starts; together they contribute to stopping distance.

For example, at 20 m/s a one-second reaction covers 20 m. A delayed response changes the space available before braking, while road friction changes the braking part. Following space is a practical buffer for both.

Injury can involve rapid changes in body motion and impacts inside or outside a vehicle. The source comparison of road and air travel is historical; use it to question denominators and year, not as a current personal-risk forecast.

Worked example

Find the unseen travel

A vehicle travels at a steady 18 m/s; reaction takes 1.5 s.

  1. Identify the interval before braking: 1.5 s.
  2. Compute d = vt = 18 m/s × 1.5 s = 27 m.
  3. Interpret: the vehicle covers 27 m before deceleration begins.

This is reaction distance only; it does not include braking.

Compare two responses

Start with something familiar

The long drive home

A tired person can miss a sign, react late or make a poor decision. The physics of the vehicle is unchanged, but the human part of the system is less reliable.

Alcohol, fatigue and distraction can all impair driving in different ways. An awake feeling after a stimulant does not prove restored judgment or safe performance.

A table is not permission to drive

The workbook asks students to rank people by drinks and uses a fixed BAC table and elimination rate to predict a safe driving time. Real BAC varies with many factors, and the table is a historical classroom model, not a personal calculator.

Alberta distinguishes federal criminal and provincial impaired-driving thresholds. The 2023 key’s claim of one “legal limit” is too simple. Never use the workbook timing calculation to decide when to drive; arrange a sober ride instead.

For source work, identify the table inputs and explain why the model is uncertain. You can reproduce arithmetic as historical analysis while rejecting its use for real decisions.

Worked example

Audit the table claim

A workbook table says a person with a specified mass and number of drinks has one predicted BAC.

  1. Identify assumptions: standard drink size, body mass categories and a fixed elimination rate.
  2. Name omitted variation such as actual intake, timing, medications and individual response.
  3. Conclude that the number is an estimate under assumptions, not a safe-to-drive test.

The educational task is model criticism, not personalized advice.

Start with something familiar

The road is part of the system

A collision can be less likely because of road design, lighting, maintenance and decisions. If a collision still happens, restraints and vehicle structure can change the injury outcome.

These are different safety jobs. A head restraint limits head-neck motion during some rear impacts; it does not improve tire traction or prevent every neck injury.

Layers before and during a collision

Road signs, lane markings and sightlines help people anticipate hazards. Vehicle maintenance keeps tires, lights and brakes functioning as intended. A barrier or crash cushion may redirect movement or change the impact path.

A safety feature should be evaluated by the hazard it addresses, the mechanism, and its limits. A well-maintained car can still be driven inattentively; a warning sign cannot guarantee that a driver will respond.

The workbook’s road-label figure contains distinct features. Read each label on the original page rather than inferring a name from a tiny crop.

Look for the mechanism

Which devices mainly warn before a hazard? Which may change the path or severity of an impact?

The four photographs show road features with distinct jobs. The images are examples from the historical textbook; actual designs vary.

Worked example

Choose the right stage

A curve has poor visibility and a roadside barrier.

  1. Identify the hazard: a vehicle may leave the curved lane.
  2. Separate prevention: clear signs and adequate sight distance help before loss of control.
  3. Separate mitigation: a barrier changes a possible impact after leaving the lane.

The features act at different stages and neither makes the curve risk-free.

Explain maintenance without overclaiming

A car’s tires have worn tread and one light fails.

Start with something familiar

Two routes to the same place

One person walks a winding path while another goes straight to a library. Both finish at the same place, but they travel different distances.

Distance is path length. Displacement describes change in position with direction. Speed uses distance per time; velocity includes direction. The workbook sometimes uses velocity for a simple travel-rate formula, so name the quantity you actually calculate.

Rate, units and rearrangement

Average speed = total distance ÷ total elapsed time, or v = d/t in a simple one-direction model. Rearranging gives d = vt and t = d/v. Use compatible units: km with h, or m with s.

To convert km/h to m/s, divide by 3.6. The reverse conversion multiplies by 3.6. State whether a number is an average over a trip rather than an instantaneous speed.

A direction must be included to call a vector result velocity. The same speed in opposite directions corresponds to different velocities.

Worked example

Check a 300 km trip

Cameron travels 300 km in 2 h.

  1. Select average speed = distance ÷ time.
  2. Compute 300 km ÷ 2 h = 150 km/h.
  3. Interpret as the trip average, not necessarily the speed at every moment.

The units of distance over time are essential.

Start with something familiar

A runner stops for water

A distance–time graph can show a run, a pause and a second run without a picture of the track. The graph is a record of position or distance against time, not a drawing of the road.

Place time on the horizontal axis and distance on the vertical axis. A steeper rising line means more distance per second in this simple graph. A horizontal line shows no distance change during that interval.

Read rise over run

Slope = change in distance ÷ change in time. The units are metres per second if the axes use metres and seconds. Choose two clear points on the same straight segment; do not divide the final distance by the wrong interval.

A straight rising segment represents constant speed; a curve indicates changing speed. A horizontal segment represents a stop if plotted distance stays constant. A downward line requires careful context: accumulated distance cannot decrease, but position relative to a start can.

For the workbook runner comparison, read which line is steepest, which ends at the least distance and which extends longest in time. These are different questions.

Read the figure: Point to a section where slope stays constant. Which line changes slope, and what does that say about speed?

Build the six-point graph

Start with something familiar

A school zone in rain

A driver sees a reason to stop. The vehicle first travels while the driver responds, then travels farther while the brakes slow it. Rain and tire condition can change the second distance.

A higher speed also means more distance covered every second before braking. Source tables illustrate relationships under stated conditions, not guaranteed stopping distances for a particular road.

Add two distances, not two times

Stopping distance = reaction distance + braking distance. Reaction distance = initial speed × reaction time. Braking distance depends on the initial speed, deceleration and road-tire conditions.

If a simplified model assumes constant deceleration a, braking distance is v²/(2a). Doubling v makes that modeled braking distance four times as large when a is held fixed. Real roads do not provide one universal a.

For the workbook trip table, use each row’s given variables; convert minutes to hours if the speed is in km/h. Q18’s route distances and times are exercise values, not current travel recommendations.
