Ideas and Ideologies

How can people around the world prosper? Should countries protect their own industries and businesses so they can make more money? Should people be encouraged to shop locally?

In the photo, a Peruvian merchant sells her own fabric dye to a local craftswoman in a small open-air market. Could the dye-seller make more money if she sold dye that she had bought in bulk from a larger firm? Could she get cheaper dye that was mass-produced in a large factory far away? Could the buyer get a cheaper price if she was able to shop at a big box store that sold huge volumes?

Peru is a tourist destination. Over a million foreign tourists visit Peru every year and many purchase locally made craft products to take home. Other products are sold in craft shops and art galleries all over the world and over the Internet. What would happen if that market was no longer available to the crafts producers? Would the artisans be able to sell their products, or would they have to find another way to make a living? Would the people who purchased these crafts lose out as well?

What about the movement of people? Should immigration be controlled so that all the jobs in a country are only available to the people who already live there? Or will everyone make more money if the borders are open to all people and all kinds of trade?

In the past, most governments tried to protect their own industries because they believed that by doing so, they would help their citizens prosper. In the past century, more and more governments have come to believe that opening up their markets to global trade will help lead to prosperity for their people.

What is more profitable, a global business or a local business?

 Do you try to buy Canadian products when you are shopping? Should you always look for the “Made in Canada” label? What difference does it make if the car you drive was made in Japan or Oshawa, Ontario? Does it matter if the lettuce in your salad was grown by a local farmer or imported from California? Should the Canadian government encourage local farmers to produce food for the Canadian market, or should they encourage exports?

How has capitalism contributed to the expansion of global economic ties?

Over the past few decades, more and more countries have embraced capitalism as an economic system. Capitalism is an economic system based on free markets, the private ownership of property, and the profit motive. In this photo you can see a busy shopping district in Chengdu, China. China has recently embraced capitalism after years of being a communist country. Now that China has started to produce consumer goods for the global market, its wealth has increased tremendously.

You learned about early capitalism in Unit 2 and it was the focus of your social studies in Grade 9. The following information is for review.

Adam Smith lived in the 1700s. In Adam Smith’s time, his government tried to control the economy in order to protect local businesses and make more money for its own people. This practice is called protectionism. The British government put tariffs on goods that came from other countries in order to encourage people to buy local products. It put quotas on the amounts of certain goods allowed into the country in order to encourage British companies to produce what the people needed. It made all businesses buy licenses, in order to control industry.

Another form of protectionism is subsidies. Governments also try to protect local industries through subsidizing the production of their own goods. That means the government gives extra money to individuals or companies that produce certain goods so that they can compete internationally. This protects the industry, but it can also harm that same industry in other countries where there are no subsidies.

Adam Smith thought that all these regulations were not good for individuals because the regulations interfered with peoples’ freedoms. He also thought the regulations harmed industry. He thought that if all people in all countries worked to benefit themselves, there would be more competition, better products, and lower prices for shoppers. In other words, he thought the government should not try to control the economy. He thought that if it was left to run on its own, everyone would benefit. He called this the “invisible hand.”

There are many factors involved in the production of goods and services. These include the wealth or capital needed to start or expand any operation; the land and property used in production; and the people who do the work, sometimes called the “labour.” In a capitalist system, land, capital, and resource factors are mostly owned or managed by private individuals or corporations. In a capitalist system, it is believed that private ownership is the best way to manage an economy. Capitalists believe that the freedom to make choices about how to run a business should be given to individual people. The potential for wealth encourages people to work hard and to compete for business. The wealth generated by the rich through their business enterprises will “trickle down” to the workers, who will demand and deserve higher wages if their products sell. Through competition, lower-priced goods will be made available to everyone, reducing the cost of living. Everyone who is willing to work will benefit.

Adam Smith’s ideas caught on all around the world. Today, people talk about “free trade.” Free trade happens when all industries, individuals, and governments are free to operate with very few restrictions. The assumption is that when there are few barriers, such as tariffs, to prevent trade between countries, connections across the globe are forged. The following video explains the origins and definition of free trade:

Under a free trade system, each nation sells its resources, products, and labour freely to everyone. Businesses operating in countries that have a lot of natural resources, such as Canada, sell products to countries that don’t have any. Countries that have a large labour force, such as India and China, sell goods and services produced by their people who earn less per hour. The idea is that if there is a “level playing field,” or a system where every country uses the same rules, everyone wins.

Can you think of reasons why free trade would increase prosperity for people? Can you think of reasons why it might not work? Do all countries have products they can buy and sell? Is there enough capital in every country to develop products or services that people will want to buy globally? Are all nations willing to remove tariffs and subsidies so that they can compete for business.

Of course, no countries have completely free trade. There are always some controls. Can you think of any laws or controls that might be necessary?

Did communism contribute to contemporary global connections?

Capitalism is not the only kind of economic system. Imagine a country where all the money in the country was equally shared. People didn’t own their own property, but they all worked for the common good. People were provided with everything they needed to survive by the central government. If the nation itself became richer, the extra money was shared out to all the people.

Communism is an economic system that was a reaction to capitalism. The ideas behind communism came from a man named Karl Marx. Karl Marx was the son of a wealthy industrialist. He saw first-hand the inequality and poor working and living conditions that people suffered during the Industrial Revolution. In his book, The Communist Manifesto, Marx stated his belief that people’s labour should not be exploited to benefit the rich and that factors of production should not be in the hands of a few rich people. He believed they should be owned by the community at large, and all the benefits of people’s hard work should be shared out to people according to their needs.

He believed that the workers of the world would rise up against their capitalist oppressors and take over the running of the country. His ideas were taken up by powerful groups in many countries, especially those where there was extreme inequality. Communist ideas led to revolutions in Russia in 1917, in Vietnam in 1945, in China in 1949, and in Cuba in 1959. By 2008, China, Vietnam, and Cuba still have some form of communist government, as does Laos and North Korea. This led to global connections in its own way, as Communist countries forged connections with other Communist governments.

Communism worked to a certain extent in that communist countries were able to use the strong hand of government to industrialize and modernize their economies and equalize wealth. However, it also led to conflicts between capitalist and communist countries. Ideas, technology, and the exchange of goods were cut off between countries with different political beliefs. The communist system of government ownership involves a lot of rules about what can be bought and sold.

The conflict between capitalism and communism was an important factor in twentieth century history because it kept the nations of the world apart and prevented the globalization of the economy. You will learn more about this issue in future lessons.

Vocabulary

ideologies: set of beliefs, values, and opinions that form the basis of a social, economic, or political system

free trade: trade between countries with relatively few restrictions

protectionism: a set of rules designed to safeguard local industries, including tariffs, quotas, and subsidies

tariff: a duty or tax enforced by government on imported or exported goods

quota: the maximum amount that is permitted or needed

subsidy: money that a government provides a local business in order to help it function

invisible hand: the unseen force that drives the economy, where everyone, looking out for his or her best interest, benefits the whole society

communism: an economic and political system based on government or communal ownership of wealth and property, where each person works for the good of everyone according to ability and receives back according to need

factors of production: resources including land, labour, and capital that are used to produce goods and services
