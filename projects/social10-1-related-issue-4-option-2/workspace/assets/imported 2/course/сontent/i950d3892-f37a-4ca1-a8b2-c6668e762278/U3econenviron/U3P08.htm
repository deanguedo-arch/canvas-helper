How has trade liberalization affected global prosperity?

You examined free trade, or trade liberalization, in previous lessons. Free trade is based on the idea that if all nations trade freely with each other, with very few rules and regulations, everyone will benefit. True “free trade” means that every country has the same laws so no country has an unfair advantage over another. Under true free trade, no country is allowed to practise protectionism, a system in which a country sets up rules to protect its own industries. Some aspects of protectionism include the creation of subsidies, quotas, tariffs, and government programs that support one particular industry.

Trade liberalization is based on the following conditions:

  • international trade of goods and services without trade barriers
  • the free movement of labour and capital among countries
  • no policies, including all of those listed above, that give local businesses an advantage over foreign businesses
  • people having the right to own and control private property

Instead, some countries have created trade agreements with other countries, in which they agree to certain rules by which they will all live. The North American Free Trade Agreement, or NAFTA, preceded the current Canada, US, Mexico Agreement, or CUSMA, a trade agreement among Canada, the United States, and Mexico.

However, nations have their own ways of handling their affairs, so real free trade doesn’t exist.

The Canadian Softwood Lumber Dispute

In Canada, the government controls large amounts of land, called Crown land. Forestry companies can take trees off this land for what is called a stumpage fee that goes to the government. However, in the United States, almost all land is privately owned. Forestry companies have to make private agreements with land owners to buy their trees. This means that lumber in the United States is generally more expensive than it is in Canada. American companies have protested that Canadians have an unfair advantage in the forestry industry. Some American companies have accused Canadian companies of “dumping” lumber on the U.S. market. This means that Canadian companies are accused of selling lumber for less than it costs to produce. However, government ownership of land and reasonable stumpage fees can mean that wealth is more equally shared. What do you think? 

The European Union, or EU, has close to 30 democratic European member countries. In addition, the EU allows free movement of people across the borders of member countries.

How has foreign investment and foreign ownership affected global prosperity?

Foreign investment is money that is invested in one country by people, governments, or corporations in another. Foreign ownership takes place when people from one country own a resource, business, or service in another country. Sometimes foreign investment and ownership can help a developing or a developed country because more money can be put into research and resource development. Outside money can help a poor country by being used to build factories, roads, and other transportation facilities, which can lead to more jobs and more tax dollars for government. However, it can also put pressure on governments to build infrastructure and create training programs for workers. Outside money also means that the natural resource or the product being created is sold to profit people outside the country the resource or product comes from.

Foreign Investment and Ownership in Canada
Another impact of trade liberalization in Canada is increased foreign investment and ownership. This has been an issue throughout Canadian history. Some Canadians are concerned that increased foreign investment and ownership result in the loss of identity and economic independence in Canada. This concern is called economic nationalism—for Canadians, this usually means we do not want to be taken over or absorbed by the United States. This concern is understandable, since we live next door to the most powerful military and industrial country in the world.

Control of Canada's Economy
Canada's population is about the same as that of California, so the United States sometimes tends to take Canada for granted. It is hard for Canada to do the same, especially since 86 per cent of our exports go to the United States. Smaller Canadian companies cannot buy out large American businesses. However, when the reverse happens, it sets off alarm bells for economic nationalists. The last time this happened was in the 1970s, when there was growing public concern about the level of foreign ownership and investment in a number of sectors (especially oil and gas, mining, and manufacturing). This concern led to the creation of the Foreign Investment Review Agency (FIRA) in 1973 by the federal Liberal government. FIRA was part of a broader industrial strategy, based on economic nationalism, that tried to stop further US control of the Canadian economy. FIRA's purpose was to screen foreign takeovers and the creation of new businesses or subsidiaries by non-Canadians.

According to one position, FIRA did reduce the volume of foreign takeovers. According to another position, FIRA actually did little except rubber-stamp nearly all foreign investments and takeovers. After the Progressive Conservatives were elected in 1984, Prime Minister Brian Mulroney announced that Canada was "open for business" again and replaced FIRA with Investment Canada. Its purpose was to encourage foreign investment in Canada. Since then, more than 11 000 Canadian businesses have been purchased by foreign companies.

From Foreign Ownership to Globalization
The issue of foreign ownership and investment as seen by Canadian economic nationalists has basically disappeared because of globalization. The Mulroney government not only "opened Canada for business," but it also signed the Free Trade Agreement with the United States in 1988 and NAFTA in 1992. Since then, the trend has been toward economic globalization with more free trade and open borders.

How has the knowledge economy affected global prosperity?

What is the knowledge economy? This term refers to any business that uses knowledge or information to produce economic benefits. Since the beginning of time, people have used their increasing understanding of how things work to produce greater wealth for themselves. Today, rapid increases in knowledge and the technology to share ideas and developments are important factors in globalization. This sharing means that people all over the world have access to the same ideas and educational opportunities. It means that a person in India could read your medical records and diagnose an illness. It means that a person in China could build your computer, using the latest information.

How can citizens use the power of globalization to benefit everyone?

How can you, as a citizen, do your part? There are many perspectives on the benefits and shortcomings of economic globalization. Statistics tell us that economic growth is happening all over the world. We know that the world’s people are more connected than ever due to migration, trade, and technology. But we also know there is a great deal of inequality in a world where there is a handful of billionaires yet billions live on less than one dollar a day.

You may see that trade liberalization, trade agreements, foreign investment and ownership, and transnational corporations may be improving the global economy, but those improvements don’t benefit everyone. Can you use the power of globalization to contribute to sustainable prosperity for everyone?

The fair trade movement is one way in which people around the world are trying to help others earn a living wage for their labours. Fair trade is an organized movement that tries to lessen poverty and promote sustainability. Fair trade principles include safe conditions for workers, fair prices for goods, and social and environmental standards of production. For example, coffee producers who sell coffee beans on the global market are guaranteed a living wage for the coffee they grow, rather than receiving an extremely low income while most of the profits go to large corporations. Fair trade products include mainly handicrafts, coffee, sugar, tea, cocoa, honey, cotton, wine, fresh fruit, and flowers.

In the fair trade system, organizations work directly with marginalized people to help them market their products. Fair marketing allows the people to rise out of poverty and become self-sufficient, and it encourages them to take an active role in their communities in order to obtain justice and equality.

Microcredit is another example of people helping people. In 1976, Bangladeshi university professor Muhammad Yunus discovered that many people living in small towns, especially women, were being held back from developing their ability to prosper just because they needed very small amounts of money. He began by loaning small amounts to people right out of his own pocket. For instance, a woman needed five dollars to pay off her husband’s funeral expenses. Another needed money to buy thread for her sewing business. Because they needed such small sums, and because they had no real property or goods that could be used as security against a loan, banks were not interested in helping them. But Yunus found that these people were able to prosper and were reliable in repaying their loans. In fact, only two percent of people who borrow on microcredit fail to repay their loans, far fewer than people who borrow money from traditional banks.

Professor Yunus helped establish the Grameen Bank, providing very small business loans. This bank now has over 2000 branches in Bangladesh, and similar banking projects have started in over 40 countries. The World Bank is starting a similar project. In 2006, Professor Yunus was awarded the Nobel Prize for his highly successful ideas.

Mosammat Taslima Begum used a 20-dollar loan from the bank to buy a goat. She later became a successful entrepreneur and is one of the bank’s board members. She went to Norway to accept the Nobel Prize on behalf of Grameen Bank, the only corporation ever to have won a Nobel Prize.

Vocabulary

protectionism: using government controls and regulations to protect businesses

dumping: selling goods in another country at less than what they cost to produce

foreign investment: when one country or a corporation from that country invests money in another country with the goal of making more money for itself

knowledge economy: any business that uses knowledge or information to produce economic benefits

fair trade: a movement in which producers of goods are given a fair wage and practice sustainable business principles
