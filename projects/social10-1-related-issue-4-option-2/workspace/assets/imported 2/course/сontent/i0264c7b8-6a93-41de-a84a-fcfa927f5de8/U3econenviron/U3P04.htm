SS10-1 U3 Lesson 3

Creating Affluence

Where does wealth come from? How does the wealth of individuals or nations become greater or smaller? Who is wealthy and who is not? Why are some countries richer than others? Economic globalization, or the interaction between nations in terms of the buying and selling of goods and services, has an important impact on almost every nation in the world. How does it work?

When people think about globalization, they often think about money. Since early times, people have travelled in order to find the resources they need. This began in the days of early hunter-gatherers who searched for food and continued through the beginnings of global trade routes. In more recent times, economic globalization included the development of imperialism. Today, people in nations around the world are connected through the global economy.

In this lesson you will investigate how nations are connected through economic globalization. You will explore the impact those connections have on the wealth and poverty of their citizens.

How is wealth generated?

Evan is a 12-year-old student. He wants to make some money to buy a new snowboard. He doesn’t have a lot of job skills, resources, or free time.

He lives within a couple of blocks of the stadium in Edmonton. His mom hates the traffic whenever there is a game or other event, especially when people drop garbage in their yard and walk on their lawn. The crowds are going to start passing by in about an hour on their way to a late-afternoon game. Calgary’s team is in town. The game is sold out. With all that cheering, the crowds are sure to be thirsty. Evan remembers that his mom just bought a whole lot of no-name pop. He doesn’t like no-name pop. Then an idea hits him! What if he sold the pop to people on their way to the game? His mom doesn’t want to give him the pop—it was an amazing sale with the pop selling for a quarter a can—but Evan offers to buy it from her for 30 cents, and he promises to stop bugging her about the new snowboard.

Tasha is a mom and a football fan. She’s one of those 60 000 people on their way to the game. Her husband and her teenaged kids are already complaining about being thirsty. She should have brought some pop from home, she thinks.

Then she sees a smiling boy standing behind a little table on the front lawn of a nearby house. She notices a sign taped to the table—Pop $1. It’s no-name pop for a dollar. Outrageous, Tasha thinks, but there’s Evan, pulling icy-cold pop from a cooler. Tasha remembers the price and the huge crowds and line-ups inside the stadium. “I’ll take five,” Tasha says. “Have you got change for a twenty?”

“I’ve only got a ten-dollar bill,” Evan replies. “The rest are twenties.” Tasha looks him straight in the eye to determine whether or not he’s lying. People are waiting impatiently, and Tasha knows that twenty won’t go too far inside the football stadium.

“Okay,” Tasha says, “I’ll take ten.” She knows she’ll need them before the end of the game.

The interaction between Evan and Tasha shows how the demand for a product when met with a supply of that product (in this case, pop) meets a need and creates wealth.

Can people get wealth all on their own, or do they need to trade with others in order to generate wealth? In this lesson you will discover how the same economic principles that created a few dollars for Evan are also at work globally as you explore the issue question: How is wealth generated?

Supply and Demand

A boy wants to make money. He knows he can only make money by selling a service or a product. He sees a need. Football fans are walking by his house, among them a mom with a husband and three children. They want something to drink. Economists call this a demand. Evan has a certain number of cans of pop he can sell. This is called the supply(or "quantity" in the graphs below). The price is an unspoken agreement between Evan and his customers. Evan is willing to sell his pop for a dollar a can. Fans are willing to buy them for the same price.

In this first graph, the blue line represents the football fans’ demand for pop. The red line represents Evan’s supply of pop. He is trying to sell his pop for the most money he can get, and the fans are trying to buy it for the cheapest possible price. At some point, the two must meet. The price at which Evan is willing to sell his pop is equal to the amount fans are willing to pay for it.
What would happen if Su Yin from next door saw how successful Evan was at selling no-name pop, and a couple of games later, she set up her own stand? It’s another hot day and Calgary’s back in town, so the demand for pop is just the same. However, the supply has suddenly increased. People are used to buying from Evan, so Su Yin drops the price of her pop to 75 cents to attract customers. To keep up with the competition, Evan must do the same. The second graph demonstrates what happens to the price when the supply increases.

Another thing that could drive the selling price of Evan’s pop down is decreased demand. Suppose it’s a really cold day and the Eskimos are playing Toronto. There are probably far fewer people wanting a cold pop, so he might have to drop his price to sell his pop.

Take a look look at the third graph. Here you see a decreased demand, as represented by the blue lines. When the demand drops, it drives the price down.

When there are many buyers and sellers of products, selling more or less the same products, and all other factors are more or less equal, there is perfect competition.

Perfect competition requires

  • many buyers and sellers
  • goods and services that are essentially the same
  • everyone having access to information
  • everyone having access to the same production processes
  • everyone having access to the same resources
  • all firms having access to production technologies
  • resources being perfectly mobile
  • anyone being able to enter or leave the market

When there is perfect competition between sellers, everyone benefits because the costs to the buyers are driven down so that the sellers make the smallest amount of profit. Sellers who are trying to stay competitive need to improve their products or sell for less.

What global factors contribute to the distribution of wealth?

The same economic principles of supply and demand and competition for wealth that you saw in your readings about Evan drive the global economy. Nations have a wide array of resources that they can supply. Nations frequently produce more goods than they can use themselves, so they sell them. For example, Canada has many natural resources, including lumber, minerals, oil and gas, and water. Even a small country such as Guatemala can supply goods, such as bananas, oranges, and coffee, to people around the world. The world’s natural resources are not equally distributed. Some countries have a lot of needed materials, while other nations have a lot of some specific goods. Some nations have very little of any marketable product. For example, Canada is rich in its supply of oil and gas, forest products, water, and agricultural goods. A country such as Ethiopia, in Africa, has very few natural resources to sell.

The world’s population is also not equally spread out. If you have ever travelled around Canada, you will know that you can drive for a long time without seeing many people. Canada is a sparsely populated country for many reasons, especially because of its climate and geography. Examine the maps above. In what countries are most of the world’s people located? You’ll see that the bulk of the world’s people live in Asia, including China, India, Japan, and Indonesia. How could a large population be used to benefit people economically? How could a large population present problems for the economy? Some highly populated nations can supply people to produce products.

The distribution of population and resources are two factors that explain the distribution of wealth around the world. Other factors include physical location, industrialization, political and economic systems, and the values held by people in different parts of the world.

  • Physical Location. Some countries are well located in order to trade. Countries where roads are easy to build or where oceans and rivers can be used for shipping can increase their prosperity. Nations that are land-locked, or completely surrounded by land, have a much harder time of getting their supply of goods to potential buyers.
  • Industrialization. Being able to manufacture products creates wealth. Countries that industrialized early in history became wealthy sooner, while countries such as China and India are getting richer today because of their industrial growth.
  • Political and Economic Systems. Different political and economic systems can lead to different degrees of economic success. They can also determine how wealth is shared within a country. Some governments value equality, while others encourage individuals and corporations to make personal profit. In Grade 12 you will investigate these ideologies more.
  • Values. As you determined in Lesson 1, many of the people in some nations don't value money in the same way most people do in other nations. The majority of people in some nations may put less value on becoming rich and more on such matters as their religious beliefs or having a balanced lifestyle.

How do nations depend on each other for wealth?

Different parts of the world have differing supplies of natural resources, land, labour, and capital needed to expand their industries. Some countries have a lot of goods that are not in high demand, so they are hard to sell. Other nations, such as China, have a large supply of workers. Canada can export wood and pulp to China where it can be used to create manufactured goods by the large labour force and sold back to Canada. Both countries prosper.

Trade doesn’t always include money for goods and services. In 2007, the government of Venezuela agreed to provide oil to Cuba in exchange for a supply of trained doctors. This exchange benefited both nations.

Do global exchanges always benefit everyone? Go back to Evan and Su Yin. They are in perfect competition as they sell soft drinks outside their homes. But what would happen if a store opened up nearby? What would happen if that store was part of a large chain or even a large transnational chain of convenience stores?

A convenience store could offer better selection of pop and other products people might want to buy. It would have a much larger supply and a much larger demand. Its economies of scale would make its per-unit costs lower, so it could sell its products for a cheaper price, and its brand-name recognition and marketing power would give it a lot of advantages over the small local pop stands. There would no longer be perfect competition.

The same is true for most businesses around the world. As companies get larger and larger, they can distribute their goods far and wide. Since they have a large distribution, they can sell goods for less, and because they are well known, they can attract lots of business.

Most of the nations of the world have become interdependent. They depend on each other to sell their own goods and buy the goods of others. In this way, trade has been an important factor in the expansion of global connections. Does this trading system benefit everyone equally? As the pop stand example shows, there isn’t always perfect competition. Some—especially those who are already wealthy and powerful—may benefit more than others in the global economy.

Wealth Inequality and Meritocracy

When a system is referred to as meritocratic, this means that success, wealth, and power are assigned based on an individual's achievements and abilities. It's the principle that those who work the hardest and achieve the most should receive the greatest benefits. An example of this might be sporting competitions, in which a title or championship is awarded to the team which has the strongest performance. To use the example of the NHL, this would be the awarding of the President's Trophy to the team which finishes the season with the most points, or the awarding of the Stanley Cup to the team which wins the playoffs, a bracket-style tournament.

In economics, there is, at times, a similar assumption: that wealth is accumulated based on one's achievements and hard work. In cases where wealth accumulates, however there are some questions raised in regards to meritocracy. Take the case of Evan above. For Evan to start his successful business, he has a supply of extra pop from his family. How might it affect his ability to start that his successful business if Evan's family were poorer, and unable to afford more groceries beyond exactly what they needed? Likewise, if Evan's parents were wealthier, and could afford name brand soda and a wider variety of drinks, how might this impact his success? In all cases, Evan is observant enough to see an opportunity and willing to work hard to take advantage of it. However, we can see that when wealth inequality is present, this can have an impact on Evan's success, even though he has no control over it. One might contend that this undermines the idea of meritocracy; that wealth inequality can lead to unfair advantages. Others may contend that this is fair; if Evan's family is successful, they should be able to support him how they see fit. These are questions you will need to consider and answer for yourself.

Vocabulary

trade: the business of buying and selling or bartering commodities among individuals, organizations, or nations

supply: the quantity of a good or service that a producer is willing to provide at a particular price

For example, music CD producers are willing to produce CDs for sale from between $10 and $25, which gives them enough profit incentive to keep producing CDs. (Of course, a store can sell CDs for prices other than what the manufacturer suggests selling them for.)

demand: the desire and ability to buy a good or service

Usually, when demand is high for a product, its price rises. When demand for a product is low, the price goes down. For example, if crops are poor, the price of wheat should rise because demand is high.

perfect competition: the most competitive market possible, where the supply equals the demand

labour: a measure of the work done by human beings

capital: financial wealth; money available for investment

interdependence: when people, organizations, groups, or nations depend on each other for survival

Gross National Product: the total value of all goods and services produced by a country in a year, including income from investments abroad
