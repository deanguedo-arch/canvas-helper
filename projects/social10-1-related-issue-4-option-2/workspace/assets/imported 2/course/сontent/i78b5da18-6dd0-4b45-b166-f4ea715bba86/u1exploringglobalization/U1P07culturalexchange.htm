Media, Technology and Cultural Exchange

Your individual identity can be easy to describe or it may be very complex. Who you are can be connected to your community, your language, your values and beliefs—even to how you dress and the activities you prefer. Often people think of their individual identity as a matter of personal choice. When the influences of globalization in the twenty-first century are examined, you may find that global contact and connections can also influence your personal choices about who you are. Understanding the forces of globalization and how they shape who you are, both individually and collectively, is the focus of this lesson.

The Olympics, World Cup, and student exchanges are some examples of cultural exchanges. How do such exchanges affect the identities of participants? What cultural exchanges have you been a part of (example: linguistic, athletic, musical, educational)? What did you learn from those exchanges?

 

 

 

Universalization - A Canadian Example

The majority of the popular culture we watch, listen to, and read, comes from media transnationals who own many smaller media outlets which create and broadcast their own materials. For example, the transnational Comcast owns NBC, Bravo, SportsNet, History Channel, Style, and 24 other TV and Radio channels.

Transnational media and communications corporations enable a large population of people around the world to gain access to the latest movies, music and pop culture. This universalization of culture through media transnationals has both advantages and concerns. 

Within Canada, we have the Canadian Radio and Television Commission (CRTC) whose purpose is to regulate and promote Canada culture, national identity and cultural sovereignty in the broadcasting media to “ensure that all Canadians have access to a wide variety of high quality Canadian programming” (Broadcasting Act of 1991). The CRTC regulations determine the minimum amount of Canadian Content (CanCon) that Canadian broadcasters must show.

                                                   

Professor William Stanbury does not agree with the CRTC regulations regarding amount of Canadian Content: “The present Canadian content regulations have almost no redeeming social value. They are based on citizenship, not on the substantive content of TV programs or musical recordings. They alter the set of choices available to TV viewers and radio listeners by limiting the availability of foreign programs and musical recordings.” (as quoted in Atlantic Institute for Market Studies, 2008).  

With the rise of Internet streaming to watch TV shows, movies, and videos, and to listen to music and news, there is discussion regarding whether or not CRTC regulations should exist for all forms of media. In fact, the information we receive from the internet is very ‘personalized’ to give us more information that agrees with what we already think. Is this healthy? Does this promote thoughtful conversation?

Hybridization

Mixing traditional forms of music, art, communication and pop culture in new ways is referred to as hybridization. Brian Jungen is an artist with Swiss and DaneZaa First Nations heritage, who uses contemporary consumer materials in his artwork. View a photo-gallery of his work. A Tribe Called Red mixes First Nations singing and drumming with contemporary electronic music. Les Freres Diouf is a musical group that mixes a range of elements from different cultures (reggae, salsa and Quebecois fiddling). Gogol Bordello is an example of a band that combines traditional Romani folk music with punk rock. Mashups, a relatively new music genre are the combination of songs to create new sounds. Visit Moana Maniapoto's website. Hybridization mixes elements of different cultures and creates new cultural products which enable people to interact in different ways.


Diversification

"Culture takes diverse forms across time and space. This diversity is embodied in the uniqueness and plurality of the identities of the groups and societies making up humankind. As a source of exchange, innovation and creativity, cultural diversity is as necessary for humankind as biodiversity is for nature. In this sense, it is the common heritage of humanity and should be recognized and affirmed for the benefit of present and future generations." Source: The Universal Declaration on Cultural Diversity

Expressions of identity hold complex meanings. They may be an expression of individual freedoms and tastes. They may express your collective understandings and your membership in a unique group. They are outward signs to people about who you are. Increased global contact can share who you and your community are to others, but they can also open your unique expressions to change. As a result, expressions of identity can evolve and identities can evolve in a globalizing world. You can embrace a universal identity or resist the erosion of your unique collective identity. Who you are now may not be the same as globalization increases in influence.
