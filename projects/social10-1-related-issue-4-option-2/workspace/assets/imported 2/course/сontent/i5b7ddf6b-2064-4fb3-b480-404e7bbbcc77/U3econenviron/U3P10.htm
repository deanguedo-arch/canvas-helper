Economic Sovereignty

In the past few lessons you have discovered that people, resources, corporations, and nations are connected more than ever before in history. The boundaries between countries are blurred by these connections.

People can be born in one country and live and work in another. They may work for a company based somewhere else, or they may be citizens of more than one nation. A corporation may obtain oil from Canada, refine it in the United States, and sell it to China.

Whose rules apply to the places where people live and work? Are we using rules and ideas that benefit everyone or just those who are already rich? Who decides what the rules are for global economic decisions? Can national governments make decisions? Who is in charge of transnational corporations and huge organizations such as the World Bank, the World Trade Organization, and the International Monetary Fund? What role do individuals play in making choices about their opportunities to prosper? In this lesson you will examine this issue and consider this question: Who should make global economic decisions?

Who should make global economic decisions? 

Think back to Evan, the boy with the pop stand. Now that Evan has finished school, he wants to start his own business. He loves football and he still lives near the stadium, so he wants to start up a small company that makes and sells football souvenirs. 

What rules will Evan have to follow when he starts his own company? Small businesses in Canada must operate with a business licence. Evan has to follow certain municipal, provincial, and Canadian laws to make sure his workers are safe, that they are fairly treated, and that they are all paid at least the minimum wage in his province. He has to follow certain environmental standards to make sure his company is not polluting the atmosphere. He wants to sell football souvenirs, but he has to reach some kind of licensing agreement with the Edmonton Eskimos and other football teams if he wants to reproduce their logos. He must also pay taxes.

These laws were set up so that everyone in Canada will be treated fairly. For example, Evan can’t hire children to work for him. He can’t pay his workers in candy or pennies. His products and his workplace have to meet certain safety guidelines. As well, he can’t profit from the reputation that Canadian football teams have made for themselves without their permission. What if Evan’s company was competing for business with a transnational corporation? The transnational has outsourced production of its souvenirs to another site in the developing world. The country where the factory is located is very poor and doesn’t have many laws that protect workers or the environment. It allows employers to hire children, pollute the atmosphere, and pay people 90 cents an hour. Whose souvenirs are going to be cheaper? Whose will be safer? Which ones would you want to buy?

Not every country has laws about working conditions, environmental pollution, licensing of trademarks, or minimum salaries. Yet because of globalization, products from Evan’s company can be sold in the same stores as his poor-nation competitor's products. Under our current system, who decides what guidelines products have to meet? Who decides what wages people get paid? Who decides what kinds of loans will be made to poor nations and what their terms will be?

Who is in charge of transnational corporations?

Where are transnational corporations based? Who owns shares in these companies? Who makes decisions about how they should run? How does that affect the decisions they make?

If you guessed that most transnationals are based in North America and Europe; that most shareholders are primarily people in the developed world; and that their directors and board members are mostly rich men from the richest nations of the world, you are correct.

However, because of outsourcing, transnational corporations have manufacturing plants, mines, farming operations, and other business ventures in other countries. They have to follow the laws in those countries. They can take advantage of the cost savings of low wages and poor environmental protection while making a large profit for their shareholders.

Rich people in the developed world have a lot of say over what kinds of decisions are made, even though they affect people all over the world. Transnational corporations have a great deal of power—some might say that they have more power than many nations.

Ethyl Corporation won a lawsuit in 1998 against the Canadian government. The Canadian government wanted the corporation to stop selling MMT, a toxic substance used in gasoline and not sold anywhere else in the world. A NAFTA tribunal ruled in favour of the corporation.

Who controls international organizations?

The World Bank is governed by a board of 24 directors, each of whom represents a country or a group of countries. Each member nation sends a representative, usually its Minister of Finance, to the World Bank's yearly meeting.

All 14 of the presidents of the World Bank have been from the United States. Each president is chosen by the president of the United States. Looking at their names, what else can you tell about them?

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) is composed of many nations. There are 184 nations, almost every country in the world, that belong to the IMF. However, all members are not equal. The president is always from Europe. A nation’s voting power directly corresponds to the amount of money it puts into the fund. In 2007, the United States provided nearly 17 percent of the funding and has nearly 17 percent of the votes. Because of the differing amounts of wealth in the world, the poorest 165 nations of the world have control of only 28 percent of the vote.

Who belongs to the World Trade Organization? Who controls it?

 The World Trade Organization is a world body that establishes and enforces rules about international trade at a global level. There are 152 member nations who have all agreed to follow its rulings. The WTO promotes privatization and trade liberalization. It tries to make sure the rules of trade are the same for everyone.

. . . the World Trade Organization (WTO) is the only international organization dealing with the global rules of trade between nations. Its main function is to ensure that trade flows as smoothly, predictably and freely as possible.

—Source: The World Trade Organization © World Trade Organization 2008 (www.wto.org)

How does the WTO make decisions? When one country thinks that another country is breaking the rules, it makes a formal protest. The complaint goes to a court called the Dispute Settlement Body. This court hears the case and makes a ruling. There are dozens of disputes every year, and many of them are never settled, because the countries cannot agree.

The WTO’s headquarters are in Switzerland. In 2008, its director general was from France. Previous directors have been from Thailand, New Zealand, Ireland, Italy, Switzerland, and Great Britain.

Subsidized Rice in Haiti

Haiti is the least-developed country in the Western Hemisphere and one of the poorest nations in the world. Haiti was one of the first parts of the Americas to be colonized by Spain. After the destruction of its Aboriginal population, Haiti was largely inhabited by slaves and freed slaves. Later, it was colonized by France and, still later, it was occupied by the United States. Haiti has a long history of exploitation, and today 80 percent of its people live in extreme poverty. Locate Haiti on a world map (try Google Earth or Google Maps).

In 1986 Haiti grew almost all the rice it needed to feed its own people. Rice is the main food of the country. Rice production gave Haitian farmers an income and provided food for the people.

In the late 1980s, the IMF told Haiti that it must liberalize trade. It had to remove tariffs on rice imports in order to get an IMF loan. Cheap rice immediately arrived from the United States where the industry is highly subsidized—so much so that 40 percent of the profits made by American rice growers comes straight from the U.S. government. Haiti’s poor farmers could not possibly compete, and they stopped growing rice because they could not sell it. After the people of Haiti became completely dependent on American rice, import prices began to rise, and they had no choice but to pay the price or go hungry. Now Haiti imports about 200 000 tons of American rice per year.

The WTO is faced with many challenges. While it tries to establish a level playing field, or a system in which everyone follows the same rules, it has a difficult time getting rich nations such as the United States, Japan, and much of Europe to end subsidies for producers, especially in agriculture. Many of its global conferences have been met with huge protests. Representatives have been unable to agree on issues. How can the World Trade Organization tell poor nations they can’t give money to their farmers in order to produce affordable local food, when rich nations like the United States and many European nations subsidize agricultural production? When a rich country subsidizes farmers, not only is food cheaper for its own people, but producers can sell food to other countries and make a big profit.

Who should have more power—individuals, democratic governments, corporations, or global economic organizations?

Today’s economy is international. Improved transportation and communication technology, transnational corporations, and international organizations make almost everyone on the planet interdependent. The use of resources, the manufacturing of products, and the buying and selling of goods and services make us all interconnected.

The decisions made by a country or a corporation are often based on self-interest. That means the decisions are made to benefit the people who make the decisions. For example, Evan is likely to make decisions about his business that will make money for himself. He may decide he can’t make a profit if he hires Alberta workers to produce his souvenirs. He may have to outsource his production in order to make money for himself. By doing so, his local workers lose their jobs, but his local shoppers can buy things cheaper, and he provides employment for people in the developing world.

Who should tell Evan what to do? Should the government of Alberta or Canada tell Evan he has to hire Canadian workers? Should his government impose a tax on the souvenirs his outsourced company brings in? Should it impose safety standards on his products? Should the government in the country where his outsourcing plant is located protect its own workers’ safety and income levels? Or should an international body like the World Trade Organization or the International Labour Organization make rules to make sure everyone follows the same laws? Or should there be no rules?

Now consider a different perspective. In this section you have investigated the diamond industry. In many parts of the world, the production of diamonds leads to great prosperity for miners, shareholders, and community members. In other parts of the world, conflict diamonds are produced. In these areas, workers are often mistreated and diamond profits go to rebel leaders who promote warfare, violence, and the use of child labour and child soldiers. Who should decide if those activities should be allowed?

Did you know that Canada is one of the leading supporters of ending the trade in conflict diamonds? Canada produces diamonds from its large mining operations in the Far North. Canada has been a strong supporter of the Kimberley Process. In 2002, Canada passed the Export and Import of Rough Diamonds Act. In essence, this act says that no conflict diamonds can be imported into Canada.

As well, the government of the Northwest Territories, where most Canadian diamonds are mined, has a unique process whereby every diamond that is mined, cut, and polished in the NWT is certificated. Some large transnationals are beginning to market Canadian diamonds worldwide with the promise that they are conflict free. You can verify a diamond’s authenticity by visiting the Canadamark website. This site will tell you exactly where your diamond came from!

While Canadian diamonds may not be classified as conflict diamonds, are they ethical in terms of the environment? Are they "conflict free" for the First Nations communities on whose traditional lands the diamonds are being mined? Are other Canadian natural resources and infrastructure "conflict free"?

You also have a say in the global economy. If you don’t like what is happening with Evan’s company, you don’t have to buy his products. You are a consumer and a voter.

You have access to information, so you can find out what is happening in the factories, mines, and industries where transnational corporations do business. You have freedom of speech, so you can speak out against the injustice, exploitation, or violence that a transnational corporation might be promoting.

If you don’t want to support a corporation that abuses its workers, produces unsafe goods, or pollutes the environment, be informed and act accordingly. If you don’t want to support a mining corporation that refuses to promise its diamonds are conflict free, you don’t have to buy one. 

Lesson Summary

In this lesson you explored the great deal of power that wealthy individuals and nations have over economic decision making. You considered the power of nations such as the United States and the countries of Europe. You identified who sits on the boards of transnational corporations and institutions such as the World Bank, the World Trade Organization, and the International Monetary Fund. You discovered that today, very much as in the past, the people with the power in our world are members of the richest nations. Their position influences the decisions they make. You also determined that, as a consumer and future voter in one of the wealthiest nations in the world, you have an important role to play in creating a world where there is sustainable prosperity for all.
