Chapter 11: The Nervous System

Your nervous system is your body’s rapid communication network. It detects change, processes information and coordinates responses throughout your body.

How your nervous system works

Receptors notice changes inside and outside your body. Neurons carry electrical signals, chemical messengers carry information across synapses, and the brain and spinal cord interpret that information. Together, these parts help you sense your surroundings, move your body, maintain stable internal conditions and respond quickly when conditions change.

  • I can trace a signal from a sensory receptor through the central nervous system to an effector.
  • I can explain resting potential, action potentials and communication across a synapse.
  • I can compare somatic and autonomic control, including sympathetic and parasympathetic responses.
  • I can identify major brain regions and connect their structures with their functions.

Nervous communication

The nervous system allows your body to detect a change, make sense of the information, and respond. That might be a movement you choose, such as reaching for a glass, or an adjustment you do not consciously direct, such as a change in heart rate. In both cases, communication between cells is essential.

Nervous and endocrine systems work together

Your internal and external environments do not stay the same. The temperature around you changes. The amount of water in your body changes. Your muscles may suddenly need to work harder. means maintaining relatively stable internal conditions despite these changes.

The nervous system works with the endocrine system to coordinate responses. These systems differ in how their messages are delivered. The nervous system uses electrochemical messages—messages involving electrical changes and chemical signals—carried along neural pathways. The endocrine system uses hormones, chemical messengers carried through the bloodstream. You will focus on the nervous system in this chapter and return to hormonal communication in the endocrine material.

A useful starting sequence is sensory input → integration → motor output. Sensory input is information about a change. Integration means processing that information and organizing an appropriate . Motor output carries instructions to structures that respond. These responding structures are called effectors; muscles and glands are important examples.

A first look at the two main divisions

The () consists of the brain and spinal cord. The () consists of nerves that connect the CNS with the rest of the body. At this point, remember the distinction between the processing centres and the pathways linking them to receptors and effectors. You will study their subdivisions later.

Two main types of cells

A is the basic functional unit of the nervous system. It is specialized to receive information, conduct a nerve impulse and pass information to another cell. Glial cells support neurons structurally and nutritionally. They also help remove wastes and protect against infection.

Do not use neuron and nerve as interchangeable terms. A neuron is one cell. A nerve is a larger structure containing many nerve fibres organized into bundles and surrounded by protective connective tissue. Think of a cable containing many individual fibres. That comparison helps you see why a nerve can carry information along many pathways.

Myelin and Schwann cells

Many axons are surrounded by a fatty insulating layer called the sheath. Schwann cells are glial cells that wrap around an to form myelin. The small gaps between adjacent myelin segments are the nodes of Ranvier.

Myelin increases the rate of nerve impulse conduction. In a myelinated axon, the —the electrical nerve impulse—is regenerated at successive nodes. This is called . It is often described as “jumping” from node to node. The axon itself is still continuous beneath the myelin; the gap is in the insulating sheath.

In an unmyelinated axon, neighbouring regions of membrane take part in regenerating the impulse along its length. The comparison is therefore between continuous regeneration along the membrane and regeneration at separated nodes. Both axons carry information along their length; myelin changes the way that conduction occurs.

What makes a nerve impulse electrochemical?

A nerve impulse is an electrochemical event. The electrical component is a change in voltage across the membrane. The chemical component involves charged particles called ions, including sodium ions (Na⁺) and potassium ions (K⁺). Movement of these ions across the membrane changes the voltage.

A neural impulse is therefore not identical to electric current in a metal wire. It depends on ion movement across the membrane. This distinction will help you answer the comparison question in the Section 11.1 Review.

Nerve impulse conduction can be organized into four stages: polarized/resting state, , and . Begin with the resting state. You cannot explain the change during an unless you first understand the conditions before it begins.

A resting neuron has a membrane potential

A membrane potential is a voltage difference across the cell membrane. In a resting , the inside is slightly negative relative to the outside. A common example is approximately −70 mV, called the . The negative sign describes the inside relative to the outside; it does not mean the cell contains no positive ions.

The word resting does not mean that the neuron is using no energy or that all membrane movement has stopped. It means the neuron is not currently conducting the under discussion. The membrane is maintained in a condition from which an impulse can be generated.

Ion concentrations, leakage and the sodium-potassium pump

In the resting example, there is more K⁺ inside the and more Na⁺ outside. The membrane does not allow all substances to move through equally. Potassium ions can move outward through leakage channels more readily than sodium ions move inward. Large negatively charged molecules within the cell also contribute to the distribution of charge described here.

The sodium-potassium exchange pump uses energy from ATP. For every three Na⁺ moved out, it moves two K⁺ in. This transport helps maintain the concentration differences across the membrane. Remember both the direction and the unequal numbers; reversing them reverses the explanation.

There are two different kinds of movement here. Leakage through channels is passive movement down a gradient. Pumping is active transport that requires ATP. The resting condition depends on the combined effects of ion distribution, and active transport—not on the idea that an inactive neuron has no ion movement.

Depolarization and threshold

means that the membrane potential becomes less negative than the resting value. In the example used here, the voltage may move from −70 mV toward −55 mV. The latter value is the approximate potential used here.

A that does not reach threshold does not trigger a full . If threshold is reached, voltage-gated sodium channels open and Na⁺ moves into the . The movement of positive charge into the cell causes a rapid increase in membrane potential. In this example, the voltage rises to approximately +40 mV.

Example peaks may be about +35 mV or +40 mV; the exact numbers can vary among neurons. Focus on what the sign and direction of the voltage change mean rather than treating one example value as universal.

Repolarization and hyperpolarization

After the reaches its peak, sodium entry is curtailed and voltage-gated potassium channels allow K⁺ to move out. Positive charge leaves the cell, so the membrane potential becomes negative again. This return toward the resting voltage is .

Potassium channels do not close immediately. Continued outward movement can briefly make the membrane more negative than the resting value. This is . In the example graph, the membrane overshoots toward approximately −90 mV before returning toward the resting level.

The sodium-potassium pump continues to help maintain the ion gradients. Keep the pump’s long-term maintenance role distinct from the rapid channel movements that explain the rising and falling portions of an action potential.

Voltage is not concentration. Sodium- helps end the rising phase; potassium movement produces repolarization. A reversal of membrane voltage is not a reversal of the overall Na⁺ and K⁺ concentration gradients.

The refractory period

Immediately after firing, the recently active membrane cannot simply produce another identical impulse at once. This recovery interval is the . Sodium permeability cannot immediately repeat the same change.

This helps explain normal forward . When an active region brings the next region to , the region just behind it has recently fired and is refractory. It does not immediately regenerate a new impulse in the backward direction. The sequence therefore proceeds toward the terminals.

Two parts of recovery. During the a second cannot occur at that membrane region. During the , a stronger can trigger one. Recovery is not simply an on/off event at −70 mV.

All-or-none does not mean “all stimuli feel the same”

An individual is an all-or-none event. Once is reached, a stronger does not produce a taller version of that same action potential. The either generates the action potential or it does not.

Frequency of nerve impulses is an important way of representing stimulus intensity: more impulses can occur in the same period of time. More neurons may also become active. These are distinct ideas: a higher firing frequency means more impulses from a neuron per unit time; recruitment means more neurons participating. Neither requires each individual action potential to become larger.

When interpreting an experiment, pay attention to whether you are observing one neuron and one muscle fibre, or many neurons and fibres. Excitatory and inhibitory effects

An excitatory effect makes a postsynaptic more likely. In the example here, sodium enters the receiving cell. The membrane becomes slightly less negative, moving toward . A small excitatory effect is not automatically a full action potential; the combined change must be sufficient to reach threshold.

An inhibitory effect makes an action potential less likely. In the examples here, K⁺ leaves or Cl⁻ enters, making the membrane potential more negative. In the chapter’s hyperpolarizing example, this moves the membrane away from threshold.

Keep the comparison clear: excitatory input moves membrane potential toward threshold; inhibitory input moves it away in the hyperpolarizing example. Compare the voltage of the membrane with the threshold value.

Summation combines incoming effects

A may receive input from many presynaptic neurons. Some inputs are excitatory and others are inhibitory. Summation is the combined effect of these inputs on whether the postsynaptic neuron reaches .

In spatial summation, inputs from different locations act together. In temporal summation, repeated inputs arrive close together in time. In both cases, the question is whether the net effect is enough to generate an . Do not confuse summation of incoming effects with making the resulting action potential taller.

The includes the somatic system and the autonomic system. The somatic system connects sensory information with control of skeletal muscles. It is largely under , including actions you consciously choose, such as moving your hand. Remember that skeletal muscles also participate in reflexes.

Integration and processing

The consists of the brain and spinal cord. It is the site of neural integration and processing. Damage to a particular CNS region can therefore affect movement, sensation, behaviour or the maintenance of , depending on the region and pathways involved.

Two tissue names appear repeatedly: grey matter and white matter. Grey matter contains many cell bodies, dendrites and unmyelinated portions of axons. White matter contains many myelinated axons grouped into tracts.

In the cerebral regions illustrated here, grey matter forms the outer cortex and white matter lies deeper. In the spinal cord cross-section, the arrangement is different: a central H-shaped or butterfly-shaped region of grey matter is surrounded by white matter. Do not assume the same outside–inside arrangement applies everywhere.

The spinal cord is a communication link and a reflex centre

The spinal cord provides a vital link between the brain and the . Sensory information can travel toward the brain, and motor information can travel from the brain toward effectors. The spinal cord also participates in circuits, allowing rapid processing of incoming and outgoing information.

The delicate nervous tissue is protected by the vertebral column, the meninges and cerebrospinal fluid. A vertebra is a bone in the spinal column; the spinal cord is nervous tissue within the protected canal. These are different structures and should not be labelled as if they were the same thing.

A higher injury can affect communication with more body regions below it than a lower injury. You do not need to memorize the individual spinal segments for this chapter. You do need to explain why interrupting a communication pathway can affect function below the interruption.

Protecting the brain and spinal cord

The meninges are three tissue layers surrounding the . The brain is also enclosed by the skull. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulates in spaces associated with the brain and spinal cord and provides cushioning. It acts as a shock absorber and a transport medium.

The three layers are the dura mater, arachnoid and pia mater. CSF is found between the arachnoid and pia mater as well as in the ventricular spaces. Use the diagram to distinguish outer bone, covering membranes, fluid and nervous tissue rather than memorizing a list without spatial relationships.

The blood-brain barrier is a selective boundary

The blood-brain barrier involves tightly joined cells in brain capillary walls that limit which substances can pass from circulating blood into tissue. The barrier is selective: the brain still requires a supply of oxygen and nutrients.

Keep this idea distinct from cushioning by CSF. A membrane covering, a fluid cushion and a selective barrier to exchange do not perform the same job. This distinction is important for the questions about CNS protection and cerebrospinal-fluid sampling.

The brain is involved in intelligence, consciousness, emotion and the regulation of body functions. Some activity is consciously directed; much of it is not. The brain can be organized into forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain so you can connect structures with functions.

Connect each principal structure with its function

The cerebellum helps coordinate movement, balance and fine motor control. It uses information from receptors in muscles and joints. Being able to activate a muscle is not the same as being able to coordinate a smooth, precise movement; this is why cerebellar function is important.

The medulla oblongata is at the base of the brainstem, where the brain connects with the spinal cord. It contains centres involved in involuntary functions such as heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, swallowing and coughing. The pons acts as a relay between brain regions; it is also associated with sleep and arousal.

The midbrain relays information between the hindbrain and forebrain and is associated with visual and auditory information and eye movement. Learn its general position and relay role rather than merging it with the entire brainstem.

The thalamus is a major relay station involved in connections among brain regions and in sensory processing. The lies below it and helps regulate internal conditions and drives, including body temperature, hunger, thirst, heart rate and blood pressure. The hypothalamus links nervous-system regulation with the endocrine system through its relationship with the gland.

The corpus callosum is a tract of fibres connecting the left and right cerebral hemispheres. It allows communication between them. The spinal cord provides communication between the brain and the body and participates in reflexes.

The cerebrum and cerebral cortex

The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain. It is divided into right and left cerebral hemispheres. Its outer surface is the cerebral cortex, composed of grey matter. The folds increase the amount of cortical surface that fits within the skull.

The forebrain includes the cerebrum, but the names are not synonyms. The thalamus and also belong to the forebrain. Keep the broader region distinct from one structure within it.

Each cerebral hemisphere has four principal lobes. The frontal lobe is associated with conscious thought, decision making, intelligence, memory, personality and voluntary movement. The parietal lobe processes somatosensory information such as touch and information about body position. The temporal lobe is strongly associated with hearing; it is also involved in language understanding and retrieval of visual and verbal memories. The occipital lobe processes visual information.

Motor and sensory areas

The primary motor area is in the frontal lobe and the primary somatosensory area is in the parietal lobe. These areas do not allocate equal amounts of cortex to every body part. The motor illustration emphasizes body parts requiring detailed control, while the sensory discussion emphasizes differences in the amount of incoming sensory information.

Many motor pathways cross in the brainstem. This is why a question about damage to the right motor cortex asks you to consider effects on the left side of the body. Be careful to identify which pathway the question describes rather than treating “opposite side” as a rule for every structure in the nervous system.

Hemispheres interact

Some functions show lateralization, but analytical and creative tasks involve interacting networks in both hemispheres. There is considerable overlap and interaction: cognitive functions involve components on both sides.

The central point is communication and cooperation between hemispheres. The corpus callosum carries information between them. Do not turn the broad comparison into a label for a student’s learning ability or assume that a complex activity belongs entirely to one side.
