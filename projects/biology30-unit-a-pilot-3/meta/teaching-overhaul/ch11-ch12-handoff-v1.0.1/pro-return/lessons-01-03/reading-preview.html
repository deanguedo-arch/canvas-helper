Nervous communication

A change outside can require a response inside

Step into cold air and your skin begins to cool. The air has changed, but the cells inside your body still need conditions in which they can function. How does information about the cooling reach a place where a useful response can be organized?

The nervous system provides a way to detect a change, process information about it and direct a response. A stimulus is the change being detected. A response is what the body does after that change. Cooling at the skin is a stimulus; narrowing some blood vessels near the skin is a response. Keeping those two ideas separate helps us follow the pathway rather than treating everything that happens as one event.

The purpose of many such adjustments is homeostasis: maintaining relatively stable internal conditions. Stable does not mean that a value can never change. It means that regulation keeps conditions within a workable range despite disturbances. Reducing heat loss in a cold environment can help protect internal temperature. The outside air remains cold; the response changes what happens in the body.

Other nervous-system responses allow you to act on your surroundings. You can hear an instruction, decide what to do and move a hand. Some responses involve conscious decisions, while others occur without waiting for one. We will use the same detection-to-response framework to understand both.

Follow the information before naming every part

A receptor detects a particular kind of change. A temperature-sensitive receptor responds to temperature; a receptor involved in vision responds to light. The receptor begins the input side of the pathway. It is not automatically the structure that carries out the response.

Sensory input carries information about the detected change toward the central nervous system. The central nervous system, or CNS, consists of the brain and spinal cord. These structures process information and organize responses. This processing is called integration. It can involve combining incoming information with other information, rather than simply passing a message along unchanged.

Motor output carries instructions from the CNS toward an effector. An effector is a cell, tissue or organ that carries out the response. A muscle can contract; a gland can release a secretion. A motor message is therefore an instruction on the output side, while the response is the resulting change in activity.

A map of the system

The CNS is connected with the rest of the body by the peripheral nervous system, or PNS. Peripheral nerves carry information between the CNS and receptors or effectors. Location gives us the first distinction: brain and spinal cord are central; the nerves connecting them with the body are peripheral.

Look first at the two large branches of the diagram. Find the CNS branch and name its two structures. Then follow the PNS branch to sensory input and motor output. Finally, follow the motor branch down to the kinds of effectors it controls.

The lower branches classify motor output by its target. Somatic motor pathways control skeletal muscles, including muscles that move the limbs. Autonomic pathways regulate smooth muscle, heart muscle and glands. The broader somatic system also includes sensory information from the body; the lower part of this drawing concentrates on its motor output.

A planned hand movement uses somatic motor output. A skeletal-muscle reflex also uses somatic motor output, even though you did not choose it consciously. This is why the target tissue is a more reliable starting point than treating “somatic” as a synonym for “every voluntary action.”

The autonomic system has sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions. Sympathetic activity helps prepare the body for an acute challenge, for example by increasing heart activity while reducing digestive activity. Parasympathetic activity supports functions such as digestion and can slow the heart. Their effects are specific to the organ, so neither division simply turns the whole body on or off.

For now, use this as an organization map. You can identify each division and give its broad function. We will later examine the organ responses in detail.

Work through a temperature response

Consider a simplified cold-environment example. Cooling at the skin is detected, information reaches the CNS, and motor output contributes to narrowing some blood vessels near the surface. How does that sequence help maintain homeostasis?

Start with the disturbance: the surroundings are colder and the skin cools. Temperature-sensitive receptors provide information about that change. Sensory pathways carry the information inward. The CNS processes temperature-related information and coordinates a response. Output to muscles in blood-vessel walls changes their activity so that some surface vessels narrow. Less heat is then lost through those vessels near the surface, helping conserve heat inside the body.

Notice the different jobs. The receptor detects cooling; it does not narrow the vessel. The sensory pathway brings information inward; it does not carry the motor instruction outward. The vessel-wall muscle is an effector because its contraction produces part of the response. The connection with homeostasis is the effect on heat loss, not merely the fact that a nerve carried a signal.

This is one simplified part of temperature regulation. It does not describe every response to cold or guarantee that internal temperature never changes. Its purpose is to show how detection, communication and a specific effector response can work together.

The cells that make communication possible

A neuron is a cell specialized to receive, process and pass information. Neural signalling involves electrical changes within cells and chemical communication between many cells. That is why it is often described as electrochemical.

A nerve contains many nerve fibres bundled together within protective tissue. The long conducting fibres in this bundle are axons, together with their coverings. A neuron is one cell; a nerve is a larger structure containing many fibres. A cable with many individual strands is a useful comparison for the bundle, provided you remember that a nerve signal is produced by living cells rather than by the same process as current in a metal wire.

Neurons depend on glial cells. These cells support and nourish neurons, help protect nervous tissue and perform other supporting jobs. The distinction is between signalling cells and the cells that help maintain the conditions in which signalling can occur. It does not mean that support is optional.

The nervous system also works with the endocrine system. Endocrine glands release hormones, chemical messengers carried through the bloodstream to responsive tissues. Nervous and endocrine signals can contribute to the same regulated condition, but their routes differ. A chemical message crossing a small junction between nerve cells and a hormone carried in the blood are not the same journey.

Bring the pathway together

The nervous system helps the body respond because detection, information transfer, integration and effector action are connected. A complete explanation names what changes at each stage and how that stage contributes to the next. For a homeostatic example, it also explains how the response affects the condition being regulated.

We have followed messages through a system. Next, look closely at one neuron. Its branches, long conducting region and transmitting ends help explain how a single cell can contribute to this larger pathway.

Neurons and myelin

One cell can communicate over a long distance

A command that helps move a foot may need to travel a long way from the spinal cord to the muscle. How can a living cell receive information at one region and pass it on at another, distant region?

A neuron has specialized structures for receiving, conducting and transmitting information. We will start with a typical multipolar neuron, which has many branches near its cell body. Other neurons have different shapes, so learn what each structure contributes rather than assuming every neuron looks exactly like this one.

Look at the branching region on the left of the drawing. Follow the cell into the long extension and continue to the smaller branches at the far right. The arrow below the cell shows the usual direction of information flow in this example.

Connect each structure with its job

Dendrites are branching extensions that usually receive information. Their many branches provide a large surface over which the cell can receive inputs. Information arriving at these regions can influence what happens nearer the cell body.

The cell body, also called the soma, contains the nucleus and most of the cell's organelles. It carries out the cell's life processes and helps process incoming information. A neuron remains a living cell even when we draw it as part of a signalling pathway.

The axon is the extension that conducts impulses toward the transmitting end. Some axons extend more than a metre. A long axon allows one cell to carry information to a distant region instead of requiring a new cell at every small step along that distance.

The axon hillock is the region where the cell body narrows toward the axon. In this drawing, the nearby starting region connects the combined effects of incoming information with whether an impulse begins in the axon. We will return to that decision when we study how inputs combine.

An action potential is a brief, all-or-none change in the voltage across the axon membrane. Voltage here compares the electrical state inside the cell with the state outside. An active region can bring the neighbouring membrane to the point where another action potential begins. Repeated regeneration carries the signal along the axon. We will explain the ions and membrane channels that make this possible after we have established the pathway.

At the far end, axon terminals communicate with another neuron or an effector. At a chemical synapse, the terminal releases a chemical messenger that affects the receiving cell. The small gap between the cells is the synaptic cleft. The full synapse includes the sending region, gap and receiving region; it is more than an empty space.

Return to the figure. Find a dendrite, the cell body, the continuous axon and an axon terminal. For each, say what would be missing from the pathway if that structure could not perform its job. This turns a label into an explanation of structure and function.

Myelin changes how the signal travels

Many axons have a fatty insulating covering called the myelin sheath. The covering reduces current loss through the wrapped membrane and allows electrical change to spread rapidly between exposed regions. The gaps between myelin segments are nodes of Ranvier.

Read the two panels below as a comparison of the conducting pattern. In the left panel, follow successive neighbouring regions of an unmyelinated axon. In the right panel, follow the yellow axon underneath the blue coverings, then locate the exposed nodes between those coverings.

In the unmyelinated axon, successive neighbouring membrane regions regenerate the impulse. In the myelinated axon, action potentials are regenerated at the nodes, while the electrical change spreads beneath the myelin between them. This is saltatory conduction, often described as a signal “jumping” from node to node.

The jumping description refers to where the action potentials are regenerated. The axon has not been cut into pieces, and the signal does not cross empty space from one disconnected cell fragment to another. The yellow axon continues through each blue myelin segment in the drawing.

Under comparable conditions, this pattern allows a myelinated axon to conduct faster than an unmyelinated one. Faster signalling concerns the time needed to carry information along the axon. It does not mean that the myelin itself is an action potential or that adding more myelin makes each individual action potential taller.

Which cells make the covering?

Myelin is made by glial cells. In the PNS, Schwann cells form the myelin around axon segments. In the CNS, oligodendrocytes make myelin. The wrapped cells shown in the conduction comparison are a peripheral example. Do not transfer the Schwann-cell label to every myelinated axon in the brain.

Many myelinated axons together contribute to the pale appearance of white matter. Grey matter contains many cell bodies, dendrites, synapses and unmyelinated parts of axons. We will use brain and spinal-cord cross-sections later to locate these tissues. The important point here is that myelin is a covering on axons, not a name for an entirely different kind of cell that performs every nervous-system job.

Damage to myelin can slow or disrupt signalling. In multiple sclerosis, damage to CNS myelin can affect communication along involved pathways. The consequences depend on which pathways are affected, so problems with sensation, movement or coordination can differ. Use the structure-function connection rather than treating the name of a disorder as a complete explanation of every symptom.

Work through two different failures

Suppose a simplified pathway has one neuron passing information to a second cell. In the intact pathway, an impulse travels along the axon, reaches a terminal and is followed by a chemical message to the receiving cell.

Now compare two hypothetical changes:

ChangeWhat the record says still works
Myelin is damaged along part of the axonThe axon is continuous, but conduction along that region is slower or disrupted
Terminal release failsThe impulse reaches the terminal, but the chemical message is not released there

The first change concerns conduction along the axon. The covering no longer supports the normal fast pattern in that region. If an impulse is delayed or fails to continue, the message cannot reach the far end normally.

The second change is at the handover to another cell. The record specifically says the impulse reaches the terminal, so failure to reach that point is not the explanation. The missing event is chemical release. The receiving cell can miss the message even though conduction along the first axon occurred.

Both changes can disrupt the overall response, but they interrupt different jobs. Locate the changed structure, identify its normal function, then connect the missing function with the observed result. Avoid beginning with a vague conclusion such as “the neuron does not work.”

Pathways and reflexes

Why can movement begin before awareness?

If a hand contacts something painfully sharp, withdrawal can begin before you consciously recognize what happened. Yet you can also see an obstacle, decide to avoid it and choose a movement. Both responses use neurons, but they do not require the same processing sequence.

To compare them, follow information from the receptor to the effector. Ask where it enters the CNS, what processing is required and how instructions leave. Speed alone will not tell you which pathway is involved.

Three jobs in a neural pathway

A sensory neuron, also called an afferent neuron, carries information from a sensory receptor toward the CNS. The receptor might detect a change at the skin or provide information involved in a sense such as vision. Sensory describes the input job.

An interneuron operates within the CNS. It can connect and process information between other neurons. In a withdrawal circuit, it can link incoming sensory information with a motor neuron. In more complex pathways, many interneurons participate in processing.

A motor neuron, also called an efferent neuron, carries information from the CNS toward an effector. The effector produces the response. If the effector is a skeletal muscle, its contraction produces movement.

StructureDirection or locationJob
Sensory receptorAt the point of detectionDetects a particular stimulus
Sensory neuronToward the CNSCarries sensory information inward
InterneuronWithin the CNSConnects and processes information
Motor neuronFrom the CNS toward an effectorCarries output information
EffectorAt the responding tissueCarries out the response

These are functional descriptions. Words such as multipolar, bipolar and unipolar describe a neuron's shape and branches. A question about what a neuron does is different from a question about its appearance. Trace its connections and direction before assigning a functional role.

Work through a deliberately directed movement

A driver sees a cat move onto the road and presses the brake. Where do sensory input, integration and motor output occur?

Start with the information that matters: light from the scene is detected by the visual system. Sensory information reaches the CNS. Processing in the brain allows the driver to recognize the situation and organize a response. Motor pathways then carry instructions toward the skeletal muscles used to move the foot. Muscle contraction presses the brake.

The eyes do not send a motor command directly to the foot. Their contribution is sensory information. The foot muscle does not decide that the cat is an obstacle. It is an effector carrying out motor output. The processing between these stages is essential to explaining a response selected from visual information.

This is a simplified account of a complex movement. Its purpose is to keep the direction and jobs clear. We have not described every intervening neuron or all the feedback needed for precise driving.

A withdrawal reflex uses a rapid circuit

A reflex is a rapid, involuntary response to a stimulus. A reflex arc is the neural pathway that produces it. In the withdrawal example below, the spinal circuit can initiate output without waiting for the conscious recognition required in the driver example.

Begin at the skin on the left. Follow the sensory route into the spinal cord, then follow the interneuron and motor route to the muscle on the right. After tracing that circuit, look for the separate path carrying information toward the brain.

A receptor detects the potentially harmful stimulus. The sensory neuron carries information into the spinal cord. An interneuron in this circuit passes information to a motor neuron. Motor output reaches the muscle, which contracts and moves the hand away. The response reduces continued contact with the harmful stimulus.

Information also travels toward the brain. That ascending communication contributes to conscious awareness of the stimulus. The spinal route does not have to wait for that awareness before beginning the withdrawal response. Saying that withdrawal can start before conscious pain does not mean that no information reaches the brain.

The motor neuron's cell body is inside the spinal cord in this drawing; its axon extends outward toward the muscle. The sensory pathway enters the cord. Use those locations to distinguish central processing from the peripheral connections. The picture is a schematic, not a scale drawing or a complete map of every branch.

Not every reflex uses the exact same circuit. Some do not require an interneuron between the sensory and motor neurons. Other reflexes use different CNS regions. The five-component withdrawal route is the model being explained here, not a claim that all involuntary activity follows these exact five structures.

Use intermediate evidence to reason about a pathway

A final observation such as “the muscle did not contract” could have several causes. To locate an interruption, look for evidence of where signalling still occurs.

For example, suppose a hypothetical record shows that the receptor detects the stimulus and the sensory signal enters the spinal cord normally, but no motor signal leaves toward the muscle. This evidence rules out failure of detection or the incoming sensory route in the stated model. The interruption must be investigated at the processing-to-output part of the pathway. It does not by itself distinguish every possible cell or junction there.

That last limitation matters. A useful explanation says what the evidence supports and what it leaves unresolved. It should not turn a simple pathway exercise into a certain diagnosis.

Connect the pathway back to the cell

A pathway explanation needs more than an ordered list of names. It identifies what each structure does, how information travels between stages and why the response follows. It also distinguishes a circuit that can act locally from a response that depends on further brain processing.

So far, an impulse has been the signal carried by a neuron. We will now look at the axon membrane itself. To understand how a neuron fires, first establish what “resting” means and why the inside and outside of the membrane have different electrical potentials.
