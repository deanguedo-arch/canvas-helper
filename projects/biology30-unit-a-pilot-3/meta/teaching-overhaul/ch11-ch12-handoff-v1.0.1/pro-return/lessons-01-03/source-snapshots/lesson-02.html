Follow the parts of one neuron

A typical has dendrites, a , an and axon terminals. The cell body, also called the soma, contains the nucleus and other organelles and carries out the cell’s life processes.

Dendrites are short, branching extensions that receive information and carry it toward the cell body. Their branches provide a large receiving surface. The cell body processes incoming information. The axon carries nerve impulses away from the cell body.

Myelin and Schwann cells

Many axons have a fatty insulating covering called the sheath. Schwann cells form myelin around axons in the PNS. The gaps between myelin segments are nodes of Ranvier.

Myelin speeds up nerve impulses. In a myelinated axon, the is regenerated at each node. This is , often described as “jumping” from node to node. The axon remains continuous underneath the myelin.

In an unmyelinated axon, each neighbouring section of membrane produces the next part of the impulse. In a myelinated axon, the impulse is regenerated at the nodes instead.

Why myelin damage matters

In multiple sclerosis (MS), damage to disrupts nerve signals. Symptoms can include numbness, tingling, muscle spasms, and problems with balance or coordination. Other possible symptoms include blurred vision, weakness, fatigue and slurred speech.

Key connection: damaged myelin → disrupted nerve signals → changes in sensation or movement. The effects depend on which pathways are affected.
