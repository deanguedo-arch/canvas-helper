The camera receives infrared energy and converts its detection into a visible display. Human photoreceptors then receive visible light from the display, so the eye's optics, retinal conversion and visual pathways still need to function. The camera has extended what information can reach the eyes without changing human photoreceptors into infrared detectors.

In the cochlear-implant example, a microphone detects sound and processing produces a pattern of electrical stimulation for remaining auditory nerve fibres. This bypasses some damaged hair-cell function rather than repairing the receptors. The remaining auditory pathway and the person's ability to learn and interpret the stimulation pattern still matter.

In both cases, producing an output signal is only one stage. A useful sensory experience also requires the output to contain information the remaining pathway can process. Evidence of a visible screen change or a nerve response does not, by itself, demonstrate accurate object recognition, speech understanding or restoration of a complete natural sense. A fair evaluation would state the intended task and examine what the person can distinguish under specified conditions.

If you said that both devices replace the receptors, locate their points of entry again: the display uses the user's functioning photoreceptors, while the implant enters after some receptor function. If you promised complete restoration, identify the missing evidence about interpretation.
