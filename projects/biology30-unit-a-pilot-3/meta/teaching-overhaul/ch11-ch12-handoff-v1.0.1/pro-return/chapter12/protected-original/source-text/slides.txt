SLIDE 3
Sensation
Sensation
 occurs when neural impulses generated by the stimulation of sensory receptors arrive at the cerebral cortex. 
Perception
 occurs in the brain as it 
makes sense of the received input.  
Example:
When a ray of sunlight hits your skin, sensory receptors send a message to the brain, which receives and processes this information, causing you to feel warmth.

* 
each person’s unique perception of sensation 
  depends on how their cerebral cortex interprets 
  this sensory information

SLIDE 4
Sensory Adaptation
Do you constantly feel your clothes against your skin? Sometimes the brain can actually filter out redundant sensory information so as to prevent over-stimulation. This is known as 
sensory adaptation
.
 
Another example is when you walk into a coffee shop and smell the fresh smell of coffee, but after a while you are not aware of it. Or when you no longer hear/notice a background noise. 

When your sensory receptors detect a 
significant change in external or internal 
conditions, the body readjusts.

SLIDE 5
Sensory Receptors
Four general categories of receptors:
Photoreceptors
	Stimulated by light (e.g. rods & cones in the 
retina of our eyes)

Chemoreceptors
	Stimulated by chemicals (e.g. taste buds on 
our tongue)

Mechanoreceptors
	Stimulated by pressure (e.g. hair cells of the 
inner ear)

Thermoreceptors 
	Stimulated by heat/cold (e.g. nerve cells in 
our skin)

SLIDE 9
Olfaction/Smell (Chemoreception)
Humans can distinguish between over 10,000 different odours. Each of these odour particles is thought to fit like a lock & key into specific chemoreceptors which line the nasal cavity, known as 
olfactory cells
.
 
When stimulated, olfactory cells transmit signals to the 
olfactory bulb 
in the cerebrum of  the brain, leading to the sensation of smell
The olfactory bulb of dogs is more than twice the size of humans, explaining their heightened sense of smell

SLIDE 13
Anatomy of the Eye & Vision
Light enters the eye through the 
cornea, 
which is the clear part of the 
sclera
 in front of the eye.
 
The 
iris
, is the colored part of the eye and is a muscle that contracts and relaxes to control how much light actually enters the eye through the 
pupil
, the hole in the iris. 
Ciliary muscles 
hold the 
lens
 in place and control its shape, allowing it to focus on objects closer or further away and focus the incoming light onto the 
retina
. 
(
this process is called 
accommodation
)
The focused light strikes the 
retina
, which is the inner layer of the back of the eye. 
The retina is composed of 
rods 
(sensitive to light intensity) and 
cones
 
(sensitive to wavelengths of colour)
Photoreceptors (rods and cones) relay sensory impulses to the occipital lobe of the brain via the 
optic nerve

SLIDE 14
Anatomy of the Eye & Vision
The 
aqueous humour
 supplies the cornea with nutrients, while the 
vitreous humour
 maintains the shape of the eye and permits light transmission to the retina.
The 
sclera
 protects the eye and helps to maintain its shape. 
The 
choroid layer
 contains blood vessels which nourish the retina.
The 
fovea centralis
 is a location in the retina that contains a high concentration of cones, while the 
blind spot
 (the point at which the optic nerve connects to the retina) contains neither rods nor cones. This is why when light falls on the blind spot, no image is generated.

SLIDE 16
Rods vs. Cones
The retina is composed of two types of light-sensitive photoreceptors: 
rods and cones
.
Rods respond to low-intensity light to detect black and white. 
We rely on rods during low light or nighttime vision
Cones respond to high-intensity light to identify colour.
We rely on cones to general high visual detail when there is an abundance of light

Anatomy of the 
Outer/Middle Ear
Sound waves are directed by 
pinna (aur
icle)
 
to the 
auditory canal
, where they strike the 
tympanum
 
(
eardrum
), 
generating vibrations within the middle ear.
When sound waves push against the tympanum, its vibrations are 
passed on

ossicles 
Vibrations are concentrated on the 
oval window
, causing changes in pressure within the organs of the inner ear

Anatomy of the 
Inner Ear
Changes in pressure stimulate the movement of hair cells (collectively known as the 
organ of Corti
) 
within the coiled
 
cochlea
, generating an action potential within the 
auditory nerve. 
This action potential is carried to the temporal lobes by the auditory nerve, where sound information is processed and interpreted.

Extra: 
Balance Disorders
Balance disorders commonly result from damage to the semicircular canals, including...

leakage of inner ear fluid after head injury or dramatic changes in air pressure, causing dizziness and nausea
failure of canals to flex properly in response to a change in head position, causing dizziness (vertigo) 
an increase in fluid volume, leading to a ringing or buzzing and a feeling of fullness in the ear

Extra: 
Hearing Loss
Hearing loss may be caused by a number of factors, including:

Birth defects 
	Improper development of any of the organs required for 
hearing can lead to deafness; often hereditary, but can sometimes be treated with cochlear implants

Ear infections
	Swelling of the middle ear can prevent sound waves from 
reaching the cochlea; in some cases, structures of the middle ear can become permanently damaged

Noise
	Loud noises can damage hair cells within the cochlea or 
even cause the tympanic membrane to rupture
