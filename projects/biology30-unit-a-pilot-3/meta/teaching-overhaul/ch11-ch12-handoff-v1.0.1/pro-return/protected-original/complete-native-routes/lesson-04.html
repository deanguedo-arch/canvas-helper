What makes a nerve impulse electrochemical?

A nerve impulse is electrochemical. Charged particles called ions, including sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺), move across the membrane. Their movement changes the voltage across it.

Unlike current in a metal wire, a nerve impulse depends on ions moving across the membrane.

Study four stages: resting state, , and . Start with the resting neuron before following the changes during an impulse.

1. Resting state

Membrane potential is the voltage difference across a cell membrane. A resting is slightly negative inside compared with outside. A typical is −70 mV. The minus sign tells you how the inside compares with the outside.

“Resting” means the neuron is not producing an . Ions still move, and the cell still uses energy to maintain its conditions.

Ion concentrations, leakage and the sodium-potassium pump

There is more K⁺ inside the and more Na⁺ outside. At rest, K⁺ moves out through leak channels more easily than Na⁺ moves in. Large negatively charged molecules remain inside the cell.

The sodium-potassium pump uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ moved in. This maintains the concentration differences across the membrane.

Channels and pumps do different jobs. Leakage through channels is passive. The pump uses ATP for active transport. Together, ion concentrations and the membrane’s help maintain the resting potential.
