Pathways & reflexes

A phone starts sliding off a desk and you choose to reach for it. A dangerously hot object touches your hand and your arm withdraws before you decide to pull away. Both responses move skeletal muscle. Are the neural pathways the same?

Learning goal

I can trace information from a receptor to an effector and explain how a spinal withdrawal reflex differs from a deliberately selected sensory-motor response.

Before you begin

Remember the direction rule from Lesson 1:

  • sensory / afferent information travels toward the CNS;
  • motor / efferent information travels away from the CNS toward an effector.

And remember from Lesson 2: a neuron is one cell, while nerves contain many axons or nerve fibres.

How to complete this lesson — Learn → practise → required check → finish
  1. Learn the pathway roles first. Distinguish sensory neurons, interneurons and motor neurons by what they do.
  2. Compare two routes. Work through a deliberately selected response, then a spinal withdrawal reflex. Do not decide that a movement is a reflex just because it is fast.
  3. Use the withdrawal diagram deliberately. Trace the sensory route into the spinal cord, the motor route out to the muscle, and the separate route carrying information toward the brain.
  4. Use support when it helps. The textbook and videos are optional. The essential explanation is on this page.
  5. Try the practice in your notes first. Reveal the hint only if needed, then use the solution to check your reasoning.
  6. Complete the required chapter check. Open it and select Start. Type each written response and select Save written response. Key terms: sensory neuron · interneuron · motor neuron · reflex · reflex arc

Three neuron jobs in a pathway

When you trace a route through the nervous system, classify neurons by the job they are doing.

Sensory neuron — information toward the CNS

A sensory neuron, also called an afferent neuron, carries information from a sensory receptor toward the central nervous system.

The receptor is the detector. The sensory neuron is part of the route carrying that information inward.

Interneuron — processing and connection inside the CNS

An interneuron is a neuron within the CNS that processes or relays information between neurons.

In the withdrawal pathway used in this lesson, an interneuron helps connect sensory input to motor output within the spinal cord.

Motor neuron — instructions toward an effector

A motor neuron, also called an efferent neuron, carries instructions from the CNS toward an effector such as a muscle.

For the somatic pathways in this lesson, the effector is skeletal muscle.

Put the jobs together:

receptor → sensory neuron → CNS integration → motor neuron → effector → response

That is a general route. The exact form of CNS integration is what changes between different responses.

Job is not the same thing as shape

You may see neurons described as multipolar, bipolar or unipolar/pseudounipolar. Those words describe neuron morphology — how the cell body and processes are arranged.

By contrast, sensory neuron, interneuron and motor neuron describe functional roles in a pathway.

Do not memorize a rule that every sensory neuron must have one shape, every interneuron another, and every motor neuron another. In this lesson, classify the pathway by function unless a question specifically asks about morphology.

Worked pathway 1: a deliberately selected response

Your phone begins sliding off the edge of a desk. You see it move and choose to reach for it.

This can happen quickly, but speed alone does not make it a reflex.

Step 1 — Detect the stimulus

Visual receptors detect the changing position of the phone.

You do not need detailed eye anatomy yet. For this pathway, the important fact is that a sensory receptor detects visual information.

Step 2 — Carry sensory information toward the CNS

Sensory information travels toward the CNS.

Step 3 — Integrate in the brain

Brain circuits process the visual information and select a response: reach for the phone.

That deliberate selection matters. The movement is not simply the automatic output of a spinal withdrawal circuit.

Step 4 — Send somatic motor output

Motor commands travel from the CNS along somatic motor pathways toward skeletal muscles in the arm and hand.

Step 5 — The effectors act

Skeletal muscles contract in a coordinated pattern and the hand reaches toward the phone.

So the simplified pathway is:

visual receptor → sensory pathway → CNS/brain integration and response selection → somatic motor pathway → skeletal-muscle effectors → reaching response

Worked pathway 2: a spinal withdrawal reflex

Now consider a different event. Your hand accidentally contacts a dangerously hot surface. Do not test this. It is only a scenario for tracing the neural circuit.

A reflex is a rapid, involuntary response to a stimulus. A reflex arc is the neural pathway that produces the reflex response.

For the spinal withdrawal example in this lesson:

receptor in skin → sensory neuron → spinal cord integration → motor neuron → skeletal-muscle effector → withdrawal

Step 1 — A receptor detects the harmful stimulus

Receptors in the skin detect the intense stimulus and sensory neurons carry information inward.

Step 2 — Sensory information enters the spinal cord

The signal enters the CNS at the spinal cord.

Step 3 — A spinal circuit begins the response

In the simplified withdrawal pathway shown here, an interneuron within the spinal cord relays the sensory information to a motor neuron.

The spinal circuit can initiate motor output before the brain has completed conscious processing of the event.

Step 4 — Motor output travels to skeletal muscle

The motor neuron carries instructions from the spinal cord toward the skeletal-muscle effector.

Step 5 — The muscle contracts and the hand withdraws

The response reduces continued contact with the harmful stimulus.

Step 6 — Information also reaches the brain

A spinal reflex does not mean that information never reaches the brain. Other neural pathways carry information upward, allowing conscious perception and further processing.

That is why the useful contrast is:

the spinal circuit can initiate withdrawal before conscious processing is complete — not “the brain never receives the information.”

Read the withdrawal diagram in the correct order

Use the figure titled A withdrawal reflex: follow the pathway.

  1. Start at the Stimulus arrow on the left.
  2. Move to the labelled Receptor in skin.
  3. Follow the orange route labelled Sensory neuron into the Spinal cord (CNS).
  4. Inside the spinal cord, find the label Interneuron in this simplified pathway.
  5. Follow the teal route labelled Motor neuron out of the spinal cord.
  6. End at the Effector: muscle.
  7. Read the teal response label: Response: the muscle contracts and the hand withdraws.
  8. Now look back inside the spinal cord and follow the dashed grey arrow labelled Information also travels to the brain. That arrow is essential: spinal withdrawal and information travelling toward the brain can happen as parts of the same overall event.

The bottom pathway sentence summarizes the route shown:

Receptor → sensory neuron → interneuron → motor neuron → effector.

Treat that sequence as the pathway in this withdrawal example, not as the wiring diagram for every reflex in the body.

Compare the two pathways

Feature Deliberately selected visual response Spinal withdrawal reflex
Stimulus is detected by a receptor Yes Yes
Sensory information travels toward the CNS Yes Yes
CNS integration occurs Yes Yes
Brain processing selects the movement before the response in this example Yes No — the spinal circuit can initiate withdrawal first
Somatic motor output reaches skeletal muscle Yes Yes
Conscious decision is required before the movement begins Yes, for the deliberately selected action described here No
Information can reach the brain Yes Yes

The shared structure matters: both use sensory input, CNS processing, motor output and skeletal-muscle effectors.

The difference is how the response is selected and where the rapid initiating integration occurs.

Two rules you should not overgeneralize

Not every reflex requires an interneuron between the sensory and motor neuron

The withdrawal reflex shown here uses an interneuron. Some simpler reflex circuits can connect the sensory input to a motor neuron without an intervening interneuron in that part of the circuit.

So do not memorize reflex = must contain an interneuron as a universal rule.

Not every reflex is a spinal reflex

This lesson focuses on a spinal withdrawal reflex, but reflexes can also be integrated in the brainstem. For example, the pupillary light reflex that constricts the pupil in response to light uses brain/midbrain circuitry.

So do not memorize reflex = always bypasses the brain and goes through the spinal cord.

The useful general definition is that a reflex is a rapid, involuntary response produced by a neural circuit.

Quick retrieval: neurons and glial cells are not the same job

Before the required check, retrieve this distinction from Lessons 1 and 2.

Neurons are specialized communication cells. They receive information, conduct electrical signals and pass information to other cells.

Glial cells support neural function. Depending on the glial cell type, that support can include maintaining the environment around neurons, forming myelin, providing structural/metabolic support, contributing to immune defence and removing debris.

The comparison is not “important cells versus unimportant cells.” Neurons and glia have different roles that work together.

Guided attempt — trace a withdrawal pathway

A person accidentally brushes the side of a hot baking tray and immediately pulls the hand away. Again, this is a scenario, not an experiment to perform.

Question: Trace the basic pathway from detection to withdrawal. Then explain why the person's hand can begin moving before conscious awareness of pain is complete.

Hint — reveal only if you need it

Start with receptor → sensory neuron. Ask where the rapid withdrawal circuit is integrated, then follow motor output to the effector. Finally, remember the dashed route in the diagram.

Check your reasoning

A suitable pathway is:

skin receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord/interneuron in this withdrawal circuit → motor neuron → skeletal-muscle effector → hand withdraws

The spinal circuit can initiate the motor response before the brain has completed conscious processing of the sensory information. Information also travels toward the brain, so the person can become consciously aware of the painful stimulus even though the withdrawal has already started.

If you wrote “the brain is never involved,” use the dashed route in the diagram to correct that idea.

If you wrote that the sensory neuron carries the command to the muscle, recheck the direction rule: sensory neurons carry information toward the CNS; motor neurons carry output toward the effector.

Independent transfer — fast does not automatically mean reflex

Before a class activity, you are told: “When you hear the buzzer, raise your right hand.” The buzzer sounds, you recognize the signal, and you deliberately raise your hand.

Question: Is the hand-raising response best modelled as the spinal withdrawal reflex taught above? Trace the basic pathway and justify your answer.

Write your reasoning in your notes before revealing the model.

Model answer and feedback — reveal after you try

The response should not be modelled as the spinal withdrawal reflex simply because it happens quickly. A sound receptor detects the buzzer, sensory information travels toward the CNS, brain circuits recognize the signal and select the instructed response, motor commands travel through somatic motor pathways, and skeletal muscles raise the hand.

A suitable simplified route is:

sensory receptor → sensory pathway → CNS/brain integration and response selection → somatic motor pathway → skeletal-muscle effector → hand raises

The key distinction is that the movement is a learned, deliberately selected response to the signal, whereas the spinal withdrawal reflex can be initiated by a spinal circuit without a conscious decision before movement begins.

If you wrote “it is a reflex because it is fast,” speed alone is not enough. Identify how the response is selected.

If you wrote “autonomic because you did not spend long thinking,” remember that the effector is skeletal muscle; that makes the motor output somatic.

Before the required check

Make sure you can explain these ideas in your own words:

  • sensory neurons carry information toward the CNS;
  • interneurons process/relay information within the CNS;
  • motor neurons carry instructions toward effectors;
  • neurons communicate information, while glial cells support neural function;
  • a deliberately selected visual response and a spinal withdrawal reflex share a receptor-to-effector organization but differ in how the response is initiated;
  • a spinal withdrawal response can begin before conscious processing is complete while information also travels toward the brain;
  • fast movement alone does not prove that the movement is a reflex.

For any pathway comparison, use the same reasoning strategy:

  1. identify the detector/receptor;
  2. trace the sensory input direction toward the CNS;
  3. identify where and how the response is integrated or selected;
  4. trace the motor output toward the effector;
  5. identify the effector and response;
  6. then compare whether the movement requires a selected response before it begins or can be initiated by a reflex circuit.

Use that strategy on the required comparison instead of deciding from speed alone.

Closing answer

Information can move from a receptor through sensory pathways into the CNS and then through motor pathways to an effector. A deliberately selected response relies on brain processing to select the movement. In a spinal withdrawal reflex, a spinal circuit can initiate motor output before conscious processing is complete, while information also travels toward the brain.

You now know where information travels. The next lesson asks what must already be true across a neuron's membrane before that cell can generate an electrical signal at all.

Corrected teacher-only marking guidance for source-checkpoint-6

Historical key conflict: Chapter 11 teacher-only comprehension key, p. 2, labels the tennis-ball dodge itself as a withdrawal reflex and routes it through eye → sensory neuron → spinal cord/interneuron → motor neuron → muscle. This should not be used as the automatic/scientific authority for the current course.

Scientifically defensible marking guidance

A strong response should:

  1. identify visual detection in the eye at a general receptor level;
  2. carry sensory information toward the CNS/brain;
  3. include brain integration/processing and selection of the dodge response;
  4. carry somatic motor output toward skeletal muscles that produce the dodge;
  5. compare this with a spinal withdrawal reflex: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal integration (interneuron in the course's withdrawal diagram) → motor neuron → skeletal-muscle effector;
  6. explain that the spinal withdrawal response can begin without a prior conscious decision while information also travels toward the brain.

Acceptable nuance

A student may discuss startle/protective reflex components in a real unexpected-ball situation if the response still distinguishes those components from the simplified deliberately selected visual pathway and correctly traces the neural route. Do not require students to call the entire tennis-ball dodge a spinal withdrawal reflex.

Do not award as fully correct solely for

  • “eye → spinal cord → muscle” with no brain/visual integration for the deliberately selected dodge;
  • “both are reflexes because both are fast”;
  • “withdrawal never sends information to the brain.”

This teacher-only correction must remain outside learner automatic feedback unless separately approved.
