Learn · Chapter 11

Nervous communication

How can your body detect a change, decide what matters, and coordinate a response quickly enough to keep internal conditions workable?

Learning goal

I can explain how the nervous system detects changes, processes information, and directs responses, and I can describe the basic organization of the nervous system.

Before you begin

A stimulus is a change inside or outside the body. A response is what the body does after detecting that change.

You already know that cells and body systems have to work together. In this lesson, you will add the communication pathway that lets the nervous system detect a change, process information, and direct a response.

Key terms: stimulus · response · receptor · effector · neuron · homeostasis

Start with the problem: your body never stays perfectly unchanged

Walk from a quiet classroom into a warm gym. Start moving hard and your muscles release more heat. Your breathing changes. Your heart rate changes. After a while, you may begin to sweat.

Your body did not stay perfectly constant, and it was not supposed to. Homeostasis means regulating internal conditions within ranges that allow cells and organs to function. Conditions can move up and down; the important point is that control systems detect meaningful change and produce responses that help keep internal conditions within workable limits.

The nervous system is one of the body's major control and communication systems. It can detect changes, carry information rapidly, integrate that information in the central nervous system, and direct muscles or glands to respond. The endocrine system also contributes to homeostasis using hormones; you will study that system in more detail later.

The nervous system also does many things that are not simply homeostatic corrections. Seeing a friend, choosing to pick up a backpack, remembering a song, or deliberately moving your hand are nervous-system functions even when no internal variable needs to be returned toward a regulated range.

So keep two ideas separate:

  • The nervous system has many functions.
  • One important function is helping regulate homeostasis.

A basic nervous-system communication pathway

When the nervous system controls a response, the details can vary, but this pattern is a useful starting model:

stimulus → receptor → sensory input → integration in the CNS → motor output → effector → response

Read that pathway from left to right.

1. A stimulus changes something

A stimulus is a change that can be detected. It might come from outside the body, such as pressure on the skin, or from inside the body, such as a change in temperature or blood chemistry.

2. A receptor detects the change

A receptor is a specialized structure that detects a particular kind of stimulus. Detecting the change is not yet the response. It is the beginning of the information pathway.

3. Sensory input carries information toward the CNS

Sensory input is information travelling from receptors toward the central nervous system (CNS). The CNS is the brain and spinal cord.

You will also see the word afferent. Sensory or afferent pathways carry information toward the CNS.

4. Integration means processing the information

Integration is the processing step. The CNS receives information, combines it with other information when needed, and organizes an appropriate output.

Integration does not always mean a long conscious decision. Some responses involve brain processing and conscious choice; others can be initiated by rapid neural circuits before conscious processing is complete. You will compare those pathways in Lesson 3.

5. Motor output carries instructions away from the CNS

Motor output is information travelling from the CNS toward a structure that will carry out the response.

You will also see the word efferent. Motor or efferent pathways carry instructions away from the CNS.

6. An effector carries out the response

An effector is the responding structure. Effectors include muscles and glands.

A muscle can contract. A gland can change its secretion. The activity of the effector produces the response.

Worked example: cooling the body during hard exercise

Suppose you are exercising hard in a warm gym. Your body produces more heat, and core temperature begins to rise.

We can use the communication pathway to explain part of the body's response.

Step 1 — Identify the change

The important change is increased body temperature.

Step 2 — Detect the change

Temperature information comes from more than one place. Temperature-sensitive receptors in the body surface provide information about environmental temperature, and temperature-sensitive structures within the central nervous system can monitor the body's internal temperature.

The key idea is not that one skin receptor controls the response. The key idea is that temperature information is detected and communicated to nervous-system control centres.

Step 3 — Carry sensory information

Sensory information about temperature reaches the CNS.

Step 4 — Integrate

The CNS processes the incoming information and organizes responses that can help limit the rise in body temperature.

At this point you do not need to memorize the detailed brain anatomy of thermoregulation. The important idea is the job being done: information is detected, processed and used to organize an output.

Step 5 — Send motor output

Motor instructions can increase the activity of sweat glands.

Step 6 — The effector acts

The sweat glands release sweat onto the skin.

Step 7 — Explain how the response helps

Sweat on the skin does not cool the body simply because it is wet. Evaporation matters. When liquid water in sweat changes to water vapour, energy is required for that change of state. Heat is transferred from the body as the sweat evaporates, helping remove heat from the skin and limit the rise in body temperature.

Put the reasoning together:

body temperature rises → temperature information is detected → the CNS integrates the information → motor output increases sweat-gland activity → sweat evaporates → heat is removed → the response helps keep body temperature within a workable range

That is a homeostatic nervous-system response because the response helps oppose the original change.

Contrast: a nervous response that is not mainly a homeostatic correction

Imagine that your pencil rolls off your desk. You see it falling and deliberately reach to catch it.

The nervous system still detects information, integrates it and sends motor output to skeletal muscles. But the purpose of the response is not to return an internal condition such as body temperature toward a regulated range.

This is why you should not use nervous-system response and homeostatic response as if they mean exactly the same thing.

How the nervous system is organized

The required check in this lesson asks you to identify the different systems in the nervous system and explain their structure and function. A list of names is not enough. You need to know how the divisions are nested and which direction information travels.

Before reading the hierarchy, separate a cell from a nerve. A neuron is one specialized cell. Its axon is the long nerve fibre that carries a signal away from the cell body. In the PNS, a nerve is a larger cable-like structure containing many axons, or nerve fibres, bundled together with protective tissues. That means a sensory or motor pathway can travel through a nerve made of many separate fibres; one nerve is not one giant neuron.

Now read this tree from the top down:

Nervous system

  • Central nervous system (CNS)
    • brain
    • spinal cord
  • Peripheral nervous system (PNS)
    • sensory (afferent) pathways — carry information from receptors toward the CNS
    • motor (efferent) pathways — carry instructions from the CNS toward effectors
      • somatic nervous system — motor control of skeletal muscles
      • autonomic nervous system — motor control of smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands
        • sympathetic division
        • parasympathetic division

Now turn the tree into structure-and-function statements.

Division Basic structure Basic function in this chapter
Central nervous system (CNS) Brain and spinal cord Receives and integrates information and organizes nervous-system responses.
Peripheral nervous system (PNS) Nerves and neural pathways connecting the CNS with the rest of the body Carries sensory information toward the CNS and motor information from the CNS toward effectors.
Sensory / afferent pathways Sensory neurons and their axons/nerve fibres carry input from receptors through the PNS toward the CNS Carry information toward the CNS.
Motor / efferent pathways Motor neurons and their axons/nerve fibres connect the CNS through the PNS to effectors Carry instructions away from the CNS toward effectors.
Somatic nervous system Motor pathways from the CNS through the PNS to skeletal-muscle effectors Controls skeletal-muscle responses. Many are deliberate, but a somatic response is not automatically conscious: a withdrawal reflex also activates skeletal muscle.
Autonomic nervous system Motor pathways from the CNS through the PNS to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands Regulates involuntary organ and gland activity. Detailed organ effects come later in Lesson 8.
Sympathetic division One branch of autonomic neural pathways running from the CNS through the PNS to cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands Adjusts autonomic output during action, challenge or increased demand. Detailed organ effects come later.
Parasympathetic division The complementary branch of autonomic neural pathways running from the CNS through the PNS to cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands Adjusts autonomic output during rest, maintenance and recovery. Detailed organ effects come later.

Read the hierarchy, not just the words

Notice three different kinds of distinction:

  1. CNS versus PNS is mainly an anatomical distinction: central structures versus the neural connections linking the CNS with the body.
  2. Sensory versus motor describes the direction of information relative to the CNS.
  3. Somatic versus autonomic describes the type of motor control and the effectors being controlled.

The sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions are inside the autonomic motor system. They are not separate systems sitting beside the CNS and PNS.

Guided attempt 1 — Which way is the information travelling?

A pressure receptor in the skin of the foot is activated. Information travels through a peripheral nerve toward the spinal cord.

Question: Is this sensory/afferent or motor/efferent information? Is the spinal cord part of the CNS or PNS?

Hint — reveal only if you need it

Use direction first. Information moving toward the brain or spinal cord is sensory/afferent. Then ask where the spinal cord belongs.

Check your reasoning

This is sensory/afferent information because it is travelling from a receptor toward the CNS. The spinal cord is part of the CNS. The peripheral nerve carrying the signal is part of the PNS.

If you called the pathway motor because the body might eventually move, you skipped ahead. Classify the signal by the direction it is travelling at this part of the pathway.

Guided attempt 2 — Somatic or autonomic?

A motor command leaves the CNS and causes a skeletal muscle to contract.

Question: Is this motor pathway somatic or autonomic? Does the word somatic prove that the movement was consciously chosen?

Hint — reveal only if you need it

Classify the pathway by its effector. Which motor division controls skeletal muscle?

Check your reasoning

It is a somatic motor pathway because the effector is skeletal muscle. Somatic does not guarantee that the movement was consciously chosen. Some skeletal-muscle responses are reflexes. You will study a withdrawal reflex in Lesson 3.

The cells that make the pathway possible

Now zoom in from the system-level map to the cells and fibres carrying those messages. The nervous system contains neurons and glial cells.

A neuron is one specialized cell that receives, conducts and passes information. A glial cell supports neural function. Different glial cells can help maintain the chemical environment around neurons, support and insulate axons, remove debris and contribute to immune defence.

A nerve is not one neuron. In the PNS, a nerve is a larger structure containing many axons, or nerve fibres, bundled together with protective tissues. Think of a cable containing many separate fibres: the cable is the nerve; the individual conducting fibres are axons from neurons.

You will zoom in on one neuron and its myelin in the next lesson.

Independent transfer — what happens if the control route is interrupted?

Consider this generic homeostatic control route:

internal condition moves outside its workable range → receptor detects the change → sensory information reaches the CNS → the CNS integrates the information → motor output travels to an effector → the effector acts → the response helps move the condition back toward its workable range

Now suppose the receptor and sensory pathway work normally and the CNS correctly processes the information, but the motor pathway to the effector is blocked.

Explain:

  1. Which parts of the route can still occur?
  2. Which part fails?
  3. Why can homeostatic regulation be disrupted even though the original change was detected correctly?

Write your answer in your notes before checking the model.

Model answer and feedback — reveal after you try

The receptor can still detect the change, sensory information can still reach the CNS, and the CNS can still integrate that information. The failure occurs after integration: the motor instruction cannot reach the effector. Because the effector does not receive the command, it cannot produce the corrective response. The body can therefore detect the problem correctly but still fail to regulate the condition through that pathway.

A strong answer separates detection, integration and output instead of saying only that “the nervous system stops working.”

If you wrote that the receptor fails, check the scenario again: the receptor was specifically stated to work normally.

If you wrote that the CNS does not know a change occurred, check the direction of the sensory pathway: the sensory message still reaches the CNS.

Consequential editorial decisions

  • Homeostasis is written as regulation within workable ranges, not perfect constancy. The teacher key's “constant internal environment” wording is treated as an older simplification, while its note about dynamic equilibrium is retained conceptually.
  • The temperature example does not claim that all detection comes from skin/PNS receptors. Peripheral and central temperature sensing are both acknowledged without pulling later brain anatomy into the learner path.
  • Not every nervous-system response is described as homeostatic.
  • Somatic control is defined by skeletal-muscle effectors, not by a rule that every somatic response is consciously chosen.
  • The lesson teaches the hierarchy required by source-checkpoint-3 before the student encounters that check. Detailed sympathetic/parasympathetic organ effects remain deferred to lesson-08.
  • A nerve is described as a bundle containing many axons/nerve fibres with supporting tissues, not a bundle of whole neuron drawings.
