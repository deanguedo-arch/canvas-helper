## One cell, built for communication

Neurons share many features with other body cells: they have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus and organelles. What makes them useful in a nervous system is the way their specialized regions are arranged for communication.

A useful first model is:

**receive → integrate → conduct → pass information onward**

The figure in this lesson uses a **typical multipolar neuron**: many dendrites branch around the cell body, and one axon leaves the cell body. This is a useful model for learning structure and function, not a rule that every neuron has the same shape.

Before using the structure table, you need one signal term. An **action potential** is a brief travelling electrical event in a neuron's membrane. As it moves along an axon, active membrane regions generate the next part of the signal. That basic meaning is enough for this lesson; Lessons 4 and 5 teach the ion movements, resting conditions and threshold in detail.

The parts of a typical neuron support the communication jobs below.

| Structure | Where to find it | Main job in this lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Dendrites** | Branched extensions near the cell body | Receive incoming information from other cells or receptors and carry that influence toward the cell body. |
| **Cell body (soma)** | Main region containing the nucleus and most organelles | Maintains the cell and combines incoming influences. |
| **Nucleus** | Inside the cell body | Contains DNA and supports control of the cell's activities. |
| **Axon hillock / initial region** | Where the cell body narrows into the axon | Incoming influences are combined here; under the right electrical conditions, an action potential can begin in the axon's initial region. Detailed threshold physiology comes later. |
| **Axon** | Long extension leaving the cell body | Conducts the travelling electrical event away from the cell body. |
| **Axon terminals** | Branched end of the axon | Pass information onward at a **synapse**, the junction where a neuron communicates with another cell. Many synapses studied later in this chapter use chemical messengers; detailed synaptic transmission comes in Lesson 6. |

## Follow information through one neuron

Use the image titled **Structure of a neuron**.

Start on the left side at the label **Dendrites**. These orange branching structures provide a large receiving region.

Next, follow the branches into the **Cell body (soma)**. The labelled **Nucleus** sits inside the soma. The cell body is not just a relay point; it contains the machinery that keeps the neuron alive and it is where incoming signals are combined.

Now find the **Axon hillock** where the cell body narrows into the axon. The figure says that signals are **summed** here. In plain language, that means incoming influences are combined. Under the right electrical conditions, an action potential can begin in the axon's initial region. Lessons 4 and 5 explain those conditions.

Follow the long **Axon** to the right. The large teal arrow along the bottom shows the overall direction of information flow: from dendrites toward the axon terminals.

Around parts of the axon are blue **Myelin sheath** segments. The small exposed gaps between those segments are labelled **Nodes of Ranvier**.

Finally, follow the axon to the branching **Axon terminals** on the far right. A **synapse** is the communication junction between a neuron and another cell. The terminals pass information onward at these junctions. Many of the synapses emphasized later in this chapter use chemical messengers, but the detailed synaptic mechanism is intentionally left for Lesson 6.

The image also includes a box labelled **Glial cells support neurons**. Three examples are shown:

- an **astrocyte**, which helps support neurons and regulate their chemical environment;
- an **oligodendrocyte**, which forms myelin in the CNS;
- a **microglial cell**, which contributes to immune defence and removal of debris.

These are examples of glial cells. They are not three new types of neuron.

### One important distinction

A diagram often makes the neuron look like a complete cable. A **nerve**, however, is not this one whole neuron copied many times. In the PNS, many axons from many neurons are bundled together into nerves with protective connective tissues.

## Worked example: why this neuron shape suits the job

Imagine a neuron that receives information from many nearby cells but must send its output to a target much farther away. Why is a branching receiving region, a cell body, one long axon and terminal branches a useful arrangement?

### Step 1 — Start with the input problem

The neuron may be influenced by many other cells. A branching dendritic region provides many places where incoming information can reach the neuron. More branching does not mean the dendrites make the final response by themselves; it gives the neuron a large receiving region.

### Step 2 — Combine the incoming information

Those incoming influences converge on the **cell body (soma)** and the axon's initial region. The soma also contains the nucleus and organelles that keep the cell functioning. The communication job here is to maintain the cell and combine incoming influences before an outgoing electrical event is produced.

### Step 3 — Solve the distance problem

Once an action potential begins, one long **axon** provides a continuous cellular route for carrying that electrical event away from the soma toward a distant target. The axon is suited to transmission over distance in a way that a cluster of short receiving branches is not.

### Step 4 — Pass the output onward

At the far end, **axon terminals** branch in the target region. At synapses, those terminals can pass information to another neuron or to an effector cell. The exact synaptic mechanism is a later lesson; here the important structure-function idea is that terminal branching creates multiple communication endings at the output end of the neuron.

### Conclusion

The overall shape matches the communication problem: **branched dendrites receive many inputs → the soma maintains the cell and combines incoming influences → one long axon carries the electrical event over distance → terminal branches pass the output onward.**

This is more than a label list. Each structure helps solve a different part of the neuron's communication job.

## Why myelin changes conduction

Many axons are wrapped in a fatty insulating layer called the **myelin sheath**.

Myelin is made by glial cells, but the type of glial cell depends on location:

- **Schwann cells form myelin in the PNS.**
- **Oligodendrocytes form myelin in the CNS.**

Myelin does not replace the axon. The axon remains continuous underneath the sheath.

The gaps between adjacent myelin segments are the **nodes of Ranvier**. Those exposed regions of axon membrane are where the action potential is regenerated in a myelinated axon.

### Compare the two patterns

In an **unmyelinated axon**, neighbouring regions of axon membrane are activated one after another. The travelling electrical event is regenerated continuously along the membrane.

In a **myelinated axon**, the insulating myelin changes how local electrical current spreads. The action potential is regenerated mainly at the nodes of Ranvier. This pattern is called **saltatory conduction**.

You will often hear saltatory conduction described as the impulse “jumping” from node to node. That is a useful visual shorthand, but do not imagine a signal teleporting through empty space. The axon is continuous beneath the myelin. Current spreads along the axon under the insulated regions, and the full action potential is regenerated at the exposed nodes.

Because the membrane does not need to regenerate the action potential at every neighbouring point along the myelinated stretch, myelinated conduction is faster than continuous conduction in a comparable unmyelinated axon.

## Compare conduction in the myelin figure

The image titled **Myelin and saltatory conduction** has two panels.

### Panel A — Unmyelinated axon: continuous conduction

Look at the exposed axon. The orange curved arrows and highlighted points run along neighbouring regions of membrane. The figure labels this **continuous conduction** and shows the direction from left to right.

Do not interpret the separate highlighted points as separate signals. They represent successive regions participating in the propagation of one travelling impulse.

### Panel B — Myelinated axon: saltatory conduction

Now look at the blue myelin sheath segments around the axon. The gaps are labelled **Node of Ranvier (gap in myelin)**. The orange arrows span from one node region toward the next.

The figure also labels a **Schwann cell**, because this drawing is showing peripheral myelin. If the axon were in the CNS, myelin would be formed by oligodendrocytes instead.

The bottom message is the comparison to remember:

**myelin increases the speed of nerve impulse conduction.**

The figure also warns that damage to myelin can slow or block signalling.

## Worked example: same distance, different insulation

Two axons carry signals the same distance. They are similar in other relevant ways, but Axon A is myelinated and Axon B is unmyelinated.

**Question:** Which signal should arrive sooner, and what biological mechanism explains the difference?

### Step 1 — Identify the meaningful difference

The important difference is **myelin**, not the destination or the length of the route.

### Step 2 — Compare where the action potential is regenerated

In Axon B, which is unmyelinated, neighbouring regions of membrane regenerate the signal in sequence along the axon.

In Axon A, myelin insulates long stretches of membrane and the action potential is regenerated mainly at nodes of Ranvier.

### Step 3 — Link structure to function

The myelin sheath changes conduction so the signal can travel more rapidly along the axon between the exposed nodes.

### Conclusion

**Axon A should conduct the signal faster because its myelin sheath allows saltatory conduction, with action potentials regenerated at nodes of Ranvier rather than continuously at every neighbouring region of membrane.**

Notice what the answer does **not** say:

- Myelin is not the electrical signal.
- The signal does not leave one axon and jump through space to another.
- An unmyelinated axon is not “off”; it still conducts, but by a different pattern.

## Why myelin damage matters

If myelin is damaged, the normal insulation and organization of conduction are disrupted. Signals can slow, become unreliable, or fail along affected pathways.

**Multiple sclerosis (MS)** is a disorder of the CNS in which immune-mediated damage affects myelin and can also damage nerve fibres. Because different CNS pathways can be affected in different people, symptoms vary. They can include changes in sensation, vision, strength, balance or coordination.

For this lesson, keep the causal connection simple:

**myelin damage → disrupted neural signalling → changes in function depending on the pathway affected**

MS is not explained by a single “MS gene,” and the course of the disease is not identical for every person.

## Independent transfer — the axon is intact, but the myelin is damaged

A long axon is still physically continuous from its starting region to its terminals. The neuron can still produce an action potential, but much of the myelin along the axon has been damaged.

**Predict what could happen to signal conduction and explain the mechanism using the terms _myelin_, _node of Ranvier_, _action potential_ and _saltatory conduction_.**

Write your explanation in your notes before revealing the model.

<details>
<summary><strong>Model answer and feedback — reveal after you try</strong></summary>

The axon can still be physically continuous, but damaged myelin reduces the insulation that normally supports rapid saltatory conduction. In a healthy myelinated axon, the action potential is regenerated at nodes of Ranvier while current spreads efficiently beneath myelinated segments. When myelin is lost, that pattern is disrupted, so conduction can slow or the signal can fail before reaching the terminals.

A strong answer connects **structure to mechanism to consequence**.

If you wrote “the signal has nowhere to jump,” replace that picture with the continuous-axon model: the signal does not teleport. Myelin supports efficient current spread between nodes.

If you wrote “the axon disappears,” check the scenario: it explicitly states that the axon is still continuous.

</details>

## Closing answer

A neuron is shaped for directional communication: dendrites receive, the cell body maintains and integrates, the axon conducts a travelling electrical event, and the terminals pass information onward. Myelin insulates many axons and allows faster saltatory conduction because the action potential is regenerated mainly at nodes of Ranvier.

Now we can follow one cell. The next question is how several cells are organized into a complete route from a receptor to an effector.
