## Catching a ball takes more than one brain region

To catch a ball, you must process visual information, organize movement, and adjust your hand as the ball approaches. Your balance and breathing also continue. There is no single “catching centre” doing all of this alone. Different brain regions make different contributions through connected pathways.

Our goal is to connect **location, structure, and function**. First build a broad map, then add details. When interpreting a problem, ask what function changed before choosing a structure.

## Build the map from the spinal cord upward

This sagittal view is a lengthwise section that exposes structures hidden inside. Locate the thalamus near the centre, then the hypothalamus below it and the attached pituitary gland below that. The three broad divisions are **forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain**. The forebrain includes the cerebrum, thalamus, hypothalamus, and other structures. The hindbrain includes the cerebellum, pons, and medulla. Use the named structures to determine membership, rather than assuming that a bracket's height assigns every structure beside it to that region. The cerebellum is part of the hindbrain but is not part of the brainstem.

## Start with movement coordination and automatic functions

The **cerebellum** helps coordinate movement, posture, and balance. It receives sensory information, including information about body position from muscles and joints, and contributes to adjustments that make movements precise. It does not replace the motor command; it helps the movement match the intended result.

The **medulla oblongata** contains centres involved in automatic functions such as breathing, heart rate, blood-vessel control, swallowing, and coughing. These functions help maintain internal conditions while you attend to other tasks.

The **pons** relays information among regions, including connections between cerebral and cerebellar systems. It also contributes to breathing regulation and sleep/arousal. Its relay role helps coordinate activity across structures.

The **midbrain** contributes to visual and auditory processing, orienting responses, eye movement, and motor functions. It connects with forebrain and hindbrain pathways. Do not confuse it with the hypothalamus, which has a central role in temperature and other internal regulation.

### Worked example: strength is present, but movement is poorly coordinated

In a simplified case, a person can activate an arm muscle but has difficulty making a smooth, accurate reaching movement.

1. **Name the impaired function.** The main reported difficulty is coordination and accuracy, not a complete inability to activate the muscle.
2. **Match the function to a contribution.** The cerebellum is a strong candidate because it contributes to adjusting and coordinating movement.
3. **Explain the connection.** Disrupted use of sensory feedback can make an intended movement poorly timed or inaccurate.
4. **Limit the conclusion.** This is a function-matching explanation. A symptom alone does not establish the location or cause of a real injury.

## The forebrain includes relays, regulation, and the cerebrum

The **thalamus** is a major relay and processing station. Much sensory information passes through thalamic pathways on its way to the cerebral cortex; smell is a major exception to that general initial relay pattern. Relaying is more than sending every message unchanged: the thalamus participates in how information is passed among regions.

The **hypothalamus** helps regulate temperature, hunger, thirst, autonomic activity, and other internal functions. It links nervous and endocrine control through its relationship with the **pituitary gland**. When a question asks about maintaining internal conditions, explain which regulated condition is involved rather than answering only “it maintains homeostasis.”

The **cerebrum** has left and right cerebral hemispheres. Its outer **cerebral cortex** is grey matter. Folds increase the surface area that can fit inside the skull, providing space for extensive processing networks. White-matter connections underneath link areas within and between hemispheres. The cerebrum is part of the forebrain, not another name for the entire forebrain.

The **corpus callosum** is a major bundle of axons connecting the hemispheres. It allows information processed on one side to be shared with the other. It is a communication route, not a separate lobe or a storage site for all memories.

| --- | --- | --- |

## Distinguish a motor command from sensory information

Find the **primary motor area** in the frontal lobe, just in front of the central boundary. It contributes to voluntary movement. Just behind that boundary, in the parietal lobe, the **primary somatosensory area** processes information such as touch from the body. The areas lie close together, but receiving sensory information and producing motor output are different jobs.

Panel B uses a distorted body to show that cortical representation is uneven. Hands and face occupy relatively large representations because of detailed control requirements. The exaggerated sizes do not show actual body sizes, and this stylized inset should not be used as a precise anatomical atlas of every body part's position.

Many major motor pathways cross in the brainstem. Damage to the right primary motor cortex can therefore affect movement on the left side of the body. Trace the pathway: right cortex, descending fibres, crossing, then output influencing left-side muscles. Do not apply an opposite-side rule automatically to every brain structure or every symptom.

## Language illustrates specialization within a network

**Broca's area**, commonly in the left frontal region, contributes to speech production and language processing. Damage can make speech effortful and nonfluent. **Wernicke's area**, classically associated with the posterior left temporal region and nearby language network, contributes to comprehension. Damage can leave speech fluent but less meaningful and make understanding difficult.

The contrast helps explain two different functions; it is not a complete account of language or a guarantee that every person's symptoms are identical. Producing speech is more than moving mouth muscles, and understanding a sentence involves a network of connected areas.

**Worked comparison:** if a person understands a request but struggles to produce fluent speech, a production-network problem fits better than a simple inability to hear. If another person hears speech but cannot reliably understand it, the problem concerns comprehension, not merely sound reaching the ear. Use the described function as the evidence; do not diagnose a person from one brief observation.

## The hemispheres cooperate

[![The horizontal connection represents communication through the corpus callosum. Crossing pathways illustrate the opposite-side motor relationship described in the textbook.](../review-assets/chapter11-brain-2.svg)](#ch11-embedded-image-21)


**Figure 19.** The horizontal connection represents communication through the corpus callosum. Crossing pathways illustrate the opposite-side motor relationship described in the textbook.

The horizontal connection represents communication through the corpus callosum. The crossing lines below illustrate the major opposite-side motor relationship. These are two different kinds of connections: interhemispheric communication and descending motor pathways.

Some functions are more strongly associated with one hemisphere, but complex thinking uses interacting regions on both sides. The idea that one student is a purely analytical “left-brained learner” and another a purely creative “right-brained learner” is not a sound explanation of these networks.

**Optional depth: additional forebrain and brainstem structures**

The **basal ganglia** help select and regulate movement through circuits linked with the cortex. The **hippocampus**, in the medial temporal region, is important for forming some kinds of new long-term memories. The **amygdala** contributes to processing emotional significance and learning about threats. The **reticular formation**, extending through the brainstem, contributes to arousal and attention. These structures work in networks; none is a complete “emotion centre” or a store for every kind of memory.

Memory is distributed. Frontal systems contribute to working memory and organized retrieval; temporal and hippocampal systems contribute to other memory functions. If a task asks about memory, specify what aspect of memory the example describes rather than assuming a single lobe owns it all.
