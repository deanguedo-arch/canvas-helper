<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l13-start"><h2>Use the biology to examine a broader claim</h2><p>This optional extension offers three connected questions: why a response to danger can also occur before an exam, how experience can influence neural networks, and how learning and biological change can both matter in addiction. Choose the topic you want to explore. No personal disclosure or required submission is expected unless your teacher assigns an activity.</p><p>For any topic, separate <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="mechanism" data-bio-term-route="lesson-13" type="button">mechanism</button></strong> from <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="evidence" data-bio-term-route="lesson-13" type="button">evidence</button></strong>. A plausible biological explanation is a reason to investigate a claim. It is not proof that the claim applies to every person.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l13-stress"><h2>Why can an exam produce a physical stress response?</h2><p>The nervous system can respond to a perceived challenge, not only to immediate physical damage. Interpreting an exam as threatening can influence CNS pathways that increase sympathetic activity. The result may include a faster heart rate, altered breathing, reduced watery salivation, and reduced digestive activity. These are real physiological changes even though there is no animal to escape.</p><p>Return to the pathway explanation. Brain processing evaluates the situation; autonomic output changes activity in internal effectors. Sympathetic influence and adrenal hormone release can help mobilize resources. When the challenge decreases, changes in autonomic balance can support recovery. A stress response is an adjustment, not a diagnosis by itself.</p><h3>Worked explanation: the same heart-rate change can have different causes</h3><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Describe the observation.</strong> Heart rate increased before an exam.</li><li><strong>Offer a relevant mechanism.</strong> Perceived challenge can increase sympathetic influence on the heart.</li><li><strong>Consider alternatives.</strong> Recent exercise, excitement, or other conditions can also raise heart rate.</li><li><strong>Limit the claim.</strong> One measurement does not identify an anxiety disorder, prove dishonesty, or show exactly what the person was thinking.</li></ol><p>Frequent or prolonged stress can affect how someone feels and functions, including attention and sleep. But explaining a general pathway is different from assessing a person's circumstances. You can analyze the hypothetical example without sharing experiences or offering a diagnosis.</p>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l13-experience"><h2>Experience can influence a network without determining a person's future</h2><p>Learning depends partly on changes in how neural connections function. Repeated activity can influence the effectiveness of communication within a network, changing how later input is processed. This capacity for change is often called <strong>neuroplasticity</strong>. It provides a biological way to discuss practice, learning, and development without imagining that the brain becomes permanently fixed after one event.</p><p>Evidence linking early experiences with later outcomes needs careful interpretation. People differ in many connected ways, and group averages do not describe every individual. To evaluate a claim, ask how the groups were selected, what was measured, whether other influences were considered, and whether the observations tracked change over time.</p><h3>Worked evidence check: two brain images</h3><p>Imagine a presentation displays two brain images and labels one “different childhood experiences.” First ask whether the images use the same method, scale, age range, and viewing plane. Then ask how many people were studied and whether the images are representative. Without that information, visible differences do not establish that one experience caused them or predict an individual's abilities.</p><p>A connection between experience and biology should not be used to label a person as permanently damaged. Biological systems can change, and an observed association does not determine one individual's future.</p>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l13-addiction"><h2>Addiction can involve both learning and biological change</h2><p>Some drugs alter signalling in circuits involved in reward-related learning and motivation. Dopamine can contribute to reinforcement: outcomes and cues become associated with behaviour, influencing what is sought again. With repeated exposure, signalling and behaviour can change. That does not reduce addiction to a single chemical, a single learning event, or a judgement about a person's character.</p><p>A biological account asks how cells, receptors, transmitters, and networks change. A learning account asks how consequences, repeated behaviour, and cues influence future actions. These questions can describe interacting parts of the same process. Calling something “learned” does not mean it is effortless to change; calling it a disorder does not mean learning and environment no longer matter.</p><h3>Worked connection: from synapse to repeated behaviour</h3><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Begin with a mechanism already taught.</strong> A drug may prolong transmitter availability by slowing its removal.</li><li><strong>Identify a circuit-level consequence.</strong> Altered signalling can influence reward-related processing and reinforcement.</li><li><strong>Add learning over time.</strong> Cues associated with the behaviour can come to influence motivation and expectations.</li><li><strong>State the limit.</strong> This is one explanatory route. Individual outcomes depend on interacting biological, behavioural, and environmental factors; it does not prove addiction after one use.</li></ol><p><strong>Tolerance</strong> describes a reduced response to a given exposure after adaptation. <strong>Dependence</strong> involves adaptations that can produce withdrawal when exposure stops. These ideas can be relevant, but neither word is identical to addiction. Addiction includes persistent, difficult-to-control use despite harmful consequences. A complete explanation needs more than a diagram of one synapse.</p>
