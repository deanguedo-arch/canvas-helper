## One situation can produce several kinds of output

You are about to start a race. You deliberately adjust your feet, while your heart rate rises and your mouth feels dry. These changes can happen together, but they do not all use the same kind of motor pathway. To classify a pathway, follow its destination: **what receives the output?**

The PNS links the brain and spinal cord with receptors, muscles, glands, and internal organs. Sensory pathways carry information inward toward the CNS. Motor pathways carry commands outward. The familiar count is **12 pairs of cranial nerves** associated with the brain and **31 pairs of spinal nerves** associated with the spinal cord. Individual nerves may contain several kinds of fibres, so the name of one nerve does not mean that every fibre in it performs the same job.

## Read the organization from location to function

[![Read down from nervous system to CNS and PNS, then follow the sensory and motor branches. The autonomic branch divides further into sympathetic and parasympathetic systems.](../review-assets/chapter11-pns-0.svg)](#ch11-embedded-image-14)


**Figure 13.** Read down from nervous system to CNS and PNS, then follow the sensory and motor branches. The autonomic branch divides further into sympathetic and parasympathetic systems.

Begin with the first split. **CNS versus PNS is a distinction about location.** The brain and spinal cord are central; peripheral nerves connect them to the body. Next, follow the PNS branches by function: sensory information goes toward the CNS and motor output goes away.

Below motor output, the somatic branch leads to skeletal muscle. The autonomic branch leads to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands. The connecting lines indicate “is part of” or “leads to this pathway”; they do not show a single impulse visiting every branch. The two lowest boxes, sympathetic and parasympathetic, are subdivisions of autonomic output.

The broader **sensory-somatic system** includes sensory information from the body and somatic motor output. That is why a description of the somatic system may mention both sensation and movement, while a motor-division chart places “somatic” on its output branch. Read what the chart is classifying.

## Distinguish somatic and autonomic control by their effectors

**Somatic motor neurons** activate skeletal muscles, such as the muscles that move your fingers and legs. Many such movements are voluntary, but skeletal muscles also participate in reflexes. A knee-jerk response is involuntary and still uses somatic motor output.

**Autonomic motor pathways** regulate smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands. Examples include changing the diameter of digestive-tract muscles, adjusting heart activity, and altering gland secretion. These pathways operate without requiring you to consciously set each organ's activity.

In a typical somatic motor route, one motor neuron runs from the CNS to skeletal muscle. A typical autonomic route uses a two-neuron chain, with a synapse in a peripheral **ganglion**, a cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the CNS. The first neuron is preganglionic and the second is postganglionic. This is a useful general organization; some pathways, including the adrenal medulla connection, are specialized.

### Worked classification: moving a foot while the heart speeds up

1. **Identify each effector separately.** Foot movement uses skeletal muscle; the heart uses cardiac muscle.
2. **Classify the outgoing routes.** Output to the foot is somatic. Autonomic activity helps adjust heart rate.
3. **Explain why the setting does not settle the category.** Both can occur during a race, but “exercise” is not a nervous-system division.
4. **Allow coordination.** Sensory information and CNS processing influence both routes. The categories describe linked components, not isolated systems.

Breathing also reminds us to distinguish levels: brainstem circuits help generate automatic breathing patterns, while motor output to the diaphragm reaches skeletal muscle. “It happens automatically” is not enough to classify every motor fibre as autonomic.

For the next comparison, note the effectors: the knee is moved by skeletal muscle, whereas pupil constriction is produced by smooth muscle in the iris. Use that tissue information when you classify the motor pathways; an involuntary response can use either type of effector.

## Sympathetic activity redistributes effort

The **sympathetic division** contributes to a fight-or-flight response. The heart can beat faster, air passages widen, and the liver releases glucose. These changes support rapid activity by helping supply oxygen and fuel. At the same time, digestion and bladder emptying can be reduced. “Sympathetic” does not mean that every organ speeds up.

The **parasympathetic division** supports functions often described as rest and digest: the heart slows, digestion increases, and bladder emptying is promoted. These divisions often have opposing effects on the same organ, adjusting its activity as conditions change. They are not two switches with the entire body locked into only one at a time.

This is a comparison of coordinated response patterns, not a claim that every organ receives an identical pair of opposing nerves. For example, heart rate and vascular resistance both influence blood pressure; it is too simple to call the parasympathetic division a direct opposite for every sympathetic blood-vessel effect.

## Connect the pathways to their chemical messengers

[![Figure 11.36, p. 398, retained for Section 11.3 review question 4. It shows the specific organ list and the source’s transmitter key.](../review-assets/chapter11-pns-2.webp)](#ch11-embedded-image-16)


Figure 11.36, p. 398, retained for Section 11.3 review question 4. It shows the specific organ list and the source’s transmitter key.

Use the transmitter key at the bottom of this larger diagram. The blue pathways use acetylcholine and the red pathways use norepinephrine. Those colours do not mean sensory versus motor, or simply excitatory versus inhibitory. The small ganglia mark synaptic relays outside the CNS.

Both autonomic divisions use ACh at their preganglionic synapses. Many sympathetic postganglionic neurons use norepinephrine; parasympathetic postganglionic neurons usually use ACh. There are exceptions, including sympathetic signalling to sweat glands. You need the main pattern without turning it into an absolute rule.

At the adrenal medulla, sympathetic input triggers release of epinephrine and norepinephrine into the bloodstream. In this setting they act as hormones and can influence distant target tissues. A hormone travelling in blood and a neurotransmitter released into a synaptic cleft use different delivery routes, even when a chemical name overlaps.
