## The electrical signal reaches a gap

An action potential can travel along one continuous axon membrane. At a **chemical synapse**, however, the sending and receiving cells have separate membranes. The action potential does not simply leap across the fluid-filled gap. The sending neuron converts its arriving electrical event into release of a chemical messenger.

The sending side is **presynaptic**; the receiving side is **postsynaptic**. The junction is the synapse, and the gap itself is the **synaptic cleft**. The messenger is a **neurotransmitter**. A synapse from a motor neuron to a muscle fibre is a **neuromuscular junction**.

Think of three connected events: an electrical signal arrives, a chemical messenger crosses the cleft, and the receiving cell's activity changes. The cells do not share one action potential. A new one can be generated in the receiving cell if the conditions for it are met.

## Follow release, diffusion, binding, and clearance

[![Chemical transmission at a neuron-to-neuron synapse compared with a neuromuscular junction.](../review-assets/chapter11-synapses-0.webp)](#ch11-embedded-image-8)


**Figure 9.** Trace chemical transmission at a neuron-to-neuron synapse and compare it with a neuromuscular junction.

Begin at the axon entering the top of panel A. The large arrow follows the arriving action potential toward the terminal. The terminal boundary separates the presynaptic cytoplasm from the cleft. The boundary below the gap belongs to the receiving cell. Small messenger dots cross the gap; the vesicles that held them do not travel into the next cell.

1. **The action potential depolarizes the terminal.** This voltage change opens voltage-gated calcium channels there. Na⁺ dominated the rising phase along the axon; Ca²⁺ has a different job at release.
2. **Ca²⁺ enters the terminal.** Its entry triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane.
3. **Vesicles release neurotransmitter by exocytosis.** Fusion opens the vesicle to the cleft, so its chemical contents are released outside the sending cell.
4. **Neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft.** Molecules move through the fluid to the receiving membrane.
5. **Binding changes the receiving cell's activity.** A messenger binds a suitable receptor. This can change ion-channel activity directly or through processes inside the cell. The resulting postsynaptic effect may promote or oppose firing.
6. **The signal is cleared.** Neurotransmitter can be removed by reuptake, enzymatic breakdown, or diffusion away. Clearance lets signalling end and allows later messages to remain distinct.

The blue channels at the bottom of panel A represent receptor-linked ion pathways. The final large arrow is a possible continuation, **if threshold is reached**. One arriving input does not guarantee a new action potential.

Panel B ends on a muscle fibre rather than another neuron. The axon terminal releases acetylcholine onto the muscle membrane. Activation of the muscle's receptors depolarizes it and normally initiates the events leading to contraction. The pink receiving structure is an effector, not a dendrite.

## The messenger and the enzyme have different jobs

**Acetylcholine (ACh)** is a neurotransmitter. At the skeletal neuromuscular junction, it activates receptors that allow a depolarizing membrane response and normally trigger muscle excitation. **Cholinesterase**—specifically acetylcholinesterase at this junction—is an enzyme that breaks down ACh. It helps end the message.

These are not two opposing neurotransmitters. One carries a signal; the other catalyzes its breakdown. For ACh, breakdown products can be taken up and reused. For transmitters such as norepinephrine and dopamine, reuptake of the transmitter itself is an important removal mechanism. Do not assume that every transmitter is cleared in exactly the same way.

### Worked example: an impulse arrives but no message is released

Suppose the action potential reaches a terminal normally, but its voltage-gated calcium channels cannot open. Predict what happens next.

1. **Keep the successful event.** Conduction along the axon occurred; there is no evidence of a failure there.
2. **Locate the blocked event.** Without the normal Ca²⁺ entry, the usual trigger for vesicle fusion is missing.
3. **Follow the consequence across the gap.** Much less, or no evoked, transmitter is released, so the postsynaptic receptors do not receive the usual chemical signal.
4. **Limit the conclusion.** The receiving cell might still respond to a different input. This result concerns communication through the affected terminal.

### Which side changed?

## Inputs can move a neuron toward or away from firing

An **excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)** makes an action potential more likely. In a common example, channels allow a net inward movement of positive charge, producing a small depolarization toward threshold. An **inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)** makes firing less likely. In the simple examples used here, K⁺ leaves or Cl⁻ enters, making the inside more negative.

The effect depends on the receiving membrane and its receptors. “Excitatory” and “inhibitory” describe effects at a particular synapse. They are not permanent labels that explain every action of a transmitter throughout the body. Inhibition can also work without a large visible hyperpolarization, by reducing the effect of excitatory current.

## Summation asks what the inputs do together

**Spatial summation** combines inputs from different synapses close enough in time for their effects to overlap. **Temporal summation** combines repeated input from a synapse before the earlier effects have faded. In each case, the net effect at the trigger region determines whether threshold is reached.

[![Spatial summation combines different inputs; temporal summation combines repeated inputs close together in time.](../review-assets/chapter11-synapses-2.svg)](#ch11-embedded-image-10)


**Figure 11.** Spatial summation combines different inputs; temporal summation combines repeated inputs close together in time.

On the left, several input routes converge: that spatial arrangement represents different presynaptic sources. On the right, closely repeated inputs represent a timing effect. The converging arrows do not mean that action potentials merge into a larger spike. Postsynaptic effects combine; the resulting axonal action potential remains all-or-none.

### Worked model: include inhibition in the calculation

Use a simplified additive model at the trigger region. Resting voltage is −70 mV, threshold is −55 mV, and simultaneous contributions are +8 mV, +9 mV, and −5 mV.

1. **Add all contributions.** +8 + 9 − 5 = +12 mV. Leaving out the inhibitory term would overestimate the net depolarization.
2. **Apply the net change to the starting value.** −70 + 12 = −58 mV.
3. **Compare with threshold.** −58 is still below −55, so no action potential is initiated in this model.
4. **Test the changed condition.** Removing the −5 mV input would give a predicted depolarization to −53 mV, which crosses threshold and triggers an action potential. −53 mV is the model's threshold-crossing prediction, not the voltage at which the neuron would remain afterward.

Real synapses interact in more complex ways than simple addition. The calculation is a model for reasoning about combined effects, timing, and threshold; it is not a universal equation for a real neuron.

## Learn the chemical names with a useful example

| Chemical | Example role | What to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acetylcholine | Excites skeletal muscle at the neuromuscular junction; also used in CNS and autonomic pathways | A messenger whose effect depends on the receptor. It can help slow the heart in a different pathway. |
| Cholinesterase | Breaks down acetylcholine | An enzyme that helps terminate a signal, not a neurotransmitter. |
| Norepinephrine | Used in CNS signalling and by many sympathetic neurons | Connect it with alertness and sympathetic responses; the specific target determines the effect. |
| GABA | A major inhibitory transmitter in the mature CNS | Often reduces the likelihood of firing through receptor-dependent effects on ion movement. |
| Dopamine | Contributes to movement control and reward-related learning | It is not a universal “excitatory chemical” or a complete explanation of pleasure or addiction. |
| Serotonin | Contributes to regulation of mood, sleep, and other functions | Its effects vary across receptors and circuits. |
| Glutamate and endorphins | Glutamate commonly supports excitation; endorphins help modulate pain signalling | Use the role in the specific pathway rather than treating the name as a complete mechanism. |

Give particular attention to acetylcholine, norepinephrine, and cholinesterase, and be able to use GABA in an inhibitory example. Epinephrine can act as a chemical signal too; when the adrenal medulla releases it into blood, it is functioning as a hormone.
