<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l05-start"><h2>How can a brief voltage change travel a long distance?</h2><p>A resting axon has more Na⁺ outside, more K⁺ inside, and a membrane potential often represented as −70 mV. The next step is a temporary change in the membrane's permeability. An <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="action potential" data-bio-term-route="lesson-05" type="button">action potential</button></strong> is a rapid sequence of voltage changes produced by controlled ion movement through membrane channels.</p><p>We will first study one small region of membrane over time. Only after that will we follow the signal along the axon. A graph showing one region changing with time is not a picture of sodium ions travelling from the cell body to a muscle.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l05-threshold"><h2>Reaching threshold starts a self-reinforcing rise</h2><p>Incoming stimulation can make the inside less negative: the membrane depolarizes. Some changes are too small to initiate an action potential. In our common example, the membrane must reach a <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="threshold" data-bio-term-route="lesson-05" type="button">threshold</button></strong> near −55 mV at the trigger region.</p><p>At threshold, enough voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are activated for Na⁺ entry to produce further depolarization, which opens more Na⁺ channels. This self-reinforcing process produces the rapid rising phase. Na⁺ is driven inward by its electrochemical gradient; bringing positive charge in makes the inside more positive relative to outside.</p><p><strong>Depolarization and an action potential are not synonyms.</strong> A small change from −70 to −65 mV is depolarization, but it does not reach a −55 mV threshold. An action potential is the full triggered event, including its rise and recovery. “No action potential” does not mean “no membrane change at all.”</p></section>

<p>In panel A, the horizontal axis is time in milliseconds and the vertical axis is membrane potential in millivolts. The resting line is about −70 mV and the dashed threshold line is about −55 mV. The peak in this illustration is labelled about +30 mV. Other examples may use +35 or +40 mV; these are representative values, not a different underlying process.</p><p>Read the rising section before the falling section. The lower panels show the associated membrane events. Their arrows show ions crossing a small membrane patch. They do not show the direction that the whole impulse travels along an axon.</p><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Resting state:</strong> the gradients are already present. Voltage-gated channels are not producing an action potential, although leak channels and pumps continue working.</li><li><strong>Rapid depolarization:</strong> Na⁺ permeability rises as voltage-gated sodium channels open. Na⁺ enters, making the inside less negative and then positive relative to outside.</li><li><strong>The rise stops:</strong> sodium channels become inactivated. Their temporary inability to conduct is different from simply being closed and ready to open. Delayed opening of voltage-gated K⁺ channels also changes the balance of current.</li><li><strong>Repolarization:</strong> K⁺ moves outward through those channels. Positive charge leaves, so the membrane potential falls toward its resting value.</li><li><strong>Hyperpolarization:</strong> some K⁺ channels remain open briefly, allowing an undershoot below the resting potential. As they close and normal resting conductances dominate again, the voltage returns toward resting level.</li></ol><p>The sodium–potassium pump maintains the ion gradients over time. It is not the main mechanism producing the rapid falling phase. Also, only a small fraction of the available ions crosses during an ordinary action potential: <strong>the voltage reverses, but the overall Na⁺ and K⁺ concentration gradients do not exchange sides.</strong></p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l05-worked"><h3>Worked example: name the event from the evidence</h3><p>A membrane record starts at −70 mV, rises through −55 mV, peaks above zero, falls, and briefly reaches −80 mV. At the falling part of the curve, what movement of ions best explains the change?</p><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Locate the phase.</strong> We are after the peak and moving toward a more negative inside voltage.</li><li><strong>Choose a charge movement that fits.</strong> Positive charge leaving the cell would lower its inside voltage.</li><li><strong>Match that movement to the channel sequence.</strong> Voltage-gated K⁺ channels are open and K⁺ moves out; Na⁺ channels are inactivated.</li><li><strong>Interpret the undershoot separately.</strong> Continued K⁺ permeability briefly takes the voltage below the usual resting level. That is hyperpolarization, not a second action potential.</li></ol><p>If you had answered “the pump pushes all the sodium back out,” you would have confused gradual maintenance of gradients with the fast channel events on the graph.</p></section>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l05-refractory"><h2>Recovery makes another immediate impulse impossible</h2><p>During the <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="absolute refractory period" data-bio-term-route="lesson-05" type="button">absolute refractory period</button></strong>, the affected membrane region cannot generate another action potential, even with a stronger stimulus. Many sodium channels are inactivated and must recover before they can participate again.</p><p>During the later <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="relative refractory period" data-bio-term-route="lesson-05" type="button">relative refractory period</button></strong>, some channels have recovered, but a stronger-than-usual stimulus is needed. Continuing K⁺ conductance and the more negative voltage can make reaching threshold harder. These periods overlap the later phases of the voltage event; recovery is not simply a switch that flips when the graph first touches −70 mV.</p><p>The refractory period limits how closely action potentials can follow one another. It also helps explain normal forward propagation. Local current from an active region can spread in both directions, but the region just behind the travelling impulse is recovering. The ready region ahead can reach threshold and regenerate the signal.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l05-propagation"><h2>Regeneration keeps the signal from fading</h2><p>In an unmyelinated axon, depolarization at one region produces local current that brings the next region to threshold. The next region then generates its own action potential. The signal is repeatedly renewed; one weak voltage change is not simply drifting all the way to the terminal.</p><p>In a myelinated axon, current spreads along the axon under the insulation and the next node regenerates the action potential. Return to the myelin comparison if needed: the axon is continuous, but its exposed regeneration sites are spaced apart. Sodium ions are not required to travel the entire length of the axon.</p><p>Normal impulses initiated near an axon's beginning travel toward its terminals. If an experiment stimulates an otherwise resting axon in its middle, both neighbouring directions can initially contain ready membrane. The usual one-way result depends on where an impulse starts and which regions are refractory; it is not an absolute rule that local electrical current can only move in one direction.</p>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l05-intensity"><h2>A stronger stimulus changes firing patterns, not spike height</h2><p><strong>All-or-none</strong> means that a ready membrane produces a full action potential once threshold is reached. Under the same conditions, a stronger stimulus does not make that neuron's individual spikes proportionally taller. An intense stimulus can instead increase <strong>firing frequency</strong> and recruit <strong>more neurons</strong>.</p>

<p>Compare equal lengths of time in the two rows. The heights remain the same, but the lower row contains more spikes. That is increased frequency. Recruitment would require evidence that additional neurons are active; you cannot infer it just by counting spikes from one neuron.</p><h3>Worked comparison: two recordings from the same neuron</h3><p><strong>Use these supplied teaching-model numbers, not experimental measurements.</strong> Suppose one 100 ms interval contains 2 spikes and another contains 5. Because 100 ms is 0.10 s, the frequencies are 2 ÷ 0.10 = <strong>20 impulses/s</strong> and 5 ÷ 0.10 = <strong>50 impulses/s</strong>. If the spike heights are unchanged, the evidence supports a frequency increase. It does not show that each impulse became stronger, or that more neurons fired.</p><p>In a whole muscle, recruiting more motor units (motor neurons together with the muscle fibres they control) and increasing firing frequency can increase force. In a simplified experiment with <em>one</em> motor neuron, a fixed muscle preparation, one isolated pulse per trial, and recovery between trials, an above-threshold pulse does not become a larger neuronal action potential when its stimulus voltage is increased. Always identify what the experiment holds constant.</p>
