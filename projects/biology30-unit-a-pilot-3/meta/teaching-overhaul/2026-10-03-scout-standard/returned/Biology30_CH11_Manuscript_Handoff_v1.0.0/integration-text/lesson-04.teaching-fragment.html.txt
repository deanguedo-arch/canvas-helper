<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l04-start"><h2>A resting neuron is ready, not switched off</h2><p>A neuron can send a signal within milliseconds, but it cannot create the conditions for that signal from nothing. Before an impulse begins, its membrane already separates different ion concentrations and a small amount of electrical charge. This stored electrochemical difference makes a rapid response possible.</p><p>Picture a very small patch of axon membrane. There is fluid on the inside and fluid on the outside. An <strong>ion</strong> is a charged particle: sodium, Na⁺, and potassium, K⁺, each carry one positive charge. Large negatively charged molecules are also present inside. Both positive and negative particles occur on both sides; the important voltage difference comes from a small imbalance near the membrane.</p><p>The membrane is a boundary. Its lipid interior prevents ions from passing freely, while specific proteins provide routes across. That <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="selective permeability" data-bio-term-route="lesson-04" type="button">selective permeability</button></strong> lets the cell maintain different conditions on its two sides.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l04-voltage"><h2>What does −70 mV actually say?</h2><p><strong>Membrane potential</strong> is the voltage inside relative to outside. We use the outside as our reference. A reading of <strong>−70 mV</strong> means that the inside is 70 millivolts lower in electrical potential than the outside. It does not mean there are no positive ions inside, or that the entire cell is filled with a large net negative charge.</p><p>A typical example of a <strong><button aria-haspopup="dialog" class="bio-term" data-bio-term="resting membrane potential" data-bio-term-route="lesson-04" type="button">resting membrane potential</button></strong> is −70 mV. Actual values vary among cells. “Resting” means the membrane is not producing an action potential at that time. Ions still move through channels, and the cell still uses ATP, a molecule that transfers energy for cellular work such as active transport.</p><h3>Worked example: read a change before naming it</h3><p>A recording changes from −70 mV to −60 mV. Has the inside become more negative or less negative?</p><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Keep the reference fixed.</strong> Both readings describe inside relative to outside.</li><li><strong>Compare the values.</strong> −60 is higher than −70, so the voltage rose by 10 mV.</li><li><strong>Interpret the sign.</strong> The inside is still negative relative to outside, but it is less negative than before.</li><li><strong>Name the change.</strong> Becoming less negative is depolarization. It does not yet tell us whether an action potential occurred; we need the threshold and subsequent events.</li></ol><p>From −70 mV to −80 mV would be the opposite: the inside becomes more negative. Read the numbers before using words such as “higher,” “lower,” or “more charged.”</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l04-gradients"><h2>Three connected conditions produce the resting state</h2><p><strong>First, ions are distributed unequally.</strong> There is a higher concentration of Na⁺ outside the neuron and a higher concentration of K⁺ inside. A <strong>concentration gradient</strong> is a difference in concentration between places. If a suitable route is open, that difference tends to drive net movement from higher toward lower concentration.</p><p><strong>Second, the resting membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺.</strong> Potassium can leave through leak channels more readily than sodium can enter. When positive K⁺ leaves, the inside becomes more negative relative to outside. The increasingly negative interior attracts positive ions back, opposing further net K⁺ loss. This electrical influence and the concentration difference act together; ions do not respond to concentration alone. Large negatively charged molecules that cannot leave also contribute to the conditions inside.</p><p><strong>Third, active transport maintains the gradients.</strong> The sodium–potassium pump uses ATP to move <strong>3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in</strong> per cycle. These movements are against their concentration gradients. The pump continually offsets leakage and the ion movements associated with signalling, keeping the cell ready over time.</p>

<p>Start with the membrane running horizontally across the picture. Above it is extracellular fluid; below it is intracellular fluid. Orange Na⁺ symbols are more common outside and purple K⁺ symbols are more common inside. The symbols show relative concentrations, not a literal count of all the ions in a cell.</p><p>Now read each arrow separately. The sodium leak arrow points inward; the potassium leak arrow points outward. These channels allow passive movement. At the larger pump, three Na⁺ arrows point out while two K⁺ arrows point in. That protein uses ATP. The −70 mV label describes the resulting electrical state; it is not the voltage of an individual ion.</p><p><strong>The distinction to keep:</strong> channels provide routes for ions to move down electrochemical gradients; the pump uses energy to maintain concentration gradients. The resting membrane's greater permeability to K⁺ is a major immediate contributor to resting voltage. The pump is essential, but its 3-to-2 exchange alone is not the whole explanation.</p></section>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l04-energy"><h2>What would happen if ATP became unavailable?</h2><p>Separate an immediate event from a longer-term effect. If pumping stops, the existing gradients do not disappear at the same instant. There are still different ion concentrations, and ions can still move through channels. As movement continues without active maintenance, the gradients become harder to sustain, and normal signalling eventually fails.</p><p>This explains why “resting” tissue still needs a supply of energy. Maintaining readiness is work. It also prepares us for the next lesson: the rapid upward and downward changes during an action potential are caused mainly by changes in channel permeability, not by a pump suddenly turning on and off.</p>
