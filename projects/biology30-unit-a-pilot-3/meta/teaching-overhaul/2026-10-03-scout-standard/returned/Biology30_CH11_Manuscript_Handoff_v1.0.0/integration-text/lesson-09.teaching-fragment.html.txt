<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l09-jobs"><h2>The spinal cord does more than relay messages</h2><p>A spinal withdrawal reflex can begin without waiting for a conscious decision, yet a voluntary command from the brain may travel through the same spinal cord. These observations reveal two main jobs: <strong>carrying information between the brain and body</strong>, and <strong>processing information in local circuits</strong>.</p><p>The CNS consists of the brain and spinal cord. It integrates sensory input, organizes output, and contributes to behaviour, conscious experience, and homeostasis. Different regions contribute in different ways. The consequences of damage depend on which cells and connections are affected, not simply on how large the structure looks.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l09-tissue"><h2>Tissue composition explains the grey–white distinction</h2><p><strong>Grey matter</strong> contains many neuron cell bodies, dendrites, synapses, and unmyelinated portions of axons, together with glial cells. It provides many sites of local processing. <strong>White matter</strong> contains many myelinated axons grouped into tracts that connect regions. It also contains supporting cells. These descriptions are about the main composition; neither tissue contains only one kind of structure.</p>

<p>Look at the cerebral cross-section first: the outer cortex is grey matter, with white matter deeper inside. Now compare the spinal cross-section: its butterfly-shaped central region is grey matter, surrounded by white matter. The colours and boundaries show tissue arrangement, not direction of signalling. The brain also contains deeper clusters of grey matter, so “all grey outside, all white inside” is not a rule for every brain structure.</p><p>This organization makes functional sense. Spinal grey matter contains connections used in local circuits. Axons in surrounding white matter carry information between levels of the cord and the brain. In the cerebrum, the folded cortex provides a large area for processing, while white-matter connections allow regions to communicate.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l09-injury"><h2>Follow pathways through the cord before predicting an injury's effects</h2><p>Sensory information can travel upward in <strong>ascending pathways</strong>. Commands from the brain can travel downward in <strong>descending pathways</strong>. Peripheral nerves connect these CNS pathways to body regions. In a simple spinal-root model, sensory fibres enter dorsally and motor fibres leave ventrally; a spinal nerve can carry both.</p><h3>Worked example: a complete interruption of a pathway</h3><p>Assume a model spinal injury completely interrupts the ascending and descending routes serving a leg. The peripheral nerves and leg muscles are otherwise intact.</p><ol class="pro-steps"><li><strong>Follow sensory input.</strong> Signals may enter spinal tissue below the interruption, but they cannot pass the break to reach the brain by the affected ascending route. Normal conscious sensation through that route is lost.</li><li><strong>Follow voluntary output.</strong> Commands generated above the interruption cannot reach the leg through the affected descending route. Intact muscle alone cannot restore that connection.</li><li><strong>Consider local circuits separately.</strong> Some reflex circuitry below the injury may remain anatomically intact. Its presence is different from an intact connection to the brain; real reflex activity after injury can also change over time.</li><li><strong>State the model's limit.</strong> Real injuries can be incomplete and can affect different pathways unequally. The assumption of a complete interruption is what supports the strong prediction here.</li></ol><p>A higher spinal injury can interrupt pathways serving more body regions than a lower injury because more routes still need to pass through that level. The <strong>spinal cord</strong> is nervous tissue. A <strong>vertebra</strong> is bone surrounding its canal; damage to a bone and damage to the cord are related possibilities, not identical terms.</p></section>

<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l09-protection"><h2>Different protective structures solve different problems</h2>

<p>Read this diagram from the bone toward the nervous tissue. The skull protects the brain and the vertebral column protects the spinal cord. Inside are three <strong>meninges</strong>: outer dura mater, arachnoid, and inner pia mater. The pia closely follows the nervous tissue. The bands separate the layers for clarity; their drawn thicknesses are not to scale.</p><p><strong>Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)</strong> occupies spaces including the ventricles within the brain and the subarachnoid space between arachnoid and pia. It provides buoyancy and cushioning and contributes to the chemical environment around the CNS. Bone resists external forces, membranes enclose and support the tissue, and fluid helps cushion it. These forms of protection do not make the CNS immune to injury.</p><p>The <strong>blood–brain barrier</strong> solves a different problem. Tight junctions between cells lining brain capillaries restrict movement of many substances from blood into brain tissue. Needed materials can still cross by appropriate routes: oxygen diffuses and glucose uses transport proteins. Some substances also pass readily, so the barrier is selective rather than an absolute seal.</p><p>Neurons need oxygen and nutrients to support ATP production. ATP maintains ion gradients and other cell processes even between impulses. An interruption of blood supply therefore threatens the conditions required for normal signalling. The barrier must control exchange while still permitting supply.</p></section>
<section class="content-section pro-teaching" id="pro-l09-csf"><h3>Why a CSF sample can provide different evidence from blood</h3><p>Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges, commonly due to infection. CSF is in direct contact with the spaces surrounding these tissues, so its cells, chemistry, and possible infectious agents can provide evidence about conditions there. Blood is a different compartment. It can provide useful evidence too, but it does not necessarily contain the same findings as CSF.</p><p>The biological reasoning is about sampling the relevant compartment. It is too strong to conclude that an ordinary blood sample never provides information about an infection, or that the blood–brain barrier prevents all infection.</p></section>
