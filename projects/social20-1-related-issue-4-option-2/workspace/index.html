Essential Question:

  • What is meant by "embracing" identity?

Embracing is defined as:

  • welcoming and taking advantage of something eagerly and willingly
  • adopting something, such as a belief or way of life

In the image here you see a little girl waving a Canadian flag. She is embracing the idea of being Canadian. But how does an individual or group decide to embrace nationalism? That is the question you will be exploring in this unit.

You have already explored how nations have acted as they pursued their national interests and you have seen how national interests can lead to conflict between nations. By this point in the course, you hopefully understand that acting on national interests is a big decision.

Embracing nationalism is also a big decision. Like any other commitment in your life, if you do not believe in the ideas or principles behind a concept (for example, marriage, democracy, life-long learning, and independence) you will not really embrace the concept.

Nations cannot survive unless their citizens embrace nationalism - at least to some extent.  Canada’s survival as a nation depends on citizens such as you embracing nationalism. When citizens embrace Canadian civic nationalism they vote, live in accordance with Canada’s laws, and proudly declare their Canadian citizenship. By doing these things, Canadians tell others that Canada is a unique nation and that they believe in what it stands for.

Embracing nationalism is a complex process. Canada has adopted bilingualism and multiculturalism. This means that Canada has two official languages and that diverse cultural identities are supported and protected by our laws.  This ensures rights and freedoms and preservation of culture, but it sometimes conflicts with the notion of being Canadian.

But first, let's take a look at what the notion of "being Canadian" has meant in the past.

The Vision of the Fathers of Confederation: Political Union and Federalism

The Fathers of Confederation were politicians from the British Canadian colonies who sought a union of the colonies in the 1860s. Each of the Fathers had regional, political, and economic reasons for wanting a union but an overwhelming factor was nationalism. This desire to unify the provinces (British colonies) into a single independent country was shared not only by the Fathers but by many citizens of the colonies as well. As a Toronto Globe newspaper editorial (Jan 2, 1865) put it, "The very thought of Confederation should make the heart of each Canadian leap with joy, for it is the beginning of nationality, a declaration of our right to take our place among the nations of the world."

External factors driving confederation included the US policy of Manifest Destiny (see Unit 2): The War of 1812, the American Civil War and American imperialism all contributed to the desire to unify the Canadian provinces (British colonies) into a single country to avoid being absorbed by the United States.

John A. MacDonald's political vision of Canada included but was not limited to these two main pillars:

  1. Unification of all the British colonies in North America as one country from coast to coast. One way to promote a sense of Canadian unity across thousands of kilometres was the building of the Canadian Pacific Railway. This provided an actual physical link between distant regions, encouraging East/West trade rather than North/South (the latter would encourage dependence on US trade).
  2. The provinces would be subordinate to a strong central government. This was known as federalism.

The division of powers between the federal and provincial governments continues to be a source of debate today. Although Macdonald wanted to ensure the strong central government version of federalism, it has never fully taken hold in Canada because of regional interests and consistent demands by the provinces for more powers.

As a result, federal/provincial relations have become complex and many-sided. Since Confederation there have been First Ministers' Conferences held every other year to help navigate the federal/provincial relationship. First Minister's Conferences are policy meetings between the provincial premiers, recently also including territorial premiers, and the Prime Minister.

What was his vision of Canada with respect to the monarchy and full sovereignty? Voting rights? French? Assimilation? Immigration? Peace, Order and Good Government?

George-Etienne Cartier

George-Etienne Cartier was another Father of Confederation and ally of John A. MacDonald. Cartier originally wanted strong provinces and a weak central government but he eventually came to agree with MacDonald's vision of a strong central government. Cartier also sought the re-establishment of a seperate province of Quebec in order to protect the French Canadian culture. He succeeded in having the province of Canada divided into two provinces, Ontario and Quebec.

Confederation: 1867

London Conference, December 1866-January 1867:

  • Representatives from New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and the Province of Canada went to England and worked out the final documents for Confederation with the British government.

  • A constitution for the new Canada, the British North America Act (BNA Act) was created.

  • Queen Victoria of England gave her royal assent to the BNA Act in March and the British Parliament approved it shortly after. Confederation was a reality.

The Vision of Clifford Sifton: Settlement of the West

Clifford Sifton
Source: Library and Archives Canada/PA-027942

Clifford Sifton was elected to Parliament in 1896 and became Minister of the Interior. He was responsible for promoting the agricultural settlement of the Western provinces.

Sifton wanted to attract British and American settlers in order to maintain the British identity of Canada but the need for settlers was greater than what Britain and the USA could supply. Sifton reluctantly expanded his aggressive recruitment and advertising campaign to include other nationalities from Europe (why only Europe?), especially Northern Europeans. Nevertheless, these nationalities faced discrimination once in Canada their cultures differed from that of the British.

At the same time, Asian immigration was discouraged by increasing the Head Tax. Canadian workers of European descent complained that Asian immigrants were stealing jobs from European Canadians.

Federalism in Canada

Federalism is a method of promoting a national identity in a large and diverse country. All levels of government have their powers outlined in the Canadian constitution. Federalism aims to account for regional (provincial) differences, yet still maintain national unity. The BNA Act was passed to set the legal ground rules for Canada, and divvy up the powers between the provinces and the federal government. The BNA Act was the basis for our Confederation, and it outlined the division of powers which underpins our federal system.

Generally, federalism gives the following levels of government jurisdiction or legal authority over specific areas:

  • Federal Government: matters concerning the entire country: taxes, coastal fisheries, health care, criminal law, currency and banking, armed forces and RCMP, foreign affairs, postal service, and any area not specifically controlled by the provinces.

  • Provincial and Territorial Governments: matters concerning their particular parts of the country: civil law, taxes, hospitals, education, natural resources, driver's licences, provincial courts.

  • Municipal Governments (cities and townships): matters concerning cities and municipalities: bylaws, water and sewage, fire department.

While the above division of federal/provincial/municipal powers might seem concrete, there is overlap in some areas. This overlap in powers has led to confrontation between the provinces and the federal government.

Federalism and Provincial Autonomy

Concerns over provincial autonomy have always been expressed by Quebec because of a sense of alienation from English Canada and the desire to protect the French language and culture. As a minority in Canada, French Canadians have always maintained the need for control over legislation that will preserve their unique culture. Western alienation has also been a problem, particularly in Alberta.

The Constitution Act also entrenched the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, a very important document that protects Canadians and gives them the right to freedoms that are at the core of the Canadian national identity as a democratic nation.

Historical Visions of French Canada: Sovereignty and protection of a distinct language and culture versus assimilation

 

Sovereignty has been applied to Quebec’s status in Canada to indicate a political movement dedicated to creating independent statehood or greater autonomy within Confederation. In many sources referring to Quebec’s sovereignty movement, the terms separatism and independence have been used to indicate the sovereignty movement in Quebec. 

The sovereignty movement in Quebec did not develop instantly. As Canada has grown and evolved, the Francophone or French-speaking majority in Quebec has become increasingly concerned with maintaining its French-Canadian culture.

New France

Quebec has had a long history in Canada. Explorers like Jacques Cartier (1534) and Samuel de Champlain (1608) started travel and trade that led to the settlement of New France.

Image source: John Henry de Rinzy
National Archives of Canada C-013320

British Conquest and Assimilation

The long struggle to protect the French colony and the fur trade from the Iroquois and the British was effectively lost in 1759, when the British defeated the French on the Plains of Abraham.

Britain withdrew the privileged status of the Catholic Church and ended French civil law. British soldiers were expected to settle in Québec in large numbers, and ultimately assimilate the French Canadian population. It was presumed the French Canadians would want to become English.

Image source: Atwater Library

The struggle for French Canadian rights began

In an attempt to ensure the French inhabitants would remain loyal to Britain and not get involved in the unrest growing in the colonies to the south (eventually the American Revolution), the British passed the Quebec Act of 1774, under which the colony was allowed to continue its system of land use and to retain its language, religion, legal system, and customs.

The struggle for self-government

Louis Papineau, a member of the legislative assembly of Lower Canada, demanded responsible government for all citizens. The British rejected his demands and he led an armed rebellion in 1837-38. Ten years later, avoiding violence, LaFonataine and Baldwin succeeded in bringing responsible government to Canada.

Image source: Atwater Library

Assimilation

Although the rebellion was crushed, the disturbances in Upper and Lower Canada caused the British to send Lord Durham to study conditions in the British North American colonies. They didn’t want more rebellions - or another American Revolution!

Durham's report led ultimately to internal self-government and the creation of the Canadian confederation, but it caused a huge flap before that happened.

In Lower Canada, Durham described the problems as racially, not politically, based. He found "two nations warring in the bosom of a single state." To ensure harmony and progress, he recommended assimilating the French Canadians, whom he called "a people with no literature and no history," through a legislative union of the Canadas, with responsible government dominated by an English-speaking majority.

Image source: Library and Archives Canada

Watch this CBC Digital Archives video about Rene Levesque and the Parti Quebecois demand for greater autonomy, if not full independence:

Parti Quebecois: Sovereignty through poltics

In the 1960s Quebec's “Quiet Revolution” began. Within this revolution in thinking, the concept of Deux Nations (Two Nations) developed. It was generally a move to gain more decision making powers from the federal government for Quebec so that Quebec could be “master in its own house”. The Deux Nations concept was accepted by many federalists with the exception of John Diefenbaker and Pierre Trudeau who said that Quebec was “a province like all the others” and did not need extra powers.

In keeping with the political and social climate of the 1960s, Quebecois (particularly young adults) questioned the status quo. Many Quebecois activists organized themselves into political parties aimed at formalizing Quebec’s unique character in Canada, like the Parti Quebecois. The majority of Francophone political activists did not believe that Quebec should continue to be a province in Canada and advocated the creation of a separate state or nation called Quebec.

FLQ: Sovereignty through violence

One extremist group, the Front de liberation du Quebec (FLQ), decided to use violence (bombings, bank robberies and kidnappings) in the 1960s to reach its goal of Quebec independence, eventually leading to the October Crisis of 1970, with the deployment of the Canadian army on city streets, the temporary suspension of some legal rights, and the end of the FLQ as a movement.

Historical Visions of the First Nations and Metis in Canada: Assimilation versus protection of culture; External control versus self-determination

Before colonization by Europeans, First Nations people had their own systems of government. Leaders were elected, issues debated and resolved just as governments today debate and resolve issues. The First Nations developed codes or laws to live by that were based on their particular community's values and beliefs. These laws helped to ensure the survival of the community. These laws are contained in the oral history of First Nations peoples, and this establishes what is called the inherent right to self-government in Canada today.

However, the European colonizers believed it was their “duty” to spread their culture to the peoples they came into contact with. European imperialists did not think the Aboriginal people already had cultural systems that had worked well before the Europeans interfered with them.

Assimilation

The federal government established many policies that were an attempt to assimilate the First Nations’ peoples into the larger Canadian society. The Canadian government's assimilation policies were part of the imperialist belief system that European culture was “better” than other cultures. It also reflected an ideological belief that individual rights were more important than collective rights.

Internet - Examples of Assimilation Policies of the Canadian government:

  • Residential schools were established to remove children from their families and First Nations communities in order to assimilate them into European culture and deny their own culture. This policy has been referred to as "cultural genocide" - a planned policy of destroying a culture to assmilate Aboriginal Canadians.

In the West Coast First Nations Potlatch ceremony, individual property is given away to the community. This ancient tradition was banned because it interfered with the capitalist system of the market and private property and individual accumulation of wealth.

Canada has always been a country of immigrants. Immigrants arrived from Britain and France, then from the USA, later from the rest of Europe, and eventually from all over the world.

Why do people want to come to Canada? Some are forced to leave their own country because of war, political persecution or acts of genocide. Others may come for better economic opportunities. Many are attracted by aspects of Canadian civic nationalism, such as:

  • equality rights (protection against discrimination)
  • democratic rights (voting)
  • legal rights (fair trial)
  • mobility rights (live and work anywhere in Canada)

In 1971 - in response to the findings of a Royal Commission on Biblingualism and Biculturalism (B & B Commission) and a recognition of the reality of the growing cultural diversity in Canada - the federal government, under Liberal Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau, announced a multiculturalism policy.

In 1982, when the Constitution was repatriated, Canada's multicultural identity was formally recognized in the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms - section 27 of the Charter states:

This Charter shall be interpreted in a manner consistent with the preservation and enhancement of the multicultural heritage of Canadians.

The Meaning of First Nations Self-Government

Today’s Aboriginal people are working towards self-government in their communities so they can function as other Canadian communities do. Aboriginal people have a desire to develop independent communities that meet the needs of their people without interference from the federal government.

The basic consensus among Aboriginal people is that self-government will replace outdated sections of the Indian Act and allow them to live more independently. The federal government's viewpoint of self-government is that it is a handing over of formerly federal jurisdiction for certain areas to local communities. In all cases, the federal government has committed itself to negotiating with individual Aboriginal groups to determine just what self-government means in those communities.

Self-government does not mean that First Nations will operate as independent countries. Rather, self-government is best described as follows:

  • First Nations’ communities will continue to interact with their neighbouring communities
  • The Canadian Constitution and Charter of Rights and Freedoms will apply to First Nations' governments as well
  • Provincial and federal laws must be followed, especially the Criminal Code
  • First Nations’, municipal, provincial and federal governments are in a relationship of equals (but with different responsibilities)
  • First Nations’ people receive the same services and opportunities that exist for other Canadians
  • First Nations’ practices and institutions are a key part of their governments
  • First Nations’ governments create laws and services that directly meet the needs of their citizens; an outside body such as the Department of Indian and Northern Affairs cannot define what is “best for them” (as it had in the past)
  • First Nations’ cultures are preserved and are a source of pride to First Nations’ people

The federal government will maintain control over areas that are not unique to Aboriginal communities but part of national jurisdiction. In some instances federal and provincial laws affect all Canadian citizens and these will not be given to Aboriginal control. Areas under this category would include:

  • national defence
  • emergency preparedness
  • divorce
  • labour/training
  • administration of federal and constitutional law
  • penitentiaries and parole
  • environmental protection

Consensus government

The system of government in Nunavut is based on Canadian parliamentary democracy with one very significant difference - there are no political parties! This means elected members are not required to vote along party lines. In Canada's federal and provincial governments, party members are usually expected to vote with their party - even if they privately disagree with a policy. For example, all Conservatives are expected to vote in favour of a Conservative budget.

In Nunavut, members are not restricted by party loyalty. Policies are discussed and debated until a clear majority reaches agreement. If a majority agrees, then the rest of the parliament agrees to support the decision. This form of consensus government is more in keeping with Inuit traditions.

Without political parties, there is no party leader to become Premier. Instead, the elected representatives vote to decide who among themselves will be in the cabinet and who will head the territorial government.

The Oka crisis of 1989 - 1990 was touched off when local developers decided to expand the golf course in the town of Oka, just outside of Montreal. The conflict arose from a question of ownership of the land on which the golf course was to expand.

The perspective of the Mohawk
The perspective of the Town
Mohawk from the Kanesatake reserve, next to the town of Oka, claimed that the land was traditional tribal burial grounds which had been illegally sold to private persons in the 1950s. The Mohawk wanted to ensure that nothing would be built on their traditional burial grounds. Residents of the Town of Oka had legal documents showing that the land belonged to the town.

The Sovereignty Movement in Quebec: Contending Nationalist Loyalties

The sovereignty movement in Quebec began in the 1960s but, as you learned last week, tensions between English and French began long before Canada became an independent country and continued after Confederation (for example, Louis Riel; conscription during World Wars I and II). As Canada has grown, the Francophone or French-speaking majority in Quebec has become increasingly concerned with maintaining its French-Canadian culture. The first attempt at seperation from Canada occurred in 1980.

Sovereignty Association and the 1980 Quebec Referendum

Quebec nationalism can be seen in the emergence of the Parti Québécois, whose goal was to make Quebec a sovereign nation. The political success of the Parti Québécois- PQ (first elected to govern the province in 1976) presented a challenge to the entire country.

The 1980 Quebec referendum was defeated with 60% of Quebec voters choosing to reject sovereignty association. Quebecers once again voted to stay in Canada but this time it was only by the narrowest of margins (50.58% voted to stay in Canada, 49.42% voted to separate).

Distinct Society and the Meech Lake Accord - 1990

The idea of Quebec as a “distinct society” had emerged after the 1980 Quebec referendum. This idea sought to acknowledge Quebec’s unique cultural aspects (such as French language, culture, and civil law).

The Meech Lake Accord (1987-1990) was an attempt by Progressive Conservative Prime Minister Brian Mulroney to amend the 1982 Constitution to Quebec's satisfaction by acknowledging Quebec as a distinct society (also giving the other provinces more powers). The Prime Minister and provincial premiers were in agreement but the Meech Lake Accord was rejected by the Manitoba and Newfoundland legislatures, partly because the Accord was agreed without consulting aboriginal Canadians.

Multimedia - Meech Lake Accord

  • Watch this 15-minute CBC video summarizing the difficult negotiations and the eventual rejection of the Meech Lake Accord: 10 Years after the Meech Lake Accord

Charlottetown Accord - 1992

Prime Minister Brian Mulroney made a second attempt to ammend the Constitution in 1992. This time the process was more open, including consultations with a wide range of groups (such as aboriginal Canadians) and it was put to a national referendum (instead of provincial parliaments deciding). Nevertheless, this second attempt was defeated by Canadian voters in both English and French Canada.

The Bloc Quebecois

After the accords at Meech Lake and Charlottetown were defeated, the federal Bloc Québécois party was formed. Like the provincial Parti Quebecois, the Bloc Quebecois is dedicated to securing Quebec’s sovereignty but at the federal level in the House of Commons in Ottawa. The Bloc Quebecois only runs candidates from Quebec for federal elections. The idea behind this is that a group of federal Members of Parliament from Quebec would better represent the interests of the Québécois in a national forum. The Bloc Québécois has been criticized for its regional and separatist focus. Some critics argue that the presence of the Bloc Québécois interferes with the business of Parliament, which is responsible for all of Canada.

Quebec as a "Nation within a united Canada"

In 2006, attempting to close the gap between Canadian federalism and Quebecois separatist aspirations, the House of Commons passed a motion that recognized the Quebecois as a “nation within a united Canada.” Prime Minister Steven Harper, indicated that the recognition was intended to recognize Quebec’s status within Canada.

Steven Harper, Prime Minister of Canada, November 23, 2006:

"Quebecers know who they are; they know they’ve participated in the foundation, in the founding of Canada and its development and in its greatness. They know that they’ve protected their language and unique culture but they also promoted their values and interests within Canada. The real question is straightforward: Do Quebecers form a nation within a united Canada? The answer is yes. Do Quebecers form an independent nation from Canada? The answer is no and it will always be no! I say to my federalist colleagues and I also say to the separatist side that we here will do what we must, what our forefathers have always done to preserve this country, Canada: strong, united, independent, and free."

The response across Canada and in Quebec to the "nation within a united Canada" motion was varied indicating that there was no real agreement among Canadians.

What the Future Holds for Quebec in Confederation

The issues of Quebec’s separation from Canada or recognition of Quebec as a "nation within a united Canada" are ongoing and evolving. As you have learned, the debate has raged for decades and is sure to continue. Will it ever be resolved to everyone's satisfaction? What are the implications for Canadian identity?
