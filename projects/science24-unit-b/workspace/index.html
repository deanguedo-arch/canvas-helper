Start with something familiar

A bicycle at the top of a hill

A bicycle can be still yet have energy associated with its position. As it rolls downhill, the rider sees motion increase. Energy descriptions depend on the system and what can change, not only on whether something looks active.

The Sun supplies energy that becomes light, heat and, through plants, chemical energy in food. A phone battery, raised book and moving ball illustrate different stores and transfers.

Name the form and the evidence

Kinetic energy is associated with motion. Gravitational potential energy depends on position in a gravitational field; chemical energy is associated with substances and their reactions. Thermal energy relates to the microscopic motion and interactions of particles.

An energy form is not the object itself. “The battery is energy” misses the distinction: the battery stores chemical energy that can be transferred electrically. Trace what changes to choose a form.

Worked example

Trace a bicycle trip

  1. Start with chemical energy in food that supports muscle action.
  2. During the climb, the rider and bicycle gain gravitational potential energy as height increases.
  3. During a descent, some stored energy becomes kinetic energy of motion.
  4. Tires, brakes and air transfer some energy to the surroundings as thermal energy and sound.

Diagnose the reasoning

The input can be invisible

A radio uses electrical energy supplied by a battery or outlet. Its intended output is sound, but electronic components also warm. The absence of an obvious moving part does not mean no energy is transferred.

For workbook examples, write a complete chain with the object, starting store, conversion and at least one output. A label such as “solar” alone does not explain where the energy goes.

Compare a ball and an apple

A raised ball has gravitational potential energy relative to a lower position. Once released, its motion shows kinetic energy. An apple has chemical energy in its molecules as well as gravitational potential energy when held above the floor. The context decides which store a question is asking about.

If an apple is eaten, its chemical energy supports body processes. If it falls, its gravitational store changes. Do not treat one object as having only one possible energy description.

Start with something familiar

Why does a drill press feel warm?

A drill press cuts wood by turning electrical input into motor and bit motion. The tool and wood also warm, and the cutting makes sound. A useful output and a dispersed output can occur at the same time.

A chain becomes clearer when you identify the job first. For a drill, motion at the bit is useful; for an electric heater, thermal energy is useful.

Input → converter → outputs

The input is energy supplied to a system. The converter is the device or process that changes how the energy is carried. The outputs include the intended result and other transfers.

Avoid treating the converter as an energy form. “Electrical → drill press → kinetic and thermal” names each role. An engine, muscle and motor can all be converters in different chains.

Analyze a canoe trip

  1. Food supplies chemical energy to a paddler.
  2. Muscles transfer some energy to arm and paddle motion.
  3. The paddle transfers energy to water; the canoe moves.
  4. Muscles and water also warm slightly, so not every transfer becomes forward motion.

“Waste” describes usefulness

Energy dispersed as thermal energy still exists. It is called waste energy when it does not help the stated job. In a hair dryer, warm air and moving air both help dry hair; sound usually does not.

A car in sunlight can become hot because incoming light is absorbed and converted to thermal energy. That heating is an output even when no machine was switched on.

Start with something familiar

Why is a lamp warm?

A lamp receives electrical energy. Light helps with the lighting task, while much of the input in some lamps becomes heat. Feeling warmth is evidence of another output, not missing energy.

A conversion diagram needs enough output arrows to account for input energy. The proportions can change with technology and operating conditions.

Keep the energy account complete

The conservation law says total energy is transferred or transformed rather than created or destroyed. If 100 J enters a device and 35 J becomes a useful output, 65 J must leave in other forms or remain temporarily stored, given the stated system boundary.

Useful output depends on the purpose. A heater is designed to produce heat; a lamp is designed to provide light. “Waste heat” therefore depends on the task, not on whether thermal energy is intrinsically bad.

Worked example

Account for a 100 J input

  1. Define the device and surroundings as the system being described.
  2. Record 100 J of energy supplied to the device.
  3. If 35 J is useful light, subtract 35 J from 100 J to find 65 J in other outputs.
  4. Explain that the other 65 J is not destroyed; it may be dispersed as heat.

Fuel brings chemical energy. Combustion and engine parts convert some into useful vehicle motion. Exhaust, engine warming, tire friction and sound are additional pathways.

Start with something familiar

Turning a handle to light a lamp

A hand-cranked flashlight lights when its handle turns. Motion is supplied by the hand; inside, changing magnetic conditions induce an electric current. The lamp then changes electrical energy into light and heat.

This is a sequence of conversions. The magnet and coil are generator parts, while a turbine is one possible source of the turning motion.

Original textbook figure

Read the textbook generator diagram

Follow the arrow from motion to electrical output. Identify which part changes the magnetic conditions and which part supplies the motion.

Movement and magnetic field change

A simple generator has a coil of wire and a magnet. Relative motion between them changes the magnetic field through the coil and induces an electrical output in a closed circuit. A turbine turns a shaft in many large generators.

Faster relative motion, a stronger field or more turns of wire can increase induced voltage in a suitable design. A generator does not make energy from nothing: mechanical input is required, and some energy disperses as heat.

Worked example

Label the path in a hand crank

  1. Name the hand and crank as the source of mechanical input.
  2. Identify magnet and coil as parts with relative motion.
  3. Describe changing magnetic field through the coil and induced electrical output.
  4. Trace the output through the circuit to the lamp; include light and warming.

Turbine and generator have different jobs

Water, steam or wind can push turbine blades. The turbine supplies rotational motion; the generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy. Naming “turbine” as the electrical source skips the important conversion step.

In the workbook drawing, label the coil, magnet, shaft or motion direction and circuit output. The exact internal arrangement may vary; the relative motion is the central idea.

Start with something familiar

The same outlet, different upstream pathways

A lamp at home does not reveal whether its electricity came from falling water, combustion, nuclear fission or solar cells. Compare the pathways before the outlet. In grid-connected systems, electrical energy can come from several sources.

Where a turbine is used, the generator step is shared. The source of the turning motion and the environmental effects differ.

Trace each pathway

Hydroelectric: stored water falls or flows, spins a turbine, then turns a generator. Coal-fired thermal generation: fuel combustion heats water; steam turns a turbine; a generator produces electricity. Nuclear fission supplies heat that can also make turbine-driving steam.

Solar photovoltaic cells are different: incoming light is converted directly to electrical energy without a steam turbine. Source, conversion equipment, emissions and land or water effects should be compared separately.

Use the cutaway: Explain which part supplies motion and which part produces electrical output. Then replace the water input with steam from a coal-fired station: which parts of the pathway stay the same?

Worked example

Compare hydro and coal

  1. Hydro begins with water stored at height; coal begins with chemical energy in fuel.
  2. Moving water or steam turns a turbine in the two stated designs.
  3. A generator converts that rotation to electrical energy.
  4. Hydro reservoirs can alter river habitat; coal combustion releases greenhouse gases and air pollutants. Neither pathway is impact-free.

Electricity travels and some energy disperses

Transmission lines carry electrical energy from generating stations to users. Resistance warms conductors, so some energy is dispersed as heat. High-voltage transmission reduces current for a given power and can reduce resistive losses, though voltage must be transformed for safe distribution.

Start with something familiar

Two motors, different rates

Two motors can transfer the same total energy while one finishes sooner. Power describes the rate of transfer, not the total amount on its own. A 60 W lamp transfers 60 joules each second while operating at that rated power.

The source workbook asks for energy and time calculations. Write the relationship and units before substituting numbers.

P = E ÷ t

Power P is energy transferred E divided by time t. One watt equals one joule per second. If energy is given in joules, time must be in seconds to obtain watts.

A newton is a unit of force, not power. Horsepower is another power unit sometimes used for motors. These labels should not be exchanged with joules, which measure energy.

Worked example

Convert hours before calculating

  1. A machine transfers 7,200 J in 2 h.
  2. Convert time: 2 h × 3,600 s/h = 7,200 s.
  3. Use P = E ÷ t = 7,200 J ÷ 7,200 s.
  4. The result is 1 W, meaning an average of 1 J transferred each second.

Spot the error before continuing

Diagnose the reasoning

Interpret a calculated rate

A large energy number over a long time can produce a modest power. A short intense event can have high power even if total energy is smaller. State whether a question asks for rate or total.

The workbook example 120 J in half a minute uses 30 s, so P = 4 W. It should not be divided by 0.5 unless the desired unit is J/min.

Find the energy from power

Rearrange P = E ÷ t to E = Pt. A 50 W device operating for 10 s transfers 500 J. The units confirm this: (J/s) × s = J.

This is different from a household electricity calculation in watt-hours, which uses hours and is studied next. Both use E = Pt, but the energy unit follows the time unit used.

Start with something familiar

Why bills use kilowatt-hours

A bill records energy supplied over time. A high-power device used briefly and a lower-power device used longer can use the same energy. Watt-hours and kilowatt-hours express this total conveniently.

Power is a rate. Energy use depends on that rate and how long the appliance runs.

Use matching units

E = P × t. If P is in watts and t is in hours, E is in watt-hours (Wh). If P is in kilowatts and t is in hours, E is in kilowatt-hours (kWh). One kWh equals 1,000 Wh.

Start with something familiar

The cost of useful heat

A furnace converts chemical energy in fuel into heat for a building. The same reaction changes substances and releases products. The usefulness of heat does not make every product harmless.

A source evaluation should separate combustion chemistry, greenhouse warming, air pollutants and extraction or spill risks. One measure does not capture all impacts.

Read a combustion equation

Complete propane combustion can be represented as C₃H₈ + 5 O₂ → 3 CO₂ + 4 H₂O. Propane and oxygen are reactants; carbon dioxide and water are products. Energy is transferred to the surroundings. Incomplete combustion can make carbon monoxide, so ventilation and appliance safety matter.

Combustion and aerobic cellular respiration both use oxygen and transfer chemical energy. Respiration is controlled by cells. Water can appear in different physical states depending on conditions; do not assume every product is always vapour.

  2. Count eight hydrogen atoms, so place four H₂O.
  3. Products now contain ten oxygen atoms; place five O₂ among reactants.
  4. Connect the balanced matter account to chemical energy input and heat as a useful output of a furnace.

Explain distinct impacts

Carbon dioxide from fossil combustion adds a greenhouse gas to the atmosphere. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared radiation, changing Earth’s energy balance. Sulfur and nitrogen emissions can contribute to acid deposition through atmospheric reactions.

Extracting and transporting fuels can disturb land and water. Lowering one pollutant does not necessarily eliminate greenhouse emissions or spill risk. A decision weighs a particular benefit against a particular cost.

Separate a device result from a whole-building claim
