Lesson 8: Learning from Artists 

Process, Influence & Creative Growth 

Artists rarely work completely alone or without influence. Throughout history, artists have learned from other artists, studied earlier art movements, and responded to ideas from different cultures and time periods. Art develops through conversation across generations. One artist experiments with a new idea, and others respond, adapt, or reinterpret that approach.

Learning from artists does not mean copying their images. Instead, artists study how others think, experiment, and make decisions. They observe techniques, materials, and creative processes, and then apply those ideas in their own unique way.

In this lesson you will explore how artists influence one another and how the creative process itself becomes part of the meaning of an artwork. Instead of focusing only on producing a final image, you will experiment with brainstorming, testing ideas, and revising your work.

This lesson is designed for students learning independently or at a distance. You are expected to explore ideas, make mistakes, revise your work, and reflect on what you discover. Strong artwork often develops through many small experiments rather than one quick attempt.

What You Are Learning

In this lesson you will learn how artists reflect their culture, historical moment, and personal experiences through their work. You will examine how artistic influence works across time and how artists build on ideas that came before them.

You will also begin practicing a more complete artistic process. Instead of moving directly to a finished artwork, you will explore brainstorming, experimentation, and revision. These steps allow artists to develop stronger ideas and refine their creative decisions.

You will create an artwork inspired by an artist’s approach or process. The final piece will not copy their imagery. Instead, it will reflect how their ideas influenced the way you worked and developed your own visual solution.

The goal of this lesson is to help you begin thinking like an artist rather than simply completing assignments.

Technique Focus

Artist Influence Without Copying

Artists often study the work of others in order to understand different ways of thinking about art. Inspiration can come from many aspects of another artist’s work. Sometimes artists borrow a general idea or concept. Other times they experiment with a similar process or way of working. An artist might also be inspired by a particular mood, colour approach, or method of layering materials.

However, inspiration is different from copying. Copying occurs when someone reproduces another artist’s imagery, composition, or symbols without changing the idea or meaning. When artists work from inspiration, they transform what they have learned into something new.

By studying artists thoughtfully, you can learn new creative approaches while still developing your own ideas and style.

 

The Artistic Process

Many people imagine that artists immediately know what their finished artwork will look like. In reality, most artworks develop gradually through a process of exploration and revision.

Artists often begin with brainstorming, where they generate ideas quickly without worrying about perfection. They may sketch rough drafts to test possible compositions or visual solutions. During experimentation, artists try different materials, techniques, or approaches to see what works best.

Revision is another important step in the process. Artists frequently adjust their work, changing elements that do not feel successful and refining areas that show potential.

The process can produce messy sketchbook pages filled with notes, sketches, and partial ideas. These pages are valuable because they document the thinking behind the final artwork.

Experimentation and Iteration

Experimentation means trying something new and observing the results. Artists experiment with materials, tools, colour combinations, textures, and compositions in order to discover unexpected possibilities.

Sometimes experiments work well, and sometimes they do not. Both outcomes are valuable because they help artists understand what to try next.

Iteration refers to repeating an idea while making adjustments or improvements. An artist might draw the same subject several times, each version becoming clearer or more refined.

Through repeated experimentation and iteration, artists gradually develop stronger ideas and more confident techniques.

Learning to experiment is an important step in developing creative independence.



Artists Who Explore Process and Influence

Daphne Odjig

Daphne Odjig was an influential Indigenous artist known for combining storytelling with simplified flowing shapes. Her work often represents cultural history, community, and identity.

Odjig’s paintings demonstrate how visual storytelling can reflect cultural experience. The shapes and figures in her compositions create unity and movement while communicating meaningful narratives.

Her work shows that art can carry cultural knowledge and personal history.

Diego Rivera

Diego Rivera was a Mexican muralist who used large public paintings to represent social history and the experiences of workers and communities. His murals often appear on public buildings so that art becomes accessible to everyone.

Rivera believed that art could communicate important social ideas. His compositions are carefully organized to guide viewers through historical narratives.

Rivera’s work shows how art can respond to the social conditions of its time.

Agnes Martin

Agnes Martin created quiet, meditative artworks using subtle grids and repeated lines. At first glance her paintings appear simple, but closer observation reveals delicate variations and careful attention to detail.

Her process involved repetition and careful refinement. Small changes between lines or shapes created rhythm and calm visual harmony.

Martin’s work demonstrates how repetition and subtle variation can produce powerful visual effects.

Jackson Pollock

Jackson Pollock was an American artist associated with Abstract Expressionism. Rather than painting with traditional brushstrokes on an upright canvas, Pollock placed his canvas on the floor and dripped or poured paint across the surface.

The movement of his body and the motion of the paint became central to the artwork. The painting recorded the physical gestures used during its creation.

Pollock’s work demonstrates how the act of making art can become a central element of the artwork itself.
