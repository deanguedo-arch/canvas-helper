Lesson 3: Composition & Design — Learning to See on Purpose

Artists rarely place objects randomly in their artwork. Instead, they carefully decide where each shape, line, or object will appear on the page. These decisions form what artists call composition. Composition refers to how visual elements are arranged so that the viewer’s eye moves through the artwork in a particular way.

In many beginner drawings, students focus only on what they are drawing rather than how it is arranged. However, the placement of objects can change how an artwork feels and what the viewer notices first. An object placed in the center of the page might feel calm and stable, while the same object placed near the edge might create tension or movement.

This lesson introduces the idea that artists design images intentionally. Instead of drawing immediately, artists often plan several possible arrangements before choosing the one that communicates most clearly. This process helps artists make stronger visual decisions.

This lesson is designed for students working independently or learning at a distance. You are not trying to create a perfect or detailed image yet. The goal is to understand how arrangement and placement influence what viewers notice and how they experience an artwork.

What You Are Learning

In this lesson you will begin learning how artists guide the viewer’s eye through an artwork. You will explore how composition affects both meaning and visual experience. Through sketchbook exercises you will practice planning multiple possible arrangements before creating a final piece.

You will also begin using visual elements such as line, shape, and space intentionally rather than randomly. By creating several different versions of the same subject, you will see how small changes in placement can dramatically change how an image feels.

These composition skills will be used throughout the entire course. Every artwork you create will involve decisions about placement, balance, and movement.

Technique Focus

Understanding the Focal Point

One of the most important ideas in composition is the focal point. The focal point is the area of an artwork that attracts the viewer’s attention first. When someone looks at an image, their eye usually moves to the area that stands out the most.

Artists create focal points in several ways. One method is through size, because larger objects tend to attract attention more quickly than smaller ones. Another method is through placement, such as positioning an object near the center of the composition or in an unexpected location. Artists also create emphasis through contrast, such as placing a dark object against a light background or using a detailed shape surrounded by simpler shapes.

When a focal point is clear, viewers know where to look first. Without a focal point, the viewer’s eye may wander without direction.

Understanding Balance

Balance refers to how visual weight is distributed across an artwork. When an image feels balanced, the elements within the composition feel stable and intentional.

One form of balance is symmetrical balance, which occurs when both sides of an image appear similar or mirror each other. This type of balance often feels calm, orderly, and stable.

Another form of balance is asymmetrical balance, which occurs when the two sides of an image are different but still feel visually balanced. For example, a large shape on one side of the page might be balanced by several smaller shapes on the other side.

Balance does not mean that everything must be equal. Instead, balance means that the elements feel thoughtfully arranged so the image does not appear lopsided or accidental.

Understanding Movement and Flow

Movement in art refers to the path the viewer’s eye follows when looking at an image. Artists often design compositions so the viewer’s eye moves smoothly from one area of the artwork to another.

Movement can be created through repeating shapes, directional lines, curved pathways, or patterns that guide the eye across the page. For example, a series of curved lines might lead the viewer’s eye toward the focal point, while diagonal lines can create a feeling of energy or motion.

When movement is used effectively, the viewer’s eye travels through the artwork naturally rather than stopping suddenly in one place.

Art History and Context

During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, artists began exploring new approaches to composition. Rather than focusing only on realistic representation, many artists became interested in how shapes, colours, and lines interact within the picture space.

This period of experimentation is often described as Modernism. Artists began simplifying forms, cropping images in unusual ways, and arranging shapes more intentionally. Composition became a central part of artistic decision making.

Instead of asking only what to draw, artists began asking how the image should be organized so that viewers experience it in a particular way. This shift helped artists create more expressive and visually dynamic artworks.

Artists Who Use Composition Intentionally

Bill Reid

Bill Reid was a Haida artist whose sculptures and designs are known for their careful balance and flowing forms. His work often includes animals and cultural symbols arranged so that the shapes interlock and guide the viewer’s eye smoothly across the piece.

Even in complex sculptures, Reid arranged forms so that the composition feels stable and harmonious. The curves and shapes in his work lead the viewer’s eye from one part of the artwork to another.

Reid’s work shows how balance and movement can exist even in highly detailed compositions.

Emily Carr

Emily Carr was a Canadian painter known for her expressive landscapes of British Columbia. Her paintings often include dramatic diagonal lines, twisting tree shapes, and close-up compositions that create strong movement.

Carr frequently positioned trees and landforms so that the viewer’s eye moves upward or deeper into the forest. These compositional choices make her landscapes feel energetic and alive.

Her work demonstrates how composition can transform a simple scene into a powerful visual experience.

Henri Matisse

Henri Matisse was known for simplifying shapes and arranging them carefully to create rhythm and harmony. Many of his artworks use bold colour and simple forms placed deliberately across the surface.

Rather than focusing on realistic detail, Matisse concentrated on how shapes interact and how the viewer’s eye moves through the image. Repetition, balance, and strong focal points play important roles in his compositions.

Matisse demonstrates that arrangement alone can create strong visual impact.
