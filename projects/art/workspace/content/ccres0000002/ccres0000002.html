Lesson 1: What Is Art?

Visual Language & Purpose

Art is a form of communication. Just as writers use words and sentences to communicate ideas, artists use visual tools to communicate meaning. These tools are called the Elements of Art and the Principles of Design. Learning how these tools work is the first step to becoming a stronger artist.

Many students think that art is mainly about drawing realistically or making something look “good.” In reality, artists focus much more on how visual choices create meaning. An artwork might be simple, abstract, or symbolic, but it can still communicate powerful ideas if the artist understands how to use visual language.

This lesson introduces the basic building blocks of art and the ways artists organize them. These ideas will be used throughout Art 10, Art 20, and Art 30. Every drawing, painting, sculpture, or design you create will rely on these same tools.

This course is designed so that students can work independently. You do not need to be confident in your drawing skills to complete this lesson. What matters most is that you observe carefully, try honestly, and write about what you notice. Your sketchbook is not a place for perfect artwork. Instead, it is a place for thinking, experimenting, and learning.

What You Are Learning

In this lesson you will learn that art communicates ideas, emotions, and stories. You will begin to recognize the visual tools artists use to create meaning. You will also learn how to use a sketchbook as a thinking space where artists explore ideas before creating finished work.

You will practice identifying the Elements of Art, which are the visual building blocks artists use to create images. You will also explore the Principles of Design, which describe how artists organize those elements to guide the viewer’s eye and create meaning.

By the end of this lesson you will create a small composition that intentionally uses one element and one principle. This will be your first experience making artistic decisions on purpose rather than simply drawing what you see.

Technique Focus

Understanding Visual Vocabulary (Elements of Art)

The Elements of Art are the basic building blocks artists use when creating images. Just as letters form words and words form sentences, elements combine to create visual meaning.

Line is the path created when a point moves across a surface. Lines can be straight, curved, thick, thin, jagged, smooth, or broken. A drawing made with sharp zig-zag lines might feel energetic or tense, while soft curved lines might feel calm or flowing.

Shape is an area that is enclosed by lines or boundaries. Shapes can be geometric, such as squares, triangles, and circles, or organic, meaning they resemble natural forms like leaves or clouds. Artists often simplify complex objects into basic shapes.

Colour refers to hue as well as the relationships between colours. Colours may be warm or cool, bright or dull, or intense or muted. Colour choices can create mood. For example, warm colours such as red and orange often feel energetic, while cool colours such as blue and green can feel calm or quiet.

Value describes how light or dark something appears. Artists use value to create contrast and depth. Strong differences between light and dark areas can draw the viewer’s attention.

Texture describes how a surface feels or appears to feel. Texture can be real, such as rough wood or smooth glass, or implied through drawing techniques that suggest surface qualities.

Space refers to the illusion of depth or the arrangement of objects within an artwork. Artists use techniques such as overlapping shapes, changing sizes, and placing objects higher or lower in the image to create a sense of foreground and background.

When artists combine these elements, they create the visual “words” of art.

Understanding Visual Organization (Principles of Design)

While the elements are the building blocks, the Principles of Design describe how artists organize those elements to guide the viewer’s eye.

Balance refers to the distribution of visual weight in an artwork. A balanced composition feels stable. Balance can be symmetrical, where both sides of an image look similar, or asymmetrical, where different elements create a sense of stability even though they are not identical.

Contrast occurs when strong differences appear within an image. Contrast might occur between light and dark areas, between rough and smooth textures, or between large and small shapes. Contrast creates visual interest and helps important areas stand out.

Emphasis refers to the area of an artwork that attracts the viewer’s attention first. Artists create emphasis by using contrast, size, colour, or placement to highlight a specific part of the composition.

Movement describes the path the viewer’s eye follows when looking at an artwork. Artists use lines, shapes, and patterns to guide the viewer’s gaze through the image.

When artists combine elements and principles effectively, they are able to communicate ideas visually.

Making Artistic Choices

Beginning artists often ask whether their artwork “looks good.” Experienced artists ask a different question. Instead of focusing only on appearance, artists think about what they want the viewer to notice and how the artwork communicates meaning.

An artist might decide that a bright colour should draw attention to a specific part of an image. Another artist might use strong diagonal lines to create a feeling of motion. These decisions are intentional. The goal is not simply to draw something accurately, but to design the image so that the viewer experiences it in a particular way.

As you work in your sketchbook, begin thinking about how your choices influence what the viewer sees first and how they interpret the image.

Art History - Canada’s Group of Seven

The Group of Seven was a collective of Canadian landscape painters active primarily between 1920 and 1933. The original members included Lawren Harris, A. Y. Jackson, Arthur Lismer, Frederick Varley, Franklin Carmichael, Frank Johnston, and J. E. H. MacDonald. Although not an official member, Tom Thomson greatly influenced the group and is often associated with them.

At a time when many Canadian artists looked to Europe for inspiration, the Group of Seven focused on the Canadian wilderness. They painted northern forests, rocky shorelines, lakes, and skies in bold colours and expressive brushstrokes. Their goal was to create a distinctly Canadian artistic identity rooted in the land.

 

Their style was not strictly realistic. They simplified forms, intensified colour, and used strong outlines to emphasize structure and rhythm in the landscape. Lawren Harris, for example, often reduced mountains and trees into almost spiritual, geometric forms. A.Y. Jackson used dynamic brushwork and rich colour to bring energy to rural scenes.

 

The Group of Seven helped establish landscape painting as central to Canadian art. They promoted the idea that Canada’s identity was connected to its natural environment. Their work shaped how many Canadians viewed their country—not just as wilderness, but as something powerful, vast, and uniquely beautiful.

 

However, their legacy is complex. While they celebrated the land, they often painted it as empty and untouched, overlooking the presence and histories of Indigenous peoples who had lived on and cared for these lands for thousands of years. Contemporary discussions of Canadian art now include Indigenous perspectives and critique this “empty wilderness” narrative.

 

The Group of Seven’s influence can still be seen in Canadian art education and public collections today. They helped build national pride in Canadian art and encouraged artists to look to their own surroundings for inspiration rather than copying European traditions.

 

Studying the Group of Seven provides insight into how art can shape national identity. It also opens important conversations about representation, land, and whose stories are told in art history.

Artists Who Use Visual Language Clearly

Lawren Harris

 

Lawren Harris was a member of the Group of Seven, a group of Canadian painters who became famous for painting Canadian landscapes. Harris simplified the mountains, lakes, and trees he painted so that they became strong shapes rather than detailed realistic scenes.

His paintings often use strong contrasts between light and dark areas, clear shapes, and balanced compositions. These choices create a feeling of stillness and focus. When looking at his paintings, viewers often feel a sense of quiet strength or calm.

Harris shows that artists can communicate a strong sense of place and emotion using simple shapes and careful design decisions.

Norval Morrisseau

Norval Morrisseau was an Anishinaabe artist known for developing the Woodland Style of painting. His work is recognizable for its bold black outlines and bright areas of colour.

Instead of painting realistic scenes, Morrisseau used symbolic imagery to communicate cultural stories and spiritual ideas. Animals, spirits, and humans often appear connected through flowing lines that represent relationships between living beings.

His work shows that art can communicate meaning through symbol, colour, and line rather than realism.

Piet Mondrian

Piet Mondrian was a Dutch artist who believed that art could be simplified to basic visual structures. His paintings often contain only straight black lines and blocks of primary colours.

By carefully arranging these shapes and colours, Mondrian created balanced compositions that guide the viewer’s eye across the image.

His work demonstrates that art does not need to represent real objects to communicate visually. Structure, balance, and contrast alone can create meaning.
