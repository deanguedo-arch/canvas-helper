In the eukaryotic cells considered here, nuclear DNA is associated with proteins, including histones. This DNA–protein material is called chromatin. It can be relatively spread out between divisions or tightly condensed as a cell prepares to divide. Condensation organizes long DNA molecules for movement; it does not make genetic information appear for the first time.

A chromosome is a distinct organized unit of that genetic material. Before replication, it contains one DNA molecule. After replication, the two copied DNA molecules are organized as sister chromatids of one replicated chromosome until they separate. Thus, the short definition “a DNA molecule with associated proteins” describes the unreplicated state; it is not a rule that a replicated chromosome has only one DNA molecule.

Keep condensing separate from copying. Condensing changes how the material is packed. Replication makes a second DNA copy. A nucleus may contain DNA even when its individual chromosomes cannot be distinguished in a light-microscope image. Improved microscopes and staining methods made chromosome behavior observable; the stain did not create the chromosomes.

Distinguish homologues from sister chromatids

Homologous chromosomes carry the same genes at corresponding positions, called loci. One typically comes from each parent. Their versions of a gene, called alleles, may be the same or different. Matching gene positions therefore does not mean the two chromosomes have identical DNA sequences.

Use several features together in the karyotype: overall length, centromere position and banding pattern. The centromere is a specialized, often constricted chromosome region where proteins organize spindle attachment for later movement. A stain band can contain many genes; it is not an individual allele label. The image cannot reveal every DNA sequence or prove that every possible genetic condition is absent.

Sister chromatids have a different origin: they are the copies produced when one chromosome replicates. They remain associated, particularly around the centromere region, before separation. A maternal chromosome and its paternal homologue are two chromosomes. The two joined sisters of a replicated maternal chromosome are copies of that one maternal chromosome.

In this teacher-source drawing, both the left rod and the entire right-hand X are labelled one chromosome. Each complete side of the X is a sister chromatid; an individual upper or lower arm is not a whole chromatid. The condensed shapes illustrate the relationship, not the actual appearance of DNA throughout replication. Do not call the X a homologous pair simply because it has two visible sides.

The textbook's typical human model has 22 autosome pairs and two sex chromosomes: XX in typical female cells and XY in typical male cells. The source karyotype shows XX. X and Y share some corresponding regions but do not match throughout their lengths, so the usual homologue comparison needs that qualification.
Count sets before counting copies

A chromosome set contains one of each chromosome type in the species model. A haploid cell has one set, written n; a diploid cell has two, written 2n. Normal human gametes have 23 chromosomes, and a typical nucleated body cell has 46. The number of sets is its ploidy.

For a model with five types, one set contains five chromosomes. Two sets contain 2 × 5 = 10. If instead the question gives 2n = 10, divide the diploid total by two to recover n = 5. Count matching types and complete sets; chromosome number alone is not a measure of an organism's intelligence or complexity.

Polyploid means more than two complete sets. In the textbook's set-counting model, tetraploid means four sets and octoploid means eight. If one basic set contains five chromosomes, those totals are 4 × 5 = 20 and 8 × 5 = 40. For the optional coffee question, use its supplied one-set value, multiply by the named number of sets and label each result. Its prose and picture list the categories in different orders, so attach each answer to diploid, tetraploid or octoploid rather than relying on position.

Replication is not the addition of a new set of independently counted homologues. It copies each existing chromosome into joined sisters before they are distributed. A diploid cell remains diploid after S phase. The next lesson tracks chromosome number and DNA amount separately so that an X shape does not mislead the count.
Worked example
Identify the relationship before doing the arithmetic

Given: A model cell contains a long maternal chromosome, a long paternal chromosome, a short maternal chromosome and a short paternal chromosome. The long chromosomes are one type and the short chromosomes another. Question: How many sets and chromosomes are present, and what would replication change?

Identify the types: long and short give two types. One complete set therefore contains two chromosomes, so n = 2.
Group the homologues: the maternal and paternal long chromosomes form one pair; the two short chromosomes form the other. Each pair can carry different alleles at corresponding loci.
Count the sets: two of every type means two complete sets. The cell is diploid, with 2n = 2 × 2 = 4 chromosomes.
Change the condition: after replication, each of those four chromosomes has two joined sister chromatids. There are still four replicated chromosomes before the sisters separate, not eight independently counted homologues.

Check: pairing by parental origin describes homologues; copying one chromosome produces sisters. Neither condensation nor the X-shaped drawing alone tells you how many chromosome sets a cell has.
