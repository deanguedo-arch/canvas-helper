The material remains; its packaging changes

In the eukaryotic cells considered here, nuclear DNA is associated with proteins, including histones. This DNA–protein material is called . It can be relatively spread out between divisions or tightly condensed as a cell prepares to divide. Condensation organizes long DNA molecules for movement; it does not make genetic information appear for the first time.

A is a distinct organized unit of that genetic material. Before replication, it contains one DNA molecule. After replication, the two copied DNA molecules are organized as sister chromatids of one replicated chromosome until they separate. Thus, the short definition “a DNA molecule with associated proteins” describes the unreplicated state; it is not a rule that a replicated chromosome has only one DNA molecule.

Keep condensing separate from copying. Condensing changes how the material is packed. Replication makes a second DNA copy. A nucleus may contain DNA even when its individual chromosomes cannot be distinguished in a light-microscope image. Improved microscopes and staining methods made chromosome behavior observable; the stain did not create the chromosomes.

The karyotype above is an arranged image of chromosomes, not a picture of how the pairs sit in a living nucleus. Look at the groups numbered 1–22 and the final X group. Each numbered autosome group contains two chromosomes placed beside one another for comparison. Their arrangement helps you investigate chromosome number and large structural features.
