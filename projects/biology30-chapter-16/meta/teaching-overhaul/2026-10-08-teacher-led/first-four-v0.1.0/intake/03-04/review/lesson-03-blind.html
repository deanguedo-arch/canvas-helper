Reading chromosome number and DNA amount

Why can two cells have the same chromosome number but different DNA amounts?

Learning goal

Count chromosomes and chromatids through replication and separation, stating whether a count applies to a whole cell or one future nucleus.

Before you begin

After S phase, sister chromatids remain joined until they separate. Each separated chromatid becomes a chromosome.

State the stage, boundary and quantity

A chromosome count needs more than a number. Before counting a diagram, settle three questions:

  1. Stage: has DNA replicated, and have the separated? Joined copies and separated copies are counted differently.
  2. Boundary: are you counting everything in one undivided cell, the group moving toward one pole, or one completed daughter nucleus or cell?
  3. Quantity: do you need chromosomes, chromatids or ? A chromosome count is not a measurement of how much DNA is present.

For example, the same post-replication model can have four chromosomes and eight sister chromatids. Reporting “eight” without naming the quantity could give the wrong answer even though you counted a real feature.

Read the totals below the two panels. The left contains one chromosome with one DNA molecule. The right contains one replicated chromosome with two DNA molecules, organized as joined sisters. The arrow represents DNA replication, not separation into two cells. The condensed rod/X shapes make the relationship visible; they are not literal pictures of DNA throughout S phase.

Follow one diploid model through changing boundaries

Use a normal model with 2n = 4: two types, with two homologues of each type. In G1, each of its four chromosomes is unreplicated and contains one DNA molecule. Set the DNA amount in this whole G1 cell to one relative unit so that changes can be compared.

doubles DNA amount without immediately doubling chromosome number. In anaphase, separation makes each former sister an individual chromosome, so the still-undivided cell contains eight. It has not copied its DNA again: the existing material is being distributed, four chromosomes toward each pole.

Each normal daughter receives the original diploid number, 2n = 4. The temporary whole-cell total of eight includes both future groups. It does not show that normal daughters have become tetraploid. Changing the boundary changes the reported total without changing the division mechanism.

Before S, describe four unreplicated, one-DNA chromosomes and no sister-chromatid pairs. Some counting charts call each unreplicated chromosome a single chromatid, so they would report four chromatids at that stage. State that convention if asked for a pre-S chromatid count; do not confuse “no sister pairs yet” with no DNA.

Count complete structures, not visible arms

In the diagram, A identifies the unreplicated chromosome, B and C identify the two complete sister chromatids, and D marks the region. The B/C leader lines touch their upper ends, but each chromatid includes the chromosome material on both sides of its centromere. A short upper or lower arm is not a whole chromatid.

Now imagine two similar-sized X shapes representing a replicated homologous pair. Each whole X is one chromosome, so together they contain two chromosomes and four chromatids. The homologues have corresponding genes; each has made its own sister copy. If colours distinguish parental origins, colour is a guide to those relationships, not an extra counting rule.

Check the result against normal distribution. Each daughter should receive one copy of every chromosome present before S, including both parental contributions in this diploid model. DNA copied before mitosis is distributed between daughters; it is not doubled again during separation or reduced by discarding a whole parental set.

Worked example

Explain why the same number can describe different things

Given: A normal diploid cell has 2n = 6. Question: Count its chromosomes in G1, after S, during whole-cell mitotic anaphase and in each daughter cell. Explain the changes.

  1. G1: there are six unreplicated chromosomes, each with one DNA molecule. There are no sister pairs yet.
  2. After S, before separation: every chromosome has copied its DNA. Six joined sister pairs are still six chromosomes, now containing twelve chromatids. DNA amount is twice the G1 amount.
  3. Anaphase, whole undivided cell: the twelve sisters have separated and now count as twelve individual chromosomes. Six move toward each pole. No second replication occurred, so whole-cell DNA amount is unchanged from the post-S state.
  4. Each daughter: normal division distributes six chromosomes to each cell. Its chromosome number remains 2n = 6, and each has half the preceding undivided cell's DNA amount.

Check the wording: “Twelve” describes chromatids after S but whole-cell chromosomes after separation. Neither statement means twelve chromosomes arrive in each daughter. Always attach the number to its stage, quantity and boundary.
