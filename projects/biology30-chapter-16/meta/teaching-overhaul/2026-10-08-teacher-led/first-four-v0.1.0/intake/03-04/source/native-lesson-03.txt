Reading chromosome number and DNA amount
Why can two cells have the same chromosome number but different DNA amounts?
Learning goal
Count chromosomes and chromatids through replication and separation, stating whether a count applies to a whole cell or one future nucleus.
Before you begin
After S phase, sister chromatids remain joined until they separate. Each separated chromatid becomes a chromosome.

Vocabulary help
Key terms
Words for this lesson
Use these definitions when you need help with a term.
centromere
The chromosome region associated with the attachment and separation of sister chromatids.
chromosome number
The count of individual chromosomes in a specified cell or nucleus.
DNA amount
The quantity of DNA in the specified cell or nucleus.
anaphase
The mitotic stage in which sister chromatids separate and move toward opposite poles.
Ask three questions before you count
First identify the stage. Has DNA replicated, and have 
sister chromatids
 separated? Next identify the boundary: are you counting the whole cell, one nucleus, or the chromosomes moving toward one pole? Finally state the quantity: chromosomes, chromatids or 
DNA amount
.
Many errors come from answering a different question than the one asked. “Eight” can be correct for chromatids but incorrect for chromosomes in the same model cell. A count without its quantity and boundary is incomplete.
One chromosome before and after replication
View larger
Replication doubles DNA amount without initially doubling chromosome number. The two sister chromatids remain one replicated chromosome until they separate. Schematic — not to scale.
Use a small diploid model
Consider a cell with 2n = 4. In G1 it contains four unreplicated chromosomes, each with one DNA molecule. After 
S phase
 it still contains four chromosomes, now represented by eight chromatids. DNA amount has doubled.
During mitotic 
anaphase
, the sister chromatids separate. Each is now a 
chromosome
. The still-undivided cell therefore briefly contains eight chromosomes, with four moving toward each pole. After mitosis and cytokinesis, each daughter cell contains four chromosomes.
Do not interpret the temporary whole-cell count of eight as proof that the normal daughter cells have doubled their ploidy. The separated groups are being distributed into two cells. The scope of the count explains the apparent change.
Keep diagrams consistent with their meaning
A joined pair of rods or an X often represents one replicated chromosome. Two differently coloured X shapes of corresponding size may represent a replicated homologous pair: two chromosomes and four chromatids. The colour key should identify parental origin, not create a new counting rule.
Use 
centromere
 separation rather than counting every visible arm. A replicated chromosome with two chromatids can have four visible arms, but that does not make it four chromosomes.
A useful final check is conservation of the copied genetic material. Normal mitosis distributes one copy of each chromosome type to each daughter cell; it does not selectively discard one whole parental set.
Worked example
Count at four stages
A diploid cell has 2n = 6. Count chromosomes before replication, after replication, during whole-cell mitotic anaphase and in each daughter cell.
G1: 6 chromosomes, each unreplicated.
After S: 6 chromosomes with 12 chromatids; DNA amount is doubled.
Anaphase before cytokinesis: 12 separated chromosomes in the whole cell, with 6 moving to each pole.
Each daughter cell: 6 chromosomes.
The chromosome count per daughter remains 2n = 6; specify boundaries when reporting the temporary anaphase count.
