Meiosis I and separation of homologous chromosomes

How is the number of chromosome sets reduced?

Learning goal

Trace meiosis I and explain why separation of homologues produces haploid chromosome sets while chromatids remain paired.

Before you begin

DNA replicates before meiosis begins. Homologous chromosomes are not sister chromatids.

Key terms

Words for this lesson

Use these definitions when you need help with a term.

The first meiotic division, in which homologous chromosomes separate.
Pairing of homologous chromosomes during prophase I.
A paired set of two replicated homologous chromosomes containing four chromatids.
A division that reduces chromosome sets from diploid to haploid, as in meiosis I.

Replication happens once before the two divisions

Meiosis begins after DNA replication. A diploid cell therefore has replicated chromosomes, each with two . It will undergo two divisions, but it does not perform another full round of DNA replication between them.

During I, pair through . Each paired unit contains two replicated homologues and four chromatids, so it is called a . This organized pairing allows interactions, including crossing over, between corresponding regions of non-sister chromatids.

Arrange pairs, then separate homologues

At I, homologous pairs align at the equator. Their orientation relative to other pairs can vary. A replicated maternal homologue faces one pole and its paternal partner faces the other, but different pairs can orient independently.

During I, the homologues separate. The sister chromatids of each normally remain joined. The event is therefore different from mitotic anaphase, which separates sister chromatids.

After this distribution, each future daughter receives one chromosome of each homologous pair. It has one set rather than two. That is the reduction in ploidy.

A haploid cell can still have paired chromatids

At the end of , the cells are haploid because each contains one homologue of each type. Their chromosomes are still replicated. In a 2n = 4 model, each daughter has two chromosomes, each containing two chromatids.

Do not call these daughter cells diploid merely because their chromosomes look like X shapes. The X shape represents replication, not two homologous sets. Meiosis II will separate the sister chromatids.

This distinction is central: meiosis I reduces sets; meiosis II separates the remaining copies. You cannot identify ploidy reliably by the shape of one chromosome alone.

Worked example

Follow a 2n = 4 model through meiosis I

The starting cell contains one long homologous pair and one short homologous pair, both replicated.

  1. At metaphase I, the long homologues pair and the short homologues pair.
  2. At anaphase I, one long homologue and one short homologue move to each pole.
  3. Each resulting cell has one of each chromosome type: n = 2.
  4. Each chromosome still has two sister chromatids, so each cell has two chromosomes and four chromatids.
  5. The reduction concerns homologous sets, not immediate separation of every chromatid.
