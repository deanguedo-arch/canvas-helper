How does division produce separate cells and support a multicellular body?

Before you begin

Separate the cytoplasm, then compare the boundaries

Mitosis distributes nuclear chromosomes into two groups. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm so those groups can belong to separate daughter cells. The processes can overlap near the end of mitosis; two nuclei inside one continuous cell do not yet show completed cytoplasmic separation. DNA copying happened earlier, during S phase.

In a typical animal cell, a contractile ring beneath the membrane tightens around the middle. The membrane is drawn inward, making a . As the furrow deepens, the cytoplasm separates and each daughter receives a nucleus and cytoplasmic components. Follow the teacher illustration from the narrowing middle at the top to the two separate boundaries below.

A plant cell has a rigid outer wall. Small membrane-bound vesicles gather and fuse between the daughter nuclei, forming a that grows outward. Their membranes contribute to the new cell membranes, while deposited wall material forms the partition between the daughters. In the original plant micrograph below, find the two dark chromosome groups, then the developing partition between them. The outer wall does not pinch inward like an animal membrane.

Both animal and onion cells use spindle microtubules to distribute chromosomes. Typical animal cells have centrioles associated with spindle organization; onion cells form a spindle without centrioles. Thus, a cell plate versus a furrow distinguishes the cytokinesis mechanism, while the centriole comparison concerns spindle organization. Both kinds of cell have cell membranes.

More cells must become useful tissue

Cell division supports three related functions. Growth increases cell number as an organism develops. Maintenance replaces cells lost through normal wear. Repair replaces damaged tissue, such as cells involved in closing a skin wound. Each new cell needs the genetic information required to carry out its role, and division must occur in the right place and amount.

Stem cells can keep dividing and produce descendants that become specialized. Their developmental possibilities depend on the stem-cell type and its surroundings; an adult tissue stem cell cannot automatically produce every kind of body cell. Different tissues also differ in their capacity for replacement. Many mature cells do not divide continuously.

Suppose a culture doubles its cell count after a growth signal. That shows an increase in number, but successful repair also requires the appropriate cell types, organization and function. For a damaged sensory tissue, for example, replacement cells would need the right specialized structures and connections to transmit useful signals. Evidence of those functions and controlled growth is needed before concluding that the tissue has been restored.

Control determines whether division helps

Signals and checkpoints regulate the . A cell-cycle checkpoint is a control mechanism that can delay progression when conditions are unsuitable, including when DNA damage is detected. A pause gives the cell an opportunity to respond; the checkpoint is not itself a guarantee that every error will be repaired. If damaged DNA is copied and distributed despite failed control, the damage can pass to daughter cells.

Normal root-tip growth can involve rapid division while still responding to regulatory signals. Cancer involves abnormal loss of growth control; speed alone is insufficient evidence. Changes in genes that regulate division can allow damaged lineages to keep multiplying. A tissue's organization, function and response to signals matter alongside its cell count.

Telomeres are protective regions at ends. They shorten during many somatic-cell divisions and can contribute to limits on continued proliferation. Some cells maintain them differently, including by adding repeat sequences. This is a cell-biology relationship, not a simple clock that predicts a person's age or remaining lifespan.

Worked example

Use the two images to explain a mechanism

Compare the teacher's animal-cell illustration with the plant micrograph above. Explain how each cell becomes two cells, using its boundary as evidence.

  1. Locate what changes. In the animal illustration the middle narrows inward. In the plant micrograph a partition is developing between the two dark chromosome groups.
  2. Name the mechanisms. A contractile ring produces the animal cleavage furrow. Vesicles join to establish the plant cell plate.
  3. Connect structure to mechanism. The animal's flexible membrane can be drawn inward. The plant's rigid outer wall stays in place while a new internal partition grows outward.
  4. State the shared result. Cytokinesis separates cytoplasm into daughter cells. Neither the furrow nor the plate is the event that copies DNA.

The animal illustration's “identical” label describes the normal nuclear genetic outcome of the model. It does not demonstrate exact equality of every organelle or prove that any resulting tissue is functional.
