What is prosperity? 

Although there are many definitions of prosperity, the word prosperity usually refers to money. The desire for prosperity is a powerful globalizing force. It’s often the reason why people immigrate, why nations trade with one another, and why people communicate with people in other nations. People, corporations, and nations connect with each other so that they can become more prosperous.

In today’s world, 40 percent of the world, over 2.6 billion people, live on less than two Canadian dollars per day. While there are many perspectives on what prosperity means, without money, people’s basic needs cannot be met. Without increased wealth, people in the poorest nations of the world will never have enough food, an education, proper clothing or shelter, or a high enough standard of health care to live long and healthy lives.

If the gap between rich and poor continues to grow, what possible consequences are there for both the rich and the poor? What does this kind of inequality mean to the global community? You will continue to explore these questions in this module and the modules that follow.

Your understanding of what prosperity really means is an important factor in understanding economic globalization and how it affects people around the world.

What’s the difference between standard of living and quality of life?

Prosperity can be described in many ways. It can be defined in terms of standard of living and quality of life.

Standard of living is measured in terms of income. It’s usually called per capita income and calculated as GDP per capita. GDP, or Gross Domestic Product,is one way to show how wealthy a country is.

For example, in 2006 Canada's GNP was $1 095 000 000. Its population was 32 422 000. Therefore, 1 095 000 000 divided by 32 422 000 = $33 770. The per capita income of Canadians in 2006 was $33 770.

Did each person in Canada make $33 770? Of course not—some made more and some made much less. One of the biggest problems with looking at prosperity in terms of per capita GDP is that money is not evenly shared in any country. Some people make a lot of money, and others can make almost nothing.

GINI Index

One way of looking at the lack of economic equality in a nation is to look at the distribution of money. If there is a huge gap between the richest and poorest people in the country, there is a very unequal sharing of wealth. That means that some people are very prosperous and others are very poor. Such inequality is more extreme in some countries than in others. The GINI Index is one way of measuring inequalities in income.

Gross National Happiness

In our world today, we usually describe prosperity as economic well-being. However, there is a growing movement of people worldwide who believe that things like standard of living and per capita income don’t tell the whole story about the overall well-being about people who live in a country.

In 1972, the king of Bhutan, a small country in the Himalayas, proposed something he called Gross National Happiness. Gross National Happiness is based on the Buddhist philosophy that the ultimate purpose of life is inner happiness. As the leader of a Buddhist country, the king of Bhutan felt that it was his responsibility to define the development of his country in terms of the happiness of its people, rather than how much money they made.

The ideology of GNH connects Bhutan’s development goals with the pursuit of happiness. This means that the ideology reflects Bhutan’s vision on the purpose of human life, a vision that puts the individual’s self-cultivation at the center of the nation’s developmental goals, a primary priority for Bhutanese society as a whole as well as for the individual concerned.

—Dasho Meghraj Gurung, Director General Bhutan Post

Vocabulary

standard of living: a level of material comfort as measured by the goods and services available to an individual, group, or nation, including access to health care and education

quality of life: a measure of how good one's life is, including standard of living and other, more difficult to measure aspects of well-being, such as health, happiness, human rights and freedoms, environmental well-being, and creativity

per capita income: the average income of the people in a country calculated by dividing the gross national product by the number of people in the country

GDP per capita: the average income of each person in a country, calculated by dividing the gross national product by the number of people in the country

Gross Domestic Product: the total value of goods and services produced within a country in a year

GINI Index: a measurement of the inequality of wealth distribution in a country. The smaller the number, the more equal the distribution of wealth.

What is sustainability?

What makes something “sustainable”? Sustainability is the ability of something to last or continue without causing a negative impact on the future.

There are many ways something can be sustainable, for example:

  • politically
  • environmentally
  • socially or culturally
  • economically

Think about Reena and TJ’s Pizzeria. In what ways was it sustainable?

  • Politically. The restaurant was popular and followed local laws.
  • Environmentally. The restaurant used environmentally friendly products.
  • Socially or Culturally. The restaurant considered local needs and values, including dietary considerations, the needs of students and parents, and community activities.
  • Economically. It was well located so that it had a steady clientele. Overhead was low, and at first there was not a lot of competition. But when a transnational corporation opened up nearby, with high volume of sales and more powerful marketing strategies, the smaller pizzeria was economically challenged.

Creating Affluence

Where does wealth come from? How does the wealth of individuals or nations become greater or smaller? Who is wealthy and who is not? Why are some countries richer than others? Economic globalization, or the interaction between nations in terms of the buying and selling of goods and services, has an important impact on almost every nation in the world. How does it work?

When people think about globalization, they often think about money. Since early times, people have travelled in order to find the resources they need. This began in the days of early hunter-gatherers who searched for food and continued through the beginnings of global trade routes. In more recent times, economic globalization included the development of imperialism. Today, people in nations around the world are connected through the global economy.

In this lesson you will investigate how nations are connected through economic globalization. You will explore the impact those connections have on the wealth and poverty of their citizens.

How is wealth generated?

Evan is a 12-year-old student. He wants to make some money to buy a new snowboard. He doesn’t have a lot of job skills, resources, or free time.

He lives within a couple of blocks of the stadium in Edmonton. His mom hates the traffic whenever there is a game or other event, especially when people drop garbage in their yard and walk on their lawn. The crowds are going to start passing by in about an hour on their way to a late-afternoon game. Calgary’s team is in town. The game is sold out. With all that cheering, the crowds are sure to be thirsty. Evan remembers that his mom just bought a whole lot of no-name pop. He doesn’t like no-name pop. Then an idea hits him! What if he sold the pop to people on their way to the game? His mom doesn’t want to give him the pop—it was an amazing sale with the pop selling for a quarter a can—but Evan offers to buy it from her for 30 cents, and he promises to stop bugging her about the new snowboard.

Tasha is a mom and a football fan. She’s one of those 60 000 people on their way to the game. Her husband and her teenaged kids are already complaining about being thirsty. She should have brought some pop from home, she thinks.

Then she sees a smiling boy standing behind a little table on the front lawn of a nearby house. She notices a sign taped to the table—Pop $1. It’s no-name pop for a dollar. Outrageous, Tasha thinks, but there’s Evan, pulling icy-cold pop from a cooler. Tasha remembers the price and the huge crowds and line-ups inside the stadium. “I’ll take five,” Tasha says. “Have you got change for a twenty?”

“I’ve only got a ten-dollar bill,” Evan replies. “The rest are twenties.” Tasha looks him straight in the eye to determine whether or not he’s lying. People are waiting impatiently, and Tasha knows that twenty won’t go too far inside the football stadium.

“Okay,” Tasha says, “I’ll take ten.” She knows she’ll need them before the end of the game.

The interaction between Evan and Tasha shows how the demand for a product when met with a supply of that product (in this case, pop) meets a need and creates wealth.

Can people get wealth all on their own, or do they need to trade with others in order to generate wealth? In this lesson you will discover how the same economic principles that created a few dollars for Evan are also at work globally as you explore the issue question: How is wealth generated?

Supply and Demand

A boy wants to make money. He knows he can only make money by selling a service or a product. He sees a need. Football fans are walking by his house, among them a mom with a husband and three children. They want something to drink. Economists call this a demand. Evan has a certain number of cans of pop he can sell. This is called the supply(or "quantity" in the graphs below). The price is an unspoken agreement between Evan and his customers. Evan is willing to sell his pop for a dollar a can. Fans are willing to buy them for the same price.

In this first graph, the blue line represents the football fans’ demand for pop. The red line represents Evan’s supply of pop. He is trying to sell his pop for the most money he can get, and the fans are trying to buy it for the cheapest possible price. At some point, the two must meet. The price at which Evan is willing to sell his pop is equal to the amount fans are willing to pay for it.
What would happen if Su Yin from next door saw how successful Evan was at selling no-name pop, and a couple of games later, she set up her own stand? It’s another hot day and Calgary’s back in town, so the demand for pop is just the same. However, the supply has suddenly increased. People are used to buying from Evan, so Su Yin drops the price of her pop to 75 cents to attract customers. To keep up with the competition, Evan must do the same. The second graph demonstrates what happens to the price when the supply increases.

Another thing that could drive the selling price of Evan’s pop down is decreased demand. Suppose it’s a really cold day and the Eskimos are playing Toronto. There are probably far fewer people wanting a cold pop, so he might have to drop his price to sell his pop.

Take a look look at the third graph. Here you see a decreased demand, as represented by the blue lines. When the demand drops, it drives the price down.

When there are many buyers and sellers of products, selling more or less the same products, and all other factors are more or less equal, there is perfect competition.

Perfect competition requires

  • many buyers and sellers
  • goods and services that are essentially the same
  • everyone having access to information
  • everyone having access to the same production processes
  • everyone having access to the same resources
  • all firms having access to production technologies
  • resources being perfectly mobile
  • anyone being able to enter or leave the market

When there is perfect competition between sellers, everyone benefits because the costs to the buyers are driven down so that the sellers make the smallest amount of profit. Sellers who are trying to stay competitive need to improve their products or sell for less.

The world’s natural resources are not equally distributed. Some countries have a lot of needed materials, while other nations have a lot of some specific goods. Some nations have very little of any marketable product. For example, Canada is rich in its supply of oil and gas, forest products, water, and agricultural goods. A country such as Ethiopia, in Africa, has very few natural resources to sell.

The world’s population is also not equally spread out. If you have ever travelled around Canada, you will know that you can drive for a long time without seeing many people. Canada is a sparsely populated country for many reasons, especially because of its climate and geography. Examine the maps above. In what countries are most of the world’s people located? The distribution of population and resources are two factors that explain the distribution of wealth around the world. Other factors include physical location, industrialization, political and economic systems, and the values held by people in different parts of the world.

  • Physical Location. Some countries are well located in order to trade. Countries where roads are easy to build or where oceans and rivers can be used for shipping can increase their prosperity. Nations that are land-locked, or completely surrounded by land, have a much harder time of getting their supply of goods to potential buyers.
  • Industrialization. Being able to manufacture products creates wealth. Countries that industrialized early in history became wealthy sooner, while countries such as China and India are getting richer today because of their industrial growth.
  • Political and Economic Systems. Different political and economic systems can lead to different degrees of economic success. They can also determine how wealth is shared within a country. Some governments value equality, while others encourage individuals and corporations to make personal profit. In Grade 12 you will investigate these ideologies more.
  • Values. As you determined in Lesson 1, many of the people in some nations don't value money in the same way most people do in other nations. The majority of people in some nations may put less value on becoming rich and more on such matters as their religious beliefs or having a balanced lifestyle.

How do nations depend on each other for wealth?

Different parts of the world have differing supplies of natural resources, land, labour, and capital needed to expand their industries. Some countries have a lot of goods that are not in high demand, so they are hard to sell. Other nations, such as China, have a large supply of workers. Canada can export wood and pulp to China where it can be used to create manufactured goods by the large labour force and sold back to Canada. Both countries prosper.

Trade doesn’t always include money for goods and services. In 2007, the government of Venezuela agreed to provide oil to Cuba in exchange for a supply of trained doctors. This exchange benefited both nations.

Do global exchanges always benefit everyone? Go back to Evan and Su Yin. They are in perfect competition as they sell soft drinks outside their homes. But what would happen if a store opened up nearby? What would happen if that store was part of a large chain or even a large transnational chain of convenience stores?

A convenience store could offer better selection of pop and other products people might want to buy. It would have a much larger supply and a much larger demand. Its economies of scale would make its per-unit costs lower, so it could sell its products for a cheaper price, and its brand-name recognition and marketing power would give it a lot of advantages over the small local pop stands. There would no longer be perfect competition.

The same is true for most businesses around the world. As companies get larger and larger, they can distribute their goods far and wide. Since they have a large distribution, they can sell goods for less, and because they are well known, they can attract lots of business.

Most of the nations of the world have become interdependent. They depend on each other to sell their own goods and buy the goods of others. In this way, trade has been an important factor in the expansion of global connections. Does this trading system benefit everyone equally? As the pop stand example shows, there isn’t always perfect competition. Some—especially those who are already wealthy and powerful—may benefit more than others in the global economy.

How has capitalism contributed to the expansion of global economic ties?

Over the past few decades, more and more countries have embraced capitalism as an economic system. Capitalism is an economic system based on free markets, the private ownership of property, and the profit motive. In this photo you can see a busy shopping district in Chengdu, China. China has recently embraced capitalism after years of being a communist country. Now that China has started to produce consumer goods for the global market, its wealth has increased tremendously.

You learned about early capitalism in Unit 2 and it was the focus of your social studies in Grade 9. The following information is for review.

Adam Smith lived in the 1700s. In Adam Smith’s time, his government tried to control the economy in order to protect local businesses and make more money for its own people. This practice is called protectionism. The British government put tariffs on goods that came from other countries in order to encourage people to buy local products. It put quotas on the amounts of certain goods allowed into the country in order to encourage British companies to produce what the people needed. It made all businesses buy licenses, in order to control industry.

Another form of protectionism is subsidies. Governments also try to protect local industries through subsidizing the production of their own goods. That means the government gives extra money to individuals or companies that produce certain goods so that they can compete internationally. This protects the industry, but it can also harm that same industry in other countries where there are no subsidies.

Adam Smith thought that all these regulations were not good for individuals because the regulations interfered with peoples’ freedoms. He also thought the regulations harmed industry. He thought that if all people in all countries worked to benefit themselves, there would be more competition, better products, and lower prices for shoppers. In other words, he thought the government should not try to control the economy. He thought that if it was left to run on its own, everyone would benefit. He called this the “invisible hand.”

There are many factors involved in the production of goods and services. These include the wealth or capital needed to start or expand any operation; the land and property used in production; and the people who do the work, sometimes called the “labour.” In a capitalist system, land, capital, and resource factors are mostly owned or managed by private individuals or corporations. In a capitalist system, it is believed that private ownership is the best way to manage an economy. Capitalists believe that the freedom to make choices about how to run a business should be given to individual people. The potential for wealth encourages people to work hard and to compete for business. The wealth generated by the rich through their business enterprises will “trickle down” to the workers, who will demand and deserve higher wages if their products sell. Through competition, lower-priced goods will be made available to everyone, reducing the cost of living. Everyone who is willing to work will benefit.

Adam Smith’s ideas caught on all around the world. Today, people talk about “free trade.” Free trade happens when all industries, individuals, and governments are free to operate with very few restrictions. The assumption is that when there are few barriers, such as tariffs, to prevent trade between countries, connections across the globe are forged. Businesses operating in countries that have a lot of natural resources, such as Canada, sell products to countries that don’t have any. Countries that have a large labour force, such as India and China, sell goods and services produced by their people who earn less per hour. The idea is that if there is a “level playing field,” or a system where every country uses the same rules, everyone wins.

Did communism contribute to contemporary global connections?

Capitalism is not the only kind of economic system. Imagine a country where all the money in the country was equally shared. People didn’t own their own property, but they all worked for the common good. People were provided with everything they needed to survive by the central government. If the nation itself became richer, the extra money was shared out to all the people.

Communism is an economic system that was a reaction to capitalism. The ideas behind communism came from a man named Karl Marx. Karl Marx was the son of a wealthy industrialist. He saw first-hand the inequality and poor working and living conditions that people suffered during the Industrial Revolution. In his book, The Communist Manifesto, Marx stated his belief that people’s labour should not be exploited to benefit the rich and that factors of production should not be in the hands of a few rich people. He believed they should be owned by the community at large, and all the benefits of people’s hard work should be shared out to people according to their needs.

He believed that the workers of the world would rise up against their capitalist oppressors and take over the running of the country. His ideas were taken up by powerful groups in many countries, especially those where there was extreme inequality. Communist ideas led to revolutions in Russia in 1917, in Vietnam in 1945, in China in 1949, and in Cuba in 1959. By 2008, China, Vietnam, and Cuba still have some form of communist government, as does Laos and North Korea. This led to global connections in its own way, as Communist countries forged connections with other Communist governments.

Communism worked to a certain extent in that communist countries were able to use the strong hand of government to industrialize and modernize their economies and equalize wealth. However, it also led to conflicts between capitalist and communist countries. Ideas, technology, and the exchange of goods were cut off between countries with different political beliefs. The communist system of government ownership involves a lot of rules about what can be bought and sold.

The conflict between capitalism and communism was an important factor in twentieth century history because it kept the nations of the world apart and prevented the globalization of the economy. You will learn more about this issue in future lessons.

Historic Events of the Twentieth Century

World War I, 1914–1918 

World War I was the world’s first truly global war. The initial event that started the war was the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria, but there were many real causes. The reasons included these:

  • competition between European nations for global power, including power over colonies
  • military build up, especially between Great Britain and Germany
  • alliances and secret agreements between nations, in which countries promised to support each other in times of war

While the war began as a dispute between European powers, almost every nation in the world eventually became involved because many countries were either a colony or former colony of a European country. For example, Canada was a colony of Great Britain, so it went to war when Britain went to war. This war had devastating effects on the world’s people. Over 40 million people died in battle or because of disease or poverty caused by the war. The war disrupted the global economy because of the amount of money that went into creating weapons and later into rebuilding war-torn Europe.

Creation of the Soviet Union, 1922

Russia was a huge country that was controlled by a powerful and extremely rich royal family and the upper class of people. There was a great deal of inequality between the rich and the poor, and the average person had no say in what happened in government. While many other nations of the world (especially in Europe) had industrialized and become somewhat democratic, Russia had not. During the October Revolution of 1917, the Communist Party took power in Russia in an attempt to improve the lives of the people. After a lengthy revolution, which partly took place during World War I, the Soviet Union was formed in 1922. The establishment of a communist country set the stage for future conflict.

Formation and Collapse of the League of Nations, 1920–1946

After World War I, the powerful nations of the world decided that some kind of international organization should be created in order to prevent future conflicts such as World War I. The League of Nations had many goals, including the right of nations to rule themselves, ending slavery and prostitution, and reducing the number of weapons produced around the world. The League of Nations was formed with representatives of most European nations. Many important nations, including the United States, never joined, while others came and went. The league did not have any military force and was unable to resolve many of the conflicts that occurred. Its ultimate failure to achieve its goals was shown when World War II began.

The Great Depression, 1929–1939


The Great Depression was a worldwide downturn in the economy. It devastated industrialized nations and the less-industrialized countries that sold raw materials. It had a negative impact on international trade, including heavy industry, mining, logging, and farming. It forced governments to react in different ways in order to protect their people. Some governments developed more protectionist policies in order to promote and preserve local jobs and income.

World War II, 1939–1945

World War II was the second global war that happened in the twentieth century. It involved over 100 million military personnel and resulted in over 70 million deaths, most of them civilian.

There were many causes for World War II, including unresolved disagreements resulting from World War I. An important reason was the Treaty of Versailles, the international agreement that ended the First World War. In this treaty, Germany was forced to accept most of the blame for the war and make war reparations, or repayments, to other nations. This, coupled with the Great Depression, led to a lot of unhappiness in Germany. The German people elected Adolf Hitler as their leader, and he eventually turned Germany into a fascist dictatorship. Germany rebuilt its military and began to aggressively expand into other nations. By the time other nations of the world stepped in, Germany had a powerful war machine. Again, colonies and former colonies joined the war. The war was further intensified by military action in Asia, as Japan also fought to expand its territory in other parts of Asia. Two nuclear bombs were exploded in Japan, which effectively ended the war.

The Cold War, 1945–1990

The ideas behind the ideologies of communism and capitalism are very different. Capitalists think that if people are given a lot of freedom to own property and make money, everyone will work hard and everyone will benefit. On the other hand, communists believe that prosperity should be shared; they do not agree with the large differences between rich and poor that take place in capitalist nations. Instead, communists believe the government should manage the factors of production in order to benefit everyone equally.

These two ways of thinking divided the world for many years. Two “superpowers,” the United States and the Soviet Union, competed to have their ideas dominate the world economy. As a result, the world became polarized into two large blocks, with capitalist nations on one side and communist nations on the other. These two sides traded primarily among themselves. There was limited movement of people between the two sides, and most communication was hostile. 

The communist way of managing the economy was not entirely successful. People often would not work as hard as they could if they did not see a direct benefit for themselves. Government mismanagement also led to economic failure. Failures in the communist system, along with limits on trade, movement, and communication, prevented a lot of economic growth, especially in the communist nations. But capitalism isn't a perfect system either, and it often results in the huge gaps between rich and poor that the communists have tried to eliminate.

As well, this period in history saw a huge amount of money being spent on arms, including nuclear weapons. The Collapse of the Soviet Union, 1991

By the end of the 1980s, the Soviet Union was having a great deal of difficulty meeting the needs of its citizens. There were complaints about limited products including food. State-owned businesses fell into the hands of a few, including members of crime organizations, who became extremely rich. A lot of money left the country. Many educated people moved away. Today, however, the Russian economy is growing. More and more people are working.

Foreign Investment and Ownership in Canada
Another impact of trade liberalization in Canada is increased foreign investment and ownership. This has been an issue throughout Canadian history. Some Canadians are concerned that increased foreign investment and ownership result in the loss of identity and economic independence in Canada. This concern is called economic nationalism—for Canadians, this usually means we do not want to be taken over or absorbed by the United States. This concern is understandable, since we live next door to the most powerful military and industrial country in the world.

Control of Canada's Economy
Canada's population is about the same as that of California, so the United States sometimes tends to take Canada for granted. It is hard for Canada to do the same, especially since 86 per cent of our exports go to the United States. Smaller Canadian companies cannot buy out large American businesses. However, when the reverse happens, it sets off alarm bells for economic nationalists. The last time this happened was in the 1970s, when there was growing public concern about the level of foreign ownership and investment in a number of sectors (especially oil and gas, mining, and manufacturing). This concern led to the creation of the Foreign Investment Review Agency (FIRA) in 1973 by the federal Liberal government. FIRA was part of a broader industrial strategy, based on economic nationalism, that tried to stop further US control of the Canadian economy. FIRA's purpose was to screen foreign takeovers and the creation of new businesses or subsidiaries by non-Canadians.

According to one position, FIRA did reduce the volume of foreign takeovers. According to another position, FIRA actually did little except rubber-stamp nearly all foreign investments and takeovers. After the Progressive Conservatives were elected in 1984, Prime Minister Brian Mulroney announced that Canada was "open for business" again and replaced FIRA with Investment Canada. Its purpose was to encourage foreign investment in Canada. Since then, more than 11 000 Canadian businesses have been purchased by foreign companies.

From Foreign Ownership to Globalization
The issue of foreign ownership and investment as seen by Canadian economic nationalists has basically disappeared because of globalization. The Mulroney government not only "opened Canada for business," but it also signed the Free Trade Agreement with the United States in 1988 and NAFTA in 1992. Since then, the trend has been toward economic globalization with more free trade and open borders.

Microcredit is another example of people helping people. Conditions grim in Sierra Leone’s diamond mines

4 September 2007 

Blogged by Kate Thomas

It could have been a scene from a movie. Another long, hot day was coming to an end and the African sun was sinking into the dusty red earth. As the blue sky blushed, he knelt in front of me and held out a sparkling rock. But this was no proposal. And if it were a movie it could only have been Blood Diamond.

Last year the Hollywood blockbuster brought the story of Sierra Leone’s brutal diamond-fuelled war to the silver screen. The conflict ended in 2001 and since then the recovering West African nation has made great strides in combating illegal exports of blood diamonds.

Sierra Leone’s stone exports are on track to reach £80 million ($160 million) this year—the highest level since the end of the war. But that’s not to say the gem trade is cut and polished. Profit margins between dealers and miners remain stark, and conditions in the country's dirty malarial pits still leave much to be desired.

I had woken early that morning, long before the sun, for the three-hour drive to a diamond pit near Sierra Leone’s border with Liberia. The night before, eating in a dusty, noisy chop house in town, I met a Sierra Leonean diamond dealer who had offered to take me to his mine.

As fat raindrops wash rubber plantations, we drive through cassava fields and clusters of dark green banana trees. Eventually we pull up at a traditional mud-hut village where groups of women sit singing as they cook rice beneath zinc verandas. From here it is a short hike to the diamond mine. As we walk, streams of ants zigzag beneath our feet and the diamond dealer talks.

“I have around 20 guys working for me,” he says, sucking on a cigarette. “But it's so hard to get good miners. I had to fire someone yesterday for fighting over rice at lunchtime,” he says, rolling his eyes. “We mine for everything here . . . red diamonds, gold diamonds, white diamonds, rubies . . . . This country is full of natural riches.”

Despite being blessed with a seemingly inexhaustible supply of minerals, Sierra Leone consistently ranks as one of the poorest countries in the world. The average salary for a labourer working in a diamond mine is 100,000 leones a month, or $30. That doesn't go very far around here.

At the mine I meet Thomas, a 48-year old labourer standing thigh-deep in the cool waters of the pit. He’s in the middle of telling a joke when I arrive. “We don't earn very much, but we try to smile,” he later tells me. “Maybe one day I’ll find a big diamond and become a rich man.”

Six other labourers are working hard, concealed behind a wall of thick elephant grass. It’s early but the sun is already beating down. Thomas pulls off a ripped t-shirt and throws it down on a pile of sand.

I watch as the miners dig, sift and pan for diamonds from early morning to dusk. At noon, women from the nearby village bring communal bowls of Jollof rice, a traditional dish of chicken, tomato and onion.

One of the miners is named Borbor. “My name sounds like Poor Boy,” he says with a cheeky smile. “When I become wealthy I’ll change it to Rich Boy.”

Everyone laughs, probably because we all know it isn't likely to happen anytime soon.

Government estimates say 200,000 to 300,000 labourers work in the country’s diamond mines. Many of those are supporting families, holding onto the hope that they might strike it lucky one day. It’s a constant gamble. 

When a diamond is found, the rewards are meagre. Dusk is falling when Borbor spots a stone the size of a grain of rice. The diamond dealer pays him 10,000 leones ($3.50) for his effort. That might buy a few loaves of bread, a wedge of processed cheese, a box of matches and the most precious thing of all—a lift to work in the morning. The other miners will walk. 

Later, as he blows a cloud of marijuana smoke into my face, the diamond dealer tells me he hopes to make around $15,000 from Borbor’s diamond. He plans to sell it to a retailer in North America. 

“Life is good,” the dealer laughs. He raises his glass of sweet palm wine but I'm already thinking of Borbor and I don’t meet his gaze. 

The trade in conflict diamonds may be a thing of the past in Sierra Leone but conditions in the mines are far from good. As Sierra Leone prepares to go to the polls for the second round of presidential elections on September 8, all eyes are on the importance of curbing corruption and encouraging transparency. 

Until miners like Borbor are paid fairly for their work, my glass will remain on the table. I can’t think of anything to drink to.

In countries where there is a lot of corruption, government officials may turn a blind eye to lawbreakers in exchange for bribes.
  • International Law. There are international laws about labour practices. However, these laws have to be enforced locally, and if state governments and police don’t want to follow these laws, there is little than can be done legally.
  • International Organizations. International organizations such as the United Nations can set up international guidelines and policies. Again, it’s up to local people to make sure the guidelines and policies are followed.

  • Public Pressure. One of the most powerful ways that a transnational corporation can be forced to make sure decent working conditions and environmental standards are met is through public pressure. If the international community, including people like you who buy goods, refuses to purchase items from companies with a questionable record, that puts pressure on companies to change their ways.
  • Shareholders. Transnational corporations are owned by shareholders who each own shares in the corporation. In most corporations, shareholders vote on the actions of the company. If they feel that unfair practices are being conducted by their corporation, they can vote to change things. On the other hand, if their perspective is that money is the bottom line, they may leave things as they are.

Subsidized Rice in Haiti

Haiti is the least-developed country in the Western Hemisphere and one of the poorest nations in the world. Haiti was one of the first parts of the Americas to be colonized by Spain. How does global economic growth affect the world’s environment?

Global economic growth has a powerful effect on the production and consumption of products. As people get richer, they are able to buy more products. Does global economic growth affect everyone’s environment equally?

Economic activity affects everyone. Previously, you explored supply and demand. When supplies are limited, prices increase. Climate change can result from natural changes to Earth's weather, but most of the world's scientific community—including the UN’s Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change and the U.S. National Academy of Sciences—supports the position that “most of the warming observed over the last 50 years is attributable to human activities.”

Striking a Balance

People around the world want to prosper, but they also want the world to continue to sustain itself so that future generations can also have a good quality of life. How should we respond to the environmental effects of globalization?

What does the world we want look like? Is it a natural world where people can enjoy the wilderness and live in harmony with their surroundings? Is it an urban, industrial landscape where all natural resources are used to produce goods just for people to use? Or is it a combination of both? If people want to become richer, the natural world will be affected. In a world of global economic growth, the effects are enormous.

The kind of world we want depends to an extent on our worldview and our beliefs about where humankind fits into the world. Some people believe that Earth exists to serve humankind; others believe that people should be stewards of the land.

No matter your worldview, the question of environmental sustainability remains.

How do worldviews affect responses to environmental issues?

People around the world have different perspectives about stewardship and sustainability, based on their values and life experiences. 

A First Nations view of nature is that mankind is part of nature, and mankind should not dominate but should instead live in harmony with the natural world. This perspective is common to Indigenous people the world over. The way of life for most Indigenous peoples is dependent on living in harmony with nature.

Many other cultures also believe that nature is sacred and that human beings have a responsibility to care for it and protect it. This belief can stem from a love and worship of nature itself or from other religious beliefs, including the belief that the universe was created by a supreme being; because the environment was created by a supreme being, it should be protected and cared for by mankind.

  • Environmentalism. Environmentalism is the perspective that people should care about the natural world. People with environmentalist perspectives want to conserve natural resources and prevent pollution. If you are an environmentalist, you believe that, as human beings, we must protect and preserve the world around us.
  • Imperialism. Imperialism is another perspective about how people should treat the environment. In political terms, imperialism is the domination of one country over another. In the environment, imperialism means the control or domination of the environment by human beings. Imperialists believe that human beings should dominate the environment because the world is designed to serve their needs. Science and technology are used to harness nature and make its materials useful to human beings. People with this view of nature often believe that while human use of the environment might produce problems, any resulting problems can be solved by science.

The Dakota Access Pipeline and Standing Rock

An example of where some of these beliefs about the environment clash is in the controversy over the Dakota Access Pipeline, which would go through the Standing Rock reservation in North Dakota.


A map of First Nations reservations in the North-Central United States. Standing Rock can be seen on the border of North and South Dakota. Image taken from wikimedia.org

The pipeline has seen significant opposition from First Nations on the reservation, citing the fact that the pipeline would impact territory they consider to be sacred, and arguing that the pipeline may contaminate the water in the Mississippi river. They place concerns of the environment and sovereignty (control over one's own affairs) first and foremost. This has led to widespread protests against the construction of the pipeline by both the Sioux people living on the reserve, and many other First Nations from across North America. Meanwhile proponents of the pipeline argue that pipelines are a safe method of transporting oil, and that the construction of the pipeline is necessary to take full advantage of the oil supply in North Dakota. This viewpoint prioritizes potential economic benefits of the pipeline.

The conflict over the pipeline has drawn international attention for a number of reasons. These include the issue of how to balance economic environmental concerns, the issue of sovereignty for First Nations and what say they have over their own lands, and human rights issues, as the often aggressive measures taken by law enforcement and the national guard on the reserve has led to an investigation of possible rights violations by the UN.

How should governments respond?

What kinds of laws can governments establish to protect citizens and the environment? Are local laws enough, or are global regulations needed?

Laws have been created around resource development and environmental protection. These are two areas where governments have stepped in to ensure that there is some degree of stewardship of the land.

The environmental impacts of industry and daily living do not respect the borders of nations or provinces. When pollution occurs in one country, other nations are affected. Greenhouse gases do not affect the climate of only the nation in which they are produced—they affect everyone. Ironically, their effects are even worse in areas where few emissions exist, such as sub-Saharan Africa and the Far North.

Are international agreements needed to ensure sustainable development? While individuals can take many measures to limit their negative impacts on the planet, are laws sometimes required to force corporations and individuals to make changes to protect the planet and ensure our survival? Because pollution, environmental degradation, and global warming do not respect political borders, many people believe that international agreements are necessary.

The Montreal Protocol is one piece of environmental legislation that has had a positive impact on the environment. The Montreal Protocol deals with the ozone layer, which protects Earth from harmful UV rays. The very tiny layer of ozone in Earth's atmosphere has been getting thinner over the years, and season holes have been reported in this layer over the poles. One of the chief causes for the thinning of the ozone layer is CFCs, or chlorofluorocarbons—the chemical compounds found in air conditioners, fridges, and aerosol sprays.

The Montreal Protocol, or Montreal Protocol on Substances That Deplete the Ozone Layer, is an international treaty designed to protect the ozone layer. It became law in January of 1989 and has been adopted worldwide. It has been called "the single most successful international agreement to date" by Kofi Anan of the United Nations. Since it was signed, the atmospheric concentrations of the most important chlorofluorocarbons have either leveled or decreased. On the negative side, CFCs were largely replaced with chemical compounds called HCFCs, which contribute to global warming. HCFCs are scheduled to be phased out by the year 2030.

The world’s largest intact ecosystem is the boreal area of Canada. About 35 percent of Canada is boreal forest. In an effort to conserve the boreal region of Canada, an unusual alliance of groups, including industry, First Nations, and non-profit organizations, created a plan that would protect and preserve this part of the world.

Diverse groups such as Suncor in the north-eastern Alberta tar sands; Alberta Pacific Forest Products, which owns and operates a large pulp mill; Ducks Unlimited, a non-profit organization that preserves waterfowl habitat; the World Wildlife Federation, the world's largest conservation organization; and the Dehcho First Nation formed a leadership committee.

The Canadian Boreal Initiative was a plan to “conserve the cultural, sustainable economic and natural values of the entire Canadian boreal region by employing the principles of conservation biology to protect at least half of the region by supporting sustainable communities, and developing and implementing good stewardship practices.” 

Environmental organizations are diverse groups of individuals and organizations who band together to advocate for the sustainable management of resources and the protection of the natural environment. These organizations have different goals and use different means to accomplish them. However, at the root of all of these groups is the desire to preserve the planet through changes in public policy and individual action.

Environmentalists come from many backgrounds and have a number of reasons to try to preserve and protect Earth. The environmental movement ranges from large international organizations to grassroots movements of people who act at the community level. Environmentalists may be scientists, First Nations, social activists, religious practitioners, trappers, wilderness adventurers, or sport fishers among others. Some of the main issues they address include the preservation of natural habitat, the protection of species, global population growth, poverty, and making corporations accountable for their activities.

Environmental organizations perform many functions ranging from scientific research, to monitoring pollution and endangered species and their habitats, to lobbying governments and industry. Some organizations even resort to direct action to prevent environmental destruction. The actions of environmental organizations frequently draw public attention to important issues, which often results in government or industry action.

How should individuals respond?

What about you? Seventy percent of Albertan consumers stated they were prepared to switch to banks, retail outlets, and other service providers that demonstrated a commitment to the environment. Many Canadians say they are prepared to make personal sacrifices in their lifestyles to protect the environment; others say they will do so but only if industry is prepared to take its share of the burden.

 Here are some ideas:

  • Consume less. Don't buy so much stuff!
  • Drive less. Walk, bike, carpool, or take public transit.
  • Avoid packaging and plastic bags whenever possible.
  • Replace incandescent light bulbs with compact fluorescent bulbs.
  • Change your eating habits. Eat less meat, consume organic produce, and buy locally-grown products to reduce your impact on the environment.
  • Recycle. The average household can save up to 1100 kilograms of carbon dioxide per year by recycling half of its household waste.
  • Use less hot water. Install a low-flow shower head, or take shorter or fewer showers. Turn down the heat on your hot water heater by one or two degrees.
  • Adjust your thermostat. Move your thermostat down two degrees in the winter (Wear a sweater!) and reduce the use of air-conditioning in the summer to save over 900 kilograms of carbon dioxide a year.
  • Plant a tree. One tree absorbs nearly a tonne of carbon dioxide in its lifetime.
  • Unplug your electronic devices when they are not in use. If you are using a rechargeable device, unplug it when it is finished recharging.
  • Tell others. Spread the word. Working together, we can make a difference.
