Source note page 9

Neuron Structure
  • Dendrites are short thread-like branches which act to accept nerve impulses from other neurons and carry them towards the cell body, which is the main part of the cell, containing the nucleus and other organelles
  • Axons are longer branches which carry nerve impulses away from the cell body
  • A myelin sheath surrounds many axons, insulating the neuron and speeding up the rate of impulse transmission.
View original source slide

Source note page 10

Neuron Structure — continued
  • Schwann Cells, a type of glial cell, are responsible for producing the myelin that surrounds each axon.
  • In myelinated axons, gaps between Schwann cells are referred to as the nodes of Ranvier. Electrical impulses “jump” from node to node, which is referred to as saltatory conduction. This speeds up transmission, avoiding having to travel down the entire length of the axon.
  • Once the electrical signal reaches the axon terminal, it is then passed on to the dendrites of an adjoining neuron.
  • Synapse: the space/junction between two adjoining neurons

Conduction of Nerve Impulses (Electrical Event)

A nerve impulse or action potential has both a chemical and electrical component (hence the term electrochemical impulse)

Four stages of a nerve impulse:

  1. Polarized/resting state
  2. Depolarization
  3. Repolarization
  4. Refractory Period

Source note page 22

Conduction of Nerve Impulses (Electrical Event) — continued
  1. Resting State

The difference in charge across the membrane of a resting neuron is

called resting membrane potential.

  • The inside of a neuron has a slight negative charge at rest, whereas the outside has a slight positive charge.
  • This results in a resting potential of
  • 70 mV in most neurons
  • Note: we often use specific numbers for voltages in our examples, but these can change from one neuron to another
View original source slide

Source note page 23

Resting State & the Sodium-Potassium Pump
  1. Inside the resting cell, there exists a higher concentration of potassium (K+) than sodium (Na+); outside the cell, the opposite is true.
  2. Potassium ions are able to passively diffuse out of the cell more easily than sodium ions can diffuse into the (K+ = “leaky” ion channels)
  3. In an attempt to balance this difference, the sodium-potassium pump along the membrane surface + will pump two K back into the cell in exchange for + three Na
  4. As a result, as excess of positive charge accumulates outside of the cell membrane, causing the inside of the cell to become negatively charged relative to the outside. A constant membrane potential of -70 mV is maintained.
View original source slide

Source note page 24

Resting State & the Sodium-Potassium Pump — continued
  1. Depolarization

Action potential occurs when a neuron is stimulated by an electrical impulse.

  1. If a strong enough impulse is received and the threshold potential is reached (approx. -55 mV), sodium gates will open, thus allowing Na+ to diffuse freely across the membrane.
  2. Na+ rushes into the cell (down their concentration gradient → facilitated diffusion), leading to a slight positive charge on the inside relative to the outside.
  3. This reverses the membrane potential from -70 mV to +40 mV; the membrane is now said to be depolarized.
  4. Once Na+ reaches equilibrium, the membrane once again becomes impermeable to them, and the gates close.
View original source slide

Source note page 25

Depolarization & Threshold Potential

An action potential is considered to be an “all-or-none” event because any stimulus that fails to achieve the threshold membrane potential of at least -55 mV will have no effect

  • Note that increasing the stimulus strength does not increase the impulse strength; a neuron will either fire or not fire
  • The intensity of a stimulus is instead experienced as an increased frequency of nerve impulses (i.e. a more intense stimulus will simply cause more neurons to depolarize)
View original source slide

Source note page 26

Depolarization & Threshold Potential — continued
  1. Repolarization

Once the action potential has peaked, Na+ gates close, and K+ gates open.

  • This restores the positive charge on the outside of the membrane; however, Na+ and K+ concentrations are briefly reversed compared to at the resting state (Na+ is higher inside the membrane)
  • The K+ channels are slow to close, which results in the hyperpolarization of the neuron. This means that the inside becomes slightly more negative than the resting potential.
View original source slide

Source note page 27

Na+/ K+ exchange pump helps reset the correct distribution of ions
  • Even though during repolarization the inside becomes more negative again compared to the outside, we do not have the correct distribution of Na+ and K+ across the membrane. The sodium-potassium exchange pump helps reset the original distribution by pumping 3 Na+ out for every 2 K+ it pumps in.

Signal Transduction across a Synapse (Chemical Event)
  • Once the impulse has traveled down the length of the axon, it reaches the axon terminal.
  • The axon terminal is in close contact with the dendrites of another neuron.
  • For the signal to move to the next neuron, it must cross the space between the axon terminal and the dendrites of the subsequent cell; this space is referred to as the synapse.

Source note page 32

Neurotransmitter Release

Neurotransmitters are required to help transmit the electrical impulse from one neuron to another. These are stored in vesicles in the axon bulb.

  1. An action potential reaches the axon terminal
  2. Calcium channel “gates” within the axon terminal open, causing calcium ions to flow into the cell
  3. Calcium ions trigger the movement and fusing of the vesicles with the presynaptic membrane (exocytosis). In turn, neurotransmitters are released into the synapse.
  4. Neurotransmitters diffuse across the synaptic cleft to the post-synaptic membrane

Source note page 33

Neurotransmitter Binding
  1. Once neurotransmitters have reached the post-synaptic membrane, they bind with receptors embedded in the membrane
  2. If the neurotransmitter is excitatory, binding induces an action potential in the postsynaptic neuron
  3. Neurotransmitters are then released by the receptors and either return to the presynaptic neuron or are broken down by enzymes such as cholinesterase on the post-synaptic membrane

Extra: The Stress Response and Anxiety

The stress response, which triggers when our bodies perceive a threat, served an important evolutionary purpose in helping our ancestors survive dangerous situations. However, in modern times, stressors often aren't physical threats but rather psychological ones like exams, social pressures, or work deadlines. While the stress response can still help us cope with these challenges, if it's triggered too frequently or intensely, it can lead to chronic stress and anxiety. This is because our bodies aren't designed to handle prolonged periods of stress, and the constant activation of the stress response can have negative effects on both our physical and mental health, contributing to conditions like anxiety disorders.

Sensation

Sensation occurs when neural impulses generated by the stimulation of sensory receptors arrive at the cerebral cortex. Perception occurs in the brain as it makes sense of the received input.

Example: When a ray of sunlight hits your skin, sensory receptors send a message to the brain, which receives and processes this information, causing you to feel warmth.
each person’s unique perception of sensation depends on how their cerebral cortex interprets this sensory information

Source note page 79

Anatomy of the Eye & Vision
  • Light enters the eye through the cornea, which is the clear part of the sclera in front of the eye.
  • The iris, is the colored part of the eye and is a muscle that contracts and relaxes to control how much light actually enters the eye through the pupil, the hole in the iris.
  • Ciliary muscles hold the lens in place and control its shape, allowing it to focus on objects closer or further away and focus the incoming light onto the retina. (this process is called accommodation)
  • The focused light strikes the retina, which is the inner layer of the back of the eye. The retina is composed of rods (sensitive to light intensity) and cones (sensitive to wavelengths of colour)
  • Photoreceptors (rods and cones) relay sensory impulses to the occipital lobe of the brain via the optic nerve
View original source slide

Source note page 80

Anatomy of the Eye & Vision
  • The aqueous humour supplies the cornea with nutrients, while the vitreous humour maintains the shape of the eye and permits light transmission to the retina.
  • The sclera protects the eye and helps to maintain its shape.
  • The choroid layer contains blood vessels which nourish the retina.
  • The fovea centralis is a location in the retina that contains a high concentration of cones, while the blind spot (the point at which the optic nerve connects to the retina) contains neither rods nor cones. This is why when light falls on the blind spot, no image is generated.

  • The retina is composed of two types of light-sensitive photoreceptors: rods and cones.

  • We rely on rods during low light or nighttime vision
  • Cones respond to high-intensity light to identify colour.
  • We rely on cones to general high visual detail when there is an abundance of light

  • Sound waves are directed by pinna (auricle) to the auditory canal, where they strike the tympanum (eardrum), generating vibrations within the middle ear.
  • When sound waves push against the tympanum, its vibrations are passed on and amplified by a set of three small connected bones, called the ossicles
