Study Three:  Sensory Deprivation

Sensory deprivation is the reduction or absence of the usual external stimuli a person receives.  Since the 1950s, many sensory deprivation experiments have been conducted.  In one experiment at McGill University (1958), individuals were placed alone in an air-conditioned room.  Each person wore goggles to reduce the amount of light entering the eye.  The person could hear nothing but a constant buzzing noise.  Each individual wore clothing to decrease tactile (touch) stimulation; hands were covered to prevent contact with objects.  

Subjects were offered $20 ($160 in today's dollars) for each day they could stay in the room.  Subjects had comfortable beds, good food, and panic buttons that they could press to end the experiment.  Researchers found that few participants could last more than two days.  At first the volunteers slept, but they soon found concentrating difficult and began to experience visual and auditory hallucinations.  Before the subjects were released, simple psychological tests were administered.  Results showed that their perceptions had become extremely disoriented.

In another study, test subjects were immersed in water.  The face masks they wore allowed them to breathe and prevented all light from stimulating the rods and cones of their eyes.  Because the subjects had little stimulation from light, noise, or clothing, they became bored and had difficulty concentrating.  The maximum time any participant was able to stay in the tank was three hours.  Participants reported feelings of unreality and panic.
