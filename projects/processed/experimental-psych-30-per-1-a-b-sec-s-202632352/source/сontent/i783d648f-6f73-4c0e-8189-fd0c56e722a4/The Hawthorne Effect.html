The Hawthorne Effect


From 1924 to 1932, studies performed at the Hawthorne Works (a tele-communications factory) explored the relationship between worker productivity (the responding variable) and various conditions (manipulated variables) such as rate of pay, rest breaks, lighting levels, etc.).  Researchers learned that each change (e.g., better lighting) resulted in an initial increase in productivity followed by a gradual return to the original level of productivity.  This result was true for individual workers as well as for groups or teams of workers.  

After testing and re-testing for productivity changes, researchers realized that regardless of what condition was changed (or if it was changed at all) workers would alter their behaviours simply because they were being observed.   This phenomenon of having behaviour temporarily change simply by observing the behaviour was termed the Hawthorne effect.  That is, people's behaviour and performance change following any new or increased attention.  People singled out for a study of any kind may improve their performance or behaviour, not because of any specific condition being tested, but simply because of the attention they receive.
