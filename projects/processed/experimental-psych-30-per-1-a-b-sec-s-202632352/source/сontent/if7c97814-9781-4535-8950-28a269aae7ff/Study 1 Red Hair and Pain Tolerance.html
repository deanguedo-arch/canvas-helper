Study One: Red Hair and Pain Tolerance

Surgeries occur every minute of every day.  Some operations require patients to receive only local anaesthetic while others require patients to be under general anaesthetic.  Special doctors called anaesthesiologists must use patient data such as age, mass (weight), and allergies in their calculations of how much general anaesthetic to use for a given patient.  The data from one study suggests that hair colour may also help anaesthesiologists determine how much anaesthetic to use.

Researcher Daniel Sessler believes the gene that produces red pigment also produces a hormone that increases pain sensitivity.  He hypothesizes that people with red hair feel pain more intensely than people with brown hair.  To test this theory, Dr. Sessler enlisted ten women with dark brown hair and ten women with red hair.  He then gave them a common anaesthetic gas followed by a mild electric shock.  If a participant twitched when shocked, the participant was given more anaesthetic until the reflex stopped.  If a participant did not twitch when shocked, the participant was given less anaesthetic until she reacted.  Results showed that the subjects with red hair needed 20% more anaesthetic than subjects with brown hair.
