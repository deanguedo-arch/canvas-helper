Can the absence of light alter your dining experience?  The answer depends on your expectations, noted the researchers in the following experiment.  A group of 32 individuals were invited to sample some new strawberry yogurt.  The test subjects were seated in a food lab and the lights were turned off.  They were then given chocolate yogurt.  Of the 32 people, 19 rated the chocolate yogurt as having good strawberry taste.  One individual said that strawberry was her favourite flavour of yogurt and that this new brand would be her new favourite brand!  In this case, the taste experience was biased by test subject imagination.  Psychologists call this phenomenon expectation assimilation or confirmation bias.

In this example, the manipulated variable was the misinformation given to the participants.  The yogurt was chocolate, not strawberry. 

The responding variable was the taste experience of the participants.

Assume the hypothesis was " If people expect a substance to taste a certain way, their taste experience of that substance will confirm their expectations."  Then the results confirm the hypothesis!

Labels, colours, odours, lighting, and expectations all affect how much we eat and how much we enjoy the experience.  Do serving dishes make a difference too?  Researchers asked diners how much they would be willing to pay for a brownie served on a paper plate (about 75 cents) compared to a brownie served on fine china (about $1.25).  The brownies were the same -- the only difference was how they were presented to the customer.  Would you pay more for the experience of eating a brownie served on fine china?

expectation assimilation or confirmation bias: the influence of the experience of taste (or another sense) by imagination and expectation
