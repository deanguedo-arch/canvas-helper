A factor fits inside. A multiple builds out.

Think of 24 objects arranged in equal rows. Six rows of four work because 6 × 4 = 24. That makes both 6 and 4 factors of 24. Seven is not a factor: it does not divide 24 with no remainder.

A multiple goes in the other direction. Start with 6 and make whole-number copies: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, … . There are only finitely many positive factors of 24, but its positive multiples continue without ending.

QuestionWhat you are looking forExample
“What divides this number?”A factor4 is a factor of 24.
“What comes in repeated groups of this number?”A multiple24 is a multiple of 6.

These two ideas lead to different problem-solving decisions. Dividing supplies into the greatest number of identical groups calls for a greatest common factor. Finding when two repeating events next coincide calls for a least common multiple.

Worked example

Write a number as prime building blocks

Determine the prime factorization of 180. A prime number has exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself.

  1. Break 180 into convenient factors.
    180 = 18 · 10

    Neither branch is prime yet. Continue each branch, rather than stopping at any product that happens to equal 180.

  2. Split until every factor is prime.
    180 = 2 · 3 · 3 · 2 · 5

    18 gives 2 × 3 × 3, and 10 gives 2 × 5. The order does not affect the product.

  3. Collect repeated factors.
    180 = 22 · 32 · 5

    Exponent notation records repeated multiplication. Check by multiplying: 4 × 9 × 5 = 180.

Why prime factorization helps

Every composite whole number greater than 1 can be broken into prime factors. Different factor trees can end at the same prime factors in a different order. That gives us a reliable way to compare numbers.

180
1810
2 · 3 · 32 · 5

1 is neither prime nor composite. It has only one positive factor, so it does not belong at the end of a prime factor tree.

Worked example

Choose the shared part—or enough for both

Find the GCF and LCM of 48 and 60.

  1. Write both prime factorizations.
    48 = 24 · 3; 60 = 22 · 3 · 5

    Write repeated primes carefully. Each exponent tells you how many copies are available.

  2. For the GCF, keep only what both numbers contain.
    GCF = 22 · 3 = 12

    Both have at least two 2s and one 3. Only 60 has a 5, so 5 cannot be in a common factor.

  3. For the LCM, keep enough prime factors to include each number.
    LCM = 24 · 3 · 5 = 240

    Use the greatest required number of copies of each prime. Check: 240 ÷ 48 = 5 and 240 ÷ 60 = 4.

Check the decision, not just the calculation

Suppose 48 pencils and 60 erasers are shared into identical supply bags, with no leftovers. The greatest possible number of bags is 12: the GCF. Each bag gets four pencils and five erasers. By contrast, two routines repeated every 48 and 60 minutes line up again after 240 minutes: the LCM.

Roots undo repeated multiplication

Squaring multiplies a number by itself. Cubing multiplies three copies. A square root or cube root reverses that relationship.

Build the resultUndo itGeometric meaning
122 = 144√144 = 12A square of area 144 has side length 12.
53 = 125∛125 = 5A cube of volume 125 has edge length 5.

A perfect square is a whole number that is the square of a whole number. A perfect cube is a whole number that is the cube of a whole number. Learning common examples helps, but prime factorization explains why a number belongs in either group.

Worked example

Pair the prime factors for a square root

Determine √900 without first reaching for a calculator.

  1. Factor the number.
    900 = 22 · 32 · 52

    The prime factors can all be grouped into pairs.

  2. Take one from each identical pair.
    30 · 30 = (2 · 3 · 5)(2 · 3 · 5)

    Each side of the multiplication gets one 2, one 3 and one 5.

  3. State the principal square root.
    302 = 900

    Therefore √900 = 30. The radical symbol asks for the nonnegative square root, not ±30.

Pairs for squares. Triples for cubes.

In a perfect square’s prime factorization, each exponent is even. In a perfect cube’s prime factorization, each exponent is a multiple of three. This is why checking only the last digit is not a reliable general method.

NumberPrime factorizationWhat the groups tell us
14424 · 32All factors pair: it is a perfect square.
21623 · 33All factors form triples: it is a perfect cube.
6426Six copies can form pairs or triples: both.
7223 · 32A factor is left over in pairs and in triples: neither.

If you are asked which real numbers square to 900, both 30 and −30 work. But √900 by itself means 30. Keep the question and the notation together.

Worked example

Use a root to find a dimension

A cube-shaped container has volume 1,728 cm³. Find its edge length.

  1. Match volume to the correct operation.
    V = s3

    A cube has three equal dimensions. A square root would answer an area question, not this volume question.

  2. Group the prime factors in threes.
    1728 = 26 · 33

    Two groups of three 2s give 2 × 2. One group of three 3s gives 3.

  3. Find and check the edge.
    s = 2 · 2 · 3 = 12

    Check: 12 × 12 × 12 = 1,728. The edge is 12 cm; a length is not measured in cm³.

When the number is not a perfect power

You can still locate a root between two whole numbers. Since 7² = 49 and 8² = 64, √58 lies between 7 and 8. Since 4³ = 64 and 5³ = 125, ∛100 lies between 4 and 5. An estimate should be consistent with these bounds.

Do not write √58 = 7 just because 7 is the lower whole number. Use “between 7 and 8,” or the approximation symbol when reporting a decimal estimate.

Factoring is distribution in reverse

You already know how to expand 5(x + 4): multiply 5 by every term inside the brackets to get 5x + 20. Factoring reverses that process. The expression stays the same; its form changes from a sum to a product.

5x + 20 = 5(x + 4)

Before working with trinomials, make common-factor removal a habit. Ask two questions: what numerical factor divides every coefficient, and which variables occur in every term?

For a variable, use the smallest exponent shared by all terms. The common variable part of x³ and x² is x², not x³. If one term has no x at all, x is not a factor common to every term.

Worked example

Remove the whole common factor

Factor 18x² + 30x.

  1. Compare coefficients and variables.
    GCF = 6x

    The GCF of 18 and 30 is 6. Both terms contain x, but only the first has x².

  2. Divide every term by 6x.
    18x2 ÷ 6x = 3x; 30x ÷ 6x = 5

    The terms remaining inside the brackets must rebuild the original terms when multiplied by 6x.

  3. Write a product and verify.
    18x2 + 30x = 6x(3x + 5)

    Expand: 6x × 3x = 18x² and 6x × 5 = 30x. Both original terms return.

Correct value is not the same as complete factoring

Writing 18x² + 30x as 3x(6x + 10) is not false algebra. It expands correctly. But the inside terms still share a factor of 2, so common-factor removal is unfinished.

3x(6x + 10) = 6x(3x + 5)

A frequent error is losing a term that becomes 1. If you take 7x out of 7x² + 7x, the second term becomes 1—not zero and not nothing.

7x2 + 7x = 7x(x + 1)

The check is multiplication, not appearance. A tidy-looking pair of brackets is useful only if it expands to every original term.

Worked example

Keep signs and both variables

Remove the GCF from −18x²y − 12xy².

  1. Find the common numerical and variable parts.
    GCF = 6xy

    Both coefficients contain 6, and both terms contain at least one x and one y.

  2. Choose a convenient sign.
    −18x2 · y − 12x · y2 = −6xy(3x + 2y)

    Taking out −6xy leaves positive terms inside. An equivalent correctly signed form is also valid.

  3. Distribute the entire outside factor.
    (−6xy)(3x) + (−6xy)(2y)

    These products are −18x²y and −12xy². The variables and signs both match the original.

The large rectangle is the sum of its parts

A rectangle with sides 13 and 16 can be split into sides 10 + 3 and 10 + 6. That creates four smaller rectangles. Their areas add to the same total as 13 × 16.

(10 + 3)(10 + 6) = 100 + 60 + 30 + 18 = 208

Now replace 10 with x. The structure does not change. The four regions become x², 6x, 3x and 18. Combining the like terms gives x² + 9x + 18.

This is what an algebra-tile rectangle represents: the small pieces are the terms of the expanded polynomial; the outside lengths are its binomial factors.

Explore the two side lengths

Change the positive constants below. Watch the two x-regions and the constant rectangle. The diagram is schematic: the x-region represents a variable length, not a fixed measurement.

Worked example

Read the factors from the rectangle

A collection contains one x² region, 8x in strips, and 15 unit squares. Find binomial factors.

  1. Look for a rectangle for the 15 unit squares.
    15 = 1 · 15 = 3 · 5

    The two constant dimensions must multiply to 15.

  2. Match the x-strips as well.
    3x + 5x = 8x

    Dimensions 1 and 15 would need 16x of strips, not 8x. The constant product alone is not enough.

  3. Write the side lengths.
    x2 + 8x + 15 = (x + 3)(x + 5)

    Each full side contains an x length and a constant length. Multiplication checks both the middle term and constant.

Not every set makes the required rectangle

Consider x² + 5x + 5. Integer side additions would have to multiply to 5. The positive pair 1 and 5 adds to 6, not 5. That set cannot make the required binomial rectangle with whole-number side additions. Do not add a tile just to force an answer: that would change the polynomial.

For negative terms later, we will use signed algebraic products. A negative coefficient is not a negative physical area. Keeping that distinction prevents the model from telling a misleading story.

Use both conditions—not just one

For a trinomial whose leading coefficient is 1, expansion gives a useful pattern.

(x + p)(x + q) = x2 + (p + q)x + pq

To factor x² + bx + c, look for integers p and q that multiply to c and add to b. “Monic” means the leading coefficient is 1. This sum/product rule depends on that leading 1; a different leading coefficient needs the method in 3.6.

Write the pair you are trying, then check both conditions. You are not guessing a full answer blindly—you are narrowing a small list of factor pairs.

Worked example

Build a positive factor pair

Factor x² + 11x + 24.

  1. List factor pairs for the constant.
    1 · 24; 2 · 12; 3 · 8; 4 · 6

    The signed product must be 24. Since the middle term is positive, start with positive pairs.

  2. Choose the pair with sum 11.
    3 + 8 = 11

    The other sums are 25, 14 and 10. Only 3 and 8 satisfy both conditions.

  3. Write and expand the factors.
    x2 + 11x + 24 = (x + 3)(x + 8)

    The middle products are 8x and 3x; they combine to 11x.

Make sign decisions before searching

Constant cMiddle coefficient bSigns in the factors
PositivePositiveBoth positive
PositiveNegativeBoth negative
NegativeEitherOpposite signs; the greater magnitude sets the sign of the sum.

For x² − 9x + 20, the product is positive and sum negative: use −4 and −5. For x² + x − 20, use 5 and −4. The same magnitudes can create different trinomials because the signs change the sum and product.

Worked example

Factor a trinomial with opposite signs

Factor x² − 2x − 35.

  1. Use opposite signs because the product is negative.
    (−7)(5) = −35

    The larger magnitude must be negative to produce a negative middle coefficient.

  2. Check the signed sum.
    −7 + 5 = −2

    This matches the coefficient of x.

  3. Write and verify the answer.
    x2 − 2x − 35 = (x − 7)(x + 5)

    Expanding gives x² + 5x − 7x − 35, which simplifies to the original.

The leading coefficient changes the search

When the leading coefficient is not 1, the x-terms in the factors can have different coefficients. Expanding (2x + 3)(x + 4) gives 2x² + 11x + 12. The middle coefficient 11 comes from 8x + 3x, not from 3 + 4.

(2x + 3)(x + 4) = 2x2 + 8x + 3x + 12

You may use an area table, systematic inspection of possible factors, or decomposition. The decomposition method asks for two integers whose product is a × c and whose sum is b. Then group the four terms. Do not put the two numbers straight into (x + p)(x + q); the leading coefficient still matters.

Worked example

Decompose, group, and remove a binomial factor

Factor 6x² + 13x + 6.

  1. Find the required product and sum.
    a · c = 6 · 6 = 36; b = 13

    The pair 9 and 4 has product 36 and sum 13.

  2. Replace the middle term by an equivalent sum.
    6x2 + 9x + 4x + 6

    The expression has not changed: 9x + 4x = 13x.

  3. Take a common factor from each group.
    3x(2x + 3) + 2(2x + 3)

    Both groups now contain exactly the same binomial.

  4. Factor out that whole binomial.
    (3x + 2)(2x + 3)

    Expanding gives 6x² + 9x + 4x + 6, as required.

What the two middle products must do

For (ux + v)(wx + z), the middle terms are uzx and vwx. Their coefficients add to b. Their product is (uz)(vw) = (uw)(vz) = ac. That is why decomposition uses ac rather than c alone.

Distribution works beyond two binomials

Multiplying two binomials produces four partial products. That is only one case of the distributive property. A binomial times a trinomial produces six partial products; two trinomials produce nine.

Use a table or write one distribution line for each term. A reliable method helps you account for every product before combining like terms. “FOIL” describes a two-by-two arrangement, but it is not a complete method for every polynomial product.

Worked example

Multiply a binomial by a trinomial

Expand and simplify (x + 2)(x² + 3x − 4).

  1. Distribute x and 2 separately.

    Every term in the second factor is multiplied twice: once by x and once by 2.

  2. Write all six products.

Multiply with two variables

Expand (2x + 3y)(x − 4y).

Worked example

Apply a polynomial product to volume

A rectangular prism has base area x² + 2x + 3 and height x + 1. Write a simplified volume expression.

  1. Use the geometric relationship.
    V = (x2 + 2x + 3)(x + 1)

    Volume is base area times height, so the whole area expression is multiplied by the whole height expression.

  2. Distribute all terms.
    V = x3 + 2x2 + 3x + x2 + 2x + 3

Worked example

Factor a difference of squares

Factor 49x² − 36y².

  1. Check both square terms and the subtraction.
    49x2 = (7x)2; 36y2 = (6y)2

    There are two terms, and the operation is subtraction.

  2. Use opposite signs.
    49x2 − 36y2 = (7x − 6y)(7x + 6y)

    The mixed products −42xy and +42xy cancel.

  3. Verify the cancellation.
    49x2 + 42xy − 42xy − 36y2

    There is no middle term left. This is what creates the difference-of-squares pattern.

Worked example

Continue when a factor is still factorable

Factor 3x⁴ − 48 completely over the integers.

  1. Take out the GCF first.
    3x4 − 48 = 3(x4 − 16)

    The remaining binomial is a difference of squares.

  2. Factor the difference.
    3(x2 − 4)(x2 + 4)

    This uses x⁴ = (x²)² and 16 = 4².

  3. Check each factor again.
    3(x − 2)(x + 2)(x2 + 4)

    x² − 4 factors again. x² + 4 is a sum of squares and does not factor further over the integers.

Use a consistent factoring order

First: remove a numerical and variable GCF. Next: count terms and look for a known pattern. Then: use a signed pair or decomposition if the trinomial is not a special square. Finally: inspect each factor and expand to check the whole answer.

For two-variable trinomials, the same decomposition reasoning can apply. For example, 2x² − 5xy + 2y² = (2x − y)(x − 2y). The middle products −4xy and −xy add to −5xy. A variable in the constant position does not remove the need to check both products.

Do not apply (a − b)(a + b) to a² + b². The product creates subtraction, so it would change the expression.
