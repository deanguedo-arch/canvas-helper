The phase case

Jordan returns to the next free throw

Jordan is a fictional Grade 10 basketball player. During open play, Jordan needs to notice the ball, defenders and available teammates. At the free-throw line, the task changes. Jordan has practised a brief setup: find the line, release extra shoulder tension, look at the chosen rim target and use the cue “smooth.”

At the last school practice, Jordan missed a shot after a rushed setup. Jordan then thought, “I always fail when people watch,” looked toward a spectator and missed the teammate's signal for the next drill. The coach observed the rushed setup and offered a chance to practise it again. One missed cue does not tell us its only cause. In this case, Jordan's own account supplies additional evidence about attention.

The next practice includes a planned interruption: the drill order changes before Jordan's turn. You will help Jordan prepare, notice the new demand and select a useful next cue. Treat the case as fictional. An academic parallel is preparing to explain a graph after a classroom presentation order changes. An artistic parallel is returning to an agreed musical entry after an interruption. You may use one of these settings throughout your work.

02 · What attention does

Choose useful information, stay with it, and notice when the task changes.

Learning goal: I can explain selection, sustaining attention and shifting using a specific task.

Before you begin: Picture a performer who has more information available than they can use at once.

Attention gives the task a place to start

A school gym contains voices, moving people, floor markings and a scoreboard. A player cannot treat every detail as equally important. Attention concerns how information is selected and used. Concentration means keeping attention with what matters for the task. It does not mean blocking out everything around you.

Four useful actions are selecting, sustaining, noticing and shifting. Selecting means choosing information. Sustaining means staying with it long enough to do the job. Noticing means detecting a relevant change. Shifting means moving attention when another cue becomes more useful. These actions often work together rather than appearing as four separate stages.

Select according to the demand

Ask, “What decision or action is needed now?” A player deciding where to pass needs different information from a player taking a free throw. A presenter explaining a graph needs to attend to its key relationship. Reading every word on a slide may compete with that job.

Useful information is task-relevant: it helps the current action. A distraction pulls attention toward something less useful at that moment. The same sound can serve either role. A musician may need a partner's entry cue, while an unrelated conversation adds nothing to the next phrase. Relevance depends on the task, not just whether the information is inside or outside the performer.

Capacity is limited

Trying to hold many instructions at once can make the next action unclear. “Watch everyone, remember every correction and never make a mistake” gives attention several competing jobs. A shorter cue can help organize a familiar action. It cannot supply a skill the person has not learned.

Limited capacity does not mean a person is unable to learn. It means the plan should decide what deserves attention now. During learning, there may be time to examine a movement carefully. During a familiar performance, one well-understood cue may be more usable than a list of corrections.

Notice before explaining a miss

A missed cue is an observation. Its cause is a further question. The cue might have been unclear, unexpected or hidden. The performer might have been attending elsewhere. Ask what was noticed, what the person was trying to do and whether the instructions changed.

A useful plan does not demand perfect concentration. It identifies the relevant information, provides a way to notice drift and names a return point. The next topics give more precise language for that plan.

Worked example · fictional case

One room, two different jobs

Mina is preparing a short class presentation. Before speaking, Mina checks the graph's main relationship. During the explanation, Mina notices a classmate's question. Looking at the graph was useful first; listening becomes useful next.

A phone notification is less relevant to either task. The plan is to silence it during preparation, then use “main relationship” as the opening cue. This supports selection and sustaining attention. It does not mean Mina must ignore a relevant question to prove concentration.

03 · Four ways to focus

Describe attention by its width and its direction.

Learning goal: I can distinguish the four attention quadrants in a clearly stated moment.

Before you begin: Separate how much information is used from where that information comes from.

Two questions form four quadrants

The attention framework associated with Robert Nideffer combines two dimensions. Width describes whether attention is broad or narrow. Direction describes whether it is internal or external. Broad attention takes in several pieces of information. Narrow attention selects a specific cue. Internal attention concerns thoughts or bodily sensations. External attention concerns information in the surroundings.

Combine the dimensions to name four quadrants: broad-external, broad-internal, narrow-internal and narrow-external. These labels describe the focus used in a moment. They do not divide students into permanent kinds of people. A useful performer can change focus when the demand changes.

Broad-external: scan the surroundings

A basketball player scans the ball, defenders and available teammates before a pass. Several external cues matter together. That is a broad-external example. Broad does not mean noticing everything equally. It means gathering enough relevant information to read the play.

A recreational hiker checking the route, group position and nearby conditions offers another example. The situation determines which details belong in the scan. Looking around without a purpose may simply add distractions.

Broad-internal: consider information and options

During a pause, a player considers the coach's plan, the available options and an earlier pattern in the game. Those thoughts are internal, and several pieces are being considered. This is broad-internal attention.

A student organizing the argument for a presentation also uses several internal ideas. Analysis can be useful when there is time for it. The question is whether the current moment calls for planning or for carrying out the next prepared action.

Narrow-internal: one thought or sensation

During a safe pause, a performer briefly checks whether their shoulders are unnecessarily tense. The focus is internal and specific. Recalling one short cue can also be narrow-internal. This is different from repeatedly scanning every possible bodily sensation.

An internal check should have a clear purpose and an ending. After the check, the performer returns to information needed for the task. A long search for a perfect feeling may compete with preparation.

Narrow-external: one useful outside cue

A free-throw shooter looks at a chosen part of the rim. A musician watches the conductor's entry signal. Each selects a specific external cue. Narrow attention is useful here because the next action has a precise target.

No quadrant is always best. A narrow target that helps a free throw may hide useful information during open play. Judge the focus against the task rather than calling broad, narrow, internal or external attention automatically good or bad.

Worked example · fictional case

Describe the moment, not the whole athlete

Jordan is waiting at the free-throw line. During a safe pause, Jordan briefly notices extra shoulder tension. This is narrow-internal attention: one bodily cue. Jordan then looks at the chosen rim target, which is narrow-external.

The framework describes two moments in the same routine. It does not prove that Jordan belongs to an “internal type.” Once play resumes, a wider scan may be needed. The explanation must include the task and timing of each focus.

Broad internal attention considers several thoughts or options. Broad external attention scans the environment. Narrow internal attention notices one thought or sensation. Narrow external attention focuses on one external cue. The task determines which focus is useful; no quadrant is permanently ideal.

04 · Shift as the task changes

Use a change in the task as a signal to change attention.

Learning goal: I can explain a sequence of attention shifts and the trigger for each change.

Before you begin: Bring forward the four quadrants; no single quadrant is the permanent target.

A useful focus can become an old focus

During open play, Jordan scans defenders and teammates. A whistle stops the play. The coach gives a short instruction. Later, Jordan takes a free throw. Holding exactly the same focus across these moments would not meet all three demands.

An attention shift is a change in the information selected. It may change width, direction or both. A plan is stronger when it names what triggers the shift. “Focus better” is vague. “When the whistle stops play, listen for the instruction” links an event to a useful response.

Scan, plan, check and target

One teaching sequence begins with a broad-external scan of the situation. During a suitable pause, broad-internal attention may help the performer consider options. A brief narrow-internal check may identify an unnecessary tension signal. Narrow-external attention can then select the target for a precise next action.

This sequence is an example, not a ritual everyone must complete. Some tasks need fewer shifts. Others require repeated scanning during action. The performer should not add an internal check if it distracts from information needed for safety or execution. The task decides the sequence.

Give every transition a reason

For each moment, name the demand, the information and the trigger. A passing decision calls for information about space and teammates. A planned break permits discussion. An aiming attempt calls for the established target. The transition matters because the information needs have changed.

A musician may listen broadly to the ensemble, consider an adjustment during a pause, briefly recall the agreed count and then watch a specific entry cue. A presenter may scan the audience for a question, think through a response, choose the relevant example and point to one part of a graph. Neither situation needs a sport setting to use the framework.

Avoid two common traps

The first trap is staying with the last mistake. The result has happened, but attention remains on a judgment instead of the next demand. A brief factual review can be useful later. During the next action, use a cue that belongs to that action.

The second trap is changing focus without a reason. Constantly checking body sensations, the score and other people's reactions can create competing jobs. A prepared transition can reduce unnecessary searching, while remaining flexible when new information matters.

Check whether the shift served the task

Record what the performer intended to notice and what they actually noticed. Did the cue make the next action clearer? Was important information missed? One result does not prove that the shift caused success or failure. Use the observation to decide what to keep, clarify or practise again.

Worked example · fictional case

The whistle changes the job

Jordan scans the ball and available teammates during play. When the whistle sounds, the task becomes listening to the official rather than continuing the pass. At the free-throw line, Jordan uses the familiar setup and chosen target.

A useful plan names those transitions. It does not instruct Jordan to repeat all four quadrants while the ball is live. Attention shifts because the task changes, not because completing a four-box sequence earns a better result.

Broad internal attention considers several thoughts or options. Broad external attention scans the environment. Narrow internal attention notices one thought or sensation. Narrow external attention focuses on one external cue. The task determines which focus is useful; no quadrant is permanently ideal.

05 · Observe without judging yourself

Replace a judgment about the whole person with information about an attempt.

Learning goal: I can use an attributed coaching framework without treating it as a measured equation.

Before you begin: Recall the difference between an observed action and a claim about identity.

A mistake and its interpretation are different

“The ball landed short” describes a result. “I am useless” judges the whole person. The second statement gives little direction for the next attempt. It may also compete with information the performer needs. A useful response starts with what happened and what can be examined.

Nonjudgmental observation means describing a relevant feature without turning it into a verdict on personal worth. It does not mean pretending the attempt was correct. A performer can acknowledge a missed shot and ask for feedback without calling themselves a failure.

Gallwey's Inner Game framework

Timothy Gallwey uses the coaching framework performance = potential − interference. Here, interference means influences that get in the way of using a learned skill. Excessive self-criticism or trying to control every small movement may be examples in a particular case.

Treat the equation as an organizing idea, not arithmetic. This lesson does not measure a student's potential or subtract a numerical amount of interference. Many other features of a task matter, including instruction, preparation, skill and conditions.

The framework uses Self 1 for the telling, judging voice and Self 2 for the performer using learned capabilities. These are model labels. They are not two brain structures or two separate people inside a learner. Their purpose is to help discuss how commentary can compete with execution.

Analysis still has a place

A beginner may need a demonstration and careful attention to a movement. A performer may need to analyse an error at a sensible break. “Trust the skill” cannot replace learning a missing skill or listening to accurate feedback.

The distinction concerns timing and purpose. During practice, a coach can explain one correction. During a familiar attempt, the performer may use one understood cue rather than rehearse a long list of technical instructions. Afterwards, the action can be reviewed again.

Move from judgment to evidence

Use three parts: observation, possible explanation and next action. Keep the explanation open when the evidence is limited. “My setup was shorter than usual; I may have rushed; I will use the agreed setup cue next time” is more useful than “I always choke.”

A school example works the same way. “I left out the evidence for my claim” identifies something to revise. “I am bad at every subject” does not. A realistic observation can support a clear correction while preserving the fact that the work needs improvement.

The aim is not to remove every unhelpful thought. Notice when a thought pulls attention away, then return to a task-relevant action. Review the pattern without grading yourself on whether your mind felt perfectly quiet.

Worked example · fictional case

Keep the error; change the conclusion

After the missed free throw, Jordan says, “I always fail when people watch.” The coach observed a rushed setup. A stronger statement is, “I shortened my setup on that attempt. I will use the familiar preparation cue before the next shot.”

This does not promise the next shot will score. It identifies an action Jordan can try and review. If the setup remains unclear, instruction and practice are needed. A positive phrase alone would not fix that gap.

Gallwey’s performance equation is a coaching framework, not a measured subtraction. Self 1 and Self 2 are names within that framework, not parts of the brain. Specific observation and a useful cue can guide a familiar task while instruction remains important during learning.

06 · Cue words and anchors

Give attention a short, meaningful place to return.

Learning goal: I can select a cue and anchor that fit a particular task.

Before you begin: A cue must connect to an action the performer understands and has practised.

A cue is a reminder, not a complete lesson

A cue word is a short word or phrase used to direct attention or support action. It works best when the performer already understands its meaning. “Smooth” may remind Jordan of a practised release. For someone who has not learned that action, the word may be too vague.

A useful cue is short enough to use in the available moment. “Change your feet, shoulders, hands and timing” gives several competing instructions. During learning, those features may need separate explanation. During a familiar attempt, select the cue most relevant to the identified task.

Instructional and motivational purposes

An instructional cue directs technique, timing or relevant information. “See the target,” “steady count” and “main evidence” can serve that purpose in different settings. The phrase should tell the performer something useful about what to do.

A motivational cue supports effort or commitment. “Keep going” or “commit to this phrase” can serve that purpose. It should not pressure someone to ignore a real need for support. A more intense phrase is not automatically a better one.

Classify a cue by the job it does, not only its wording. “Finish” could remind a musician to sustain a final note or encourage a student to complete a familiar task. The explanation of its purpose makes the distinction clear.

An anchor gives attention a return point

An anchor is a chosen cue, sensation or action that helps the performer return to the task. A rim target, an agreed musical count or a key word on a speaking card can be an anchor. The performer should know when to use it and what comes next.

An anchor is not a lucky object that guarantees a result. It is also not necessarily a body check. Sometimes the best return point is external information. Choose the anchor from the task rather than adding a complicated routine because it sounds professional.

Plan the return

Use the sentence, “When I notice ___, I will return to ___, then ___.” The first part identifies a signal. The second selects the anchor. The third connects attention to action. For example, “When I start replaying the last error, I will look at the agreed entry mark, count in and begin the next phrase.”

The plan must allow flexibility. A usual visual anchor may not be available in a different room. Identify an alternative that serves the same purpose. Test the cue during appropriate practice and review whether it made the action clearer. Do not claim success merely because the phrase was repeated.

Worked example · fictional case

Two cues, one selection

Jordan considers “commit” and “see the rim target.” The first supports effort. The second directs attention to specific external information. Jordan's stated problem is looking toward a spectator before shooting, so the target cue is a better first match for this case.

The anchor is the chosen part of the rim, not the word alone. After finding it, Jordan follows the practised setup. If Jordan has not learned the setup, the plan also needs instruction; repeating the cue is not enough.

07 · Prepare your attention

Build a short sequence that connects preparation to the first action.

Learning goal: I can explain the purpose of each step in a before-performance routine.

Before you begin: Use an existing skill and a realistic amount of preparation time.

A routine organizes attention

A preperformance routine is a planned sequence used before an action. It can include checking the task, choosing a cue and preparing to begin. Its purpose is to make the next action clearer. Completing a routine does not guarantee a successful outcome or require every nervous feeling to disappear.

Keep the routine suited to the setting. A free throw provides a different pause from a moving team play. A classroom presentation may allow a short check before speaking. A musical entry may need immediate attention to a conductor. A routine that takes too long can interfere with the task it is meant to support.

Begin with information and preparation

First identify what the performer must know. Where is the action taking place? What is the starting cue? Is a rule or instruction unclear? Clarifying a task can be more useful than repeating a motivational phrase over missing information.

Preparation also includes skills and materials. A presenter checks that the needed example is ready. A player knows the agreed setup. A musician has practised the entry. Mental preparation works with those resources; it does not replace them.

Give each step one job

A simple routine might have three parts: prepare, direct and begin. During preparation, the performer checks the task and uses any familiar, comfortable settling action that is useful. During direction, they choose the relevant cue. During beginning, they commit to the next practised action.

The names matter less than the purpose. Explain what each step directs attention toward. “Prepare” is too broad without a detail. “Check the starting position and listen for the official's signal” is a task-based instruction.

Use a complete fictional example

For a school presentation, the routine could be: check the graph and speaking order; recall the cue “main relationship”; look toward the audience and begin the practised opening. The student may still feel uncertain. The sequence provides an action without demanding a perfect feeling first.

For sport, a brief body check belongs only at a suitable pause. Do not add breathing drills during swimming, active play or another task needing full attention. Describing a routine in writing is an acceptable way to demonstrate its purpose in this preview.

Practise and revise the routine

A routine becomes more usable when its steps are understood and rehearsed. Try a hypothetical walkthrough or an appropriately supervised practice. Observe whether the sequence fits the available time and whether the final cue belongs to the task.

Record the routine separately from the result. One successful attempt does not prove that the routine caused success. One mistake does not automatically make the routine useless. Use specific observations to choose a sensible adjustment.

Worked example · fictional case

Prepare, direct, begin

Jordan's routine is: check the line and starting position; briefly release unnecessary shoulder tension during the pause; find the rim target, use “smooth” and shoot. Each step has a purpose. The final attention is on the target and familiar action, not on continually checking feelings.

A teammate uses a different routine. That is not a problem if it fits their task. The assignment asks Jordan to explain and adapt the chosen sequence, not prove there is one correct ritual for everybody.

A preparation routine checks the situation, uses a familiar sequence, directs attention to the next information and includes an alternative for a likely disruption. It is a plan to use and adjust, not a promise to eliminate uncertainty.

08 · Notice, reset and return

Use a disruption as a signal for a practical next response.

Learning goal: I can write an adaptable if–then plan that returns attention to useful information.

Before you begin: A reset ends with the task; it is not an endless check of feelings.

Disruption is part of a changing task

A performer may hear an unexpected sound, miss an entry or receive a new instruction. A reset is a brief response that helps them return to useful information. It does not erase the event. It creates a bridge from what happened to what the next action needs.

Start with a specific signal. “I am losing focus” is broad. “I keep replaying the last pass and have stopped checking teammates” identifies the pattern more clearly. A useful signal can be noticed without judging the whole person.

Use an if–then plan

An if–then plan links a likely event to a response. “If the speaking order changes, then I will confirm when I begin and use my opening cue” is practical. It names both a trigger and an action.

Avoid trying to predict every possible problem. Choose a likely disruption that fits the case. Explain what the performer will do and what information they will attend to next. The response should be brief enough for the setting and should not create a new safety problem.

Notice, adjust, return

One useful sequence is: notice the signal; make a proportionate adjustment during a suitable pause; return to the next task cue. A familiar comfortable breath, release of unnecessary tension or brief cue may be part of the adjustment. None is compulsory for every performer.

The goal is not to force calmness. Do not hold or force the breath, and stop a physical practice if uncomfortable. A written walkthrough is always available. A performer in active play may need to return directly to the relevant external information rather than begin an internal exercise.

When the first response does not fit

A plan should include a check. Did the instruction become clearer? Can the performer identify the next cue? If not, another response may be needed. An unclear role may require a question. An unsafe demand requires a change or appropriate support, not more self-talk.

A reset also differs from denying an error. Useful feedback can be reviewed at a suitable break. The performer does not need to rehearse a whole technical analysis while the next attempt is starting. Separate immediate refocusing from later learning.

Apply the sequence beyond sport

A musician who loses a count may listen for the next agreed entry cue. A student interrupted during a presentation may pause, identify the last completed point and return to the main claim. A recreational participant may ask for the instruction again. The wording changes, but the structure remains connected to the demand.

Judge the plan by its fit, clarity and flexibility. A successful reset is not defined by the learner claiming that every distracting thought disappeared.

Worked example · fictional case

The drill order changes

The coach changes the drill order before Jordan's turn. Jordan is unsure whether to shoot or join the passing line. The appropriate first move is to clarify the new instruction. A breathing cue would not supply that missing information.

After clarification, Jordan selects the next task cue and begins. The if–then plan is: “If the drill order changes and my role is unclear, then I will ask where to begin, identify the next cue and rejoin the task.”

Notice a specific signal, make an appropriate adjustment, return attention to a task cue, then act and review. The response should fit the situation. A familiar comfortable pause is an option, not an instant off-switch for thoughts or feelings.

09 · Practise a flexible focus plan

Use evidence and one changed condition to refine a plan.

Learning goal: I can propose a suitable variation and explain what evidence would guide an adjustment.

Before you begin: Keep practice hypothetical or appropriately supervised; it is not a punishment.

A plan should travel beyond one familiar setting

A focus plan may work in a quiet practice and need adjusting in another setting. The useful question is not, “Can I force the same feeling everywhere?” It is, “Which information and actions still fit, and which need to change?”

Flexible focus means adapting attention to the task while keeping a clear purpose. It does not mean changing every cue after one mistake. Use evidence to decide which part of the plan needs attention.

Change one condition deliberately

A hypothetical practice can vary the order of turns, the location of a visual cue or the amount of preparation time. Choose a change related to a likely performance demand. State what remains the same so the comparison is understandable.

For actual practice, use appropriate teacher or coach guidance. The variation should not involve humiliation, unsafe distraction or exhaustion as a test of character. You can demonstrate the reasoning through a written case or walkthrough without creating real pressure.

Observe the action before interpreting it

Record what the performer intended to notice and what they actually did. For example: “Jordan looked toward the spectator after the missed shot and did not see the signal.” That observation is more useful than “Jordan has bad focus.”

Then consider possible explanations. Was the signal clear? Had the drill changed? Was Jordan replaying the error? A scenario may provide evidence for one explanation, but a single missed cue does not establish every cause. A strong response identifies both the evidence and its limits.

Decide what to keep and what to adjust

Keep parts of the routine that still serve the task. Adjust the part affected by the changed condition. If the usual visual anchor is hidden, choose another relevant anchor. If the next action is unclear, clarify it rather than simply using the same cue more intensely.

A useful review includes a reason. “I would change the anchor because the usual marker is no longer visible” links the adjustment to evidence. “I would change everything because the attempt missed” makes a much larger claim than the result supports.

Bring the phase together

Your plan should connect the demand, relevant information, attention shifts, cues and a disruption response. It should also distinguish learning-time analysis from a short cue during a familiar attempt. Preparation and feedback remain important even when the performer uses a refocus routine.

In Build, you will use one case to make that connection. In the unfamiliar transfer task, you will keep the useful principles while changing the details. The aim is an explanation that can adapt—not a memorized phrase that only fits one exact question.

Worked example · fictional case

Keep the purpose; change the anchor

A presenter normally uses a highlighted word on a slide as an anchor. In a different room, the display is unavailable. The presenter can use the same key word on a speaking card, then explain the main relationship aloud.

The purpose remains directing attention to the main idea. The representation changes because the usual cue is unavailable. A useful observation is whether the speaker can resume the explanation, not whether they claim to feel exactly as they did in the original room.

A flexible focus plan connects the task, the width and direction of attention, a practised cue and evidence for adjustment. Observing a miss alone does not identify one unique attention problem.

Glossary and sources

Attention
Selecting and using information for a task.
Concentration
Sustaining attention with information that matters.
Task-relevant
Useful for the action or decision currently needed.
Distraction
Information that competes with the current task.
Broad attention
Considering several relevant pieces of information.
Narrow attention
Selecting a specific cue.
Internal attention
Attention to thoughts or bodily sensations.
External attention
Attention to information in the surroundings.
Attention shift
A change in the information selected when the demand changes.
Nonjudgmental observation
A factual description without a verdict on personal worth.
Inner Game
Gallwey’s attributed coaching framework for discussing learned execution and interference.
Self 1 / Self 2
Model labels for commentary and learned execution, not brain structures.
Instructional cue
A brief reminder directing technique, timing or information.
Motivational cue
A brief reminder supporting effort or commitment.
Anchor
A chosen return point for attention.
Preperformance routine
A planned sequence connecting preparation to the next action.
If–then plan
A stated disruption linked to a practical response.
Reset
A proportionate response ending with a useful task cue.
Practice variation
A planned, appropriate change in a practice condition.
Transfer
Adapting an idea to a different task or condition.
