How This Course Works

A repeatable learning process

  1. Read the brief and vocabulary. Identify the result you are working toward.
  2. Study the lessons and worked example. Revisit them when a decision becomes difficult.
  3. Try the practice decisions. Feedback explains the reasoning and you can retry.
  4. Create the required product in a document, presentation, drawing or recording. Use the response record to summarize decisions and identify your files.
  5. Check the rubric, revise, then choose Save to Portfolio. Print or download a backup when needed.

Plan your time

Each CTS code represents approximately 25 hours of access to instruction and practical learning. A suggested pattern is 6 hours of lessons and source investigation, 4 hours of examples and practice, 9 hours developing evidence, 4 hours testing and revising, and 2 hours evaluating and preparing submission. Adapt the pace with your teacher; there is no timer or automatic credit for hours.

Independent work and assessment

Use fictional customers and supplied data. You do not need to buy anything, travel, contact a business, recruit a partner or attend a live session. Written role responses and solo recordings let you practise applied skills. Some official outcomes require participation or performance evidence that a simulation alone may not demonstrate. Your teacher must validate those requirements before the affected module is released or awarded.

Project connections must refer to successfully completed eligible CTS courses, including one at the project’s level and in the same occupational area. Advanced projects connect to another intermediate or advanced course. Project and practicum codes cannot supply the connections. Teacher confirmation is separate from this page’s progress indicators.

MAM3010 · The Business Organization
MAM3010 · Learning module · Advanced


Northside Cycle is expanding from one shop to two. Recommend an organizational structure, responsibilities and performance measures that preserve service quality.
Evidence boundary for teacher review
Solo role and handover exercises require validation for teamwork outcomes.
Learning focus: match structure to the work; lead, delegate and communicate; evaluate success beyond one number.


1. Match structure to the work2. Lead, delegate and communicate3. Evaluate success beyond one numberPracticeYour work


Words to know
Organizational structure
how roles, authority and communication are arranged.
Span of control
the number of people directly supervised by a manager.
Delegation
assigning work and appropriate authority while retaining accountability.
Stakeholder
a person or group affected by a business decision.
1. Match structure to the work
An organization coordinates people and resources to achieve a purpose. A sole owner may make most decisions in a very small business, but growth can make that approach a bottleneck. Functional structures group work by expertise, such as operations and marketing. Divisional structures group work by product, region or location. A matrix combines reporting relationships and can support collaboration, but may create conflicting instructions.


Choose a structure by examining the actual work. Two shops need local decisions about daily service and consistent decisions about purchasing, pricing and customer promises. An organizational chart shows reporting lines; it does not by itself explain who has authority over a delayed repair or disputed invoice. Add decision responsibilities to make the chart usable.
Next lesson
2. Lead, delegate and communicate
Planning defines goals; organizing allocates resources; leading supports people in doing the work; controlling compares results with expectations. Different leadership approaches suit different situations. A safety-critical action may require a clear directive, while improving a recurring process may benefit from staff input. Do not treat leadership as a personality label that excuses poor communication.


Delegation needs an outcome, authority, resources, deadline and check-in. Assigning responsibility without authority can make success impossible. A useful handover states the current situation, verified facts, pending decision, responsible role and next step. In this independent case, create the handover for fictional staff; explain the limits of simulating actual teamwork.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Evaluate success beyond one number
Revenue, profitability, service reliability, staff retention and customer outcomes describe different parts of organizational performance. A branch can increase sales while accumulating complaints or costly rework. Select a small set of measures connected to the stated goals, define how each is collected and explain what result would trigger action.


Businesses also make decisions about responsibility and ethics. Consider owners, workers, customers, suppliers and the community. A cheaper supplier may improve short-term margin but create reliability or labour concerns. Identify evidence, uncertainty and competing interests. A defensible decision shows who benefits, who bears a cost and how the organization will monitor consequences.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. The fictional business centralizes purchasing and brand standards while giving each branch supervisor authority over scheduling and routine service recovery.
  2. The operations manager monitors on-time completion, rework and gross margin. A branch supervisor receives a clear limit for authorizing a remedy and escalates cases beyond it.
  3. This structure reduces inconsistent policies without requiring the owner to approve every appointment. A monthly review checks whether centralized purchasing is causing local delays.

MAM3020 · Business in the Canadian Economy
MAM3020 · Learning module · Advanced


Assess how a fictional Alberta repair business should respond to changing demand, costs and borrowing conditions. Explain the difference between economic evidence and prediction.
Learning focus: connect household and business decisions; interpret economic indicators carefully; use scenarios rather than one confident forecast.


1. Connect household and business decisions2. Interpret economic indicators carefully3. Use scenarios rather than one confident forecastPracticeYour work


Words to know
Opportunity cost
the next-best alternative forgone when making a choice.
Inflation
a sustained increase in a broad measure of prices.
Productivity
output produced per unit of input.
Business cycle
fluctuations in economic activity over time.
1. Connect household and business decisions
Households supply labour and buy goods and services. Businesses purchase inputs, employ workers and sell outputs. Governments collect revenue, purchase services and establish rules. Financial institutions help connect saving and investment. A decision in one part of this system affects others: reduced household spending can lower business demand, while business hiring supports household income.


Scarcity means resources have alternative uses. A shop that spends its reserve on equipment cannot use the same reserve to absorb a weak month. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best option, not simply the purchase price. Distinguish needs, incentives and constraints before predicting how people will respond.
Next lesson
2. Interpret economic indicators carefully
Gross domestic product describes production; unemployment describes a labour-market condition; a consumer price index tracks a specified basket of prices. No single measure describes every business or household. National growth can coexist with weak demand in a local sector. Check geography, date, unit, population and method when using a chart.


Monetary policy influences financial conditions, including borrowing costs. Fiscal policy involves government spending and taxation. Their effects depend on circumstances and take time. This course uses fictional changes to practise reasoning; do not label them as current forecasts. When researching real conditions, consult dated Statistics Canada or Bank of Canada information and identify the observation period.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Use scenarios rather than one confident forecast
Build a baseline, a favourable scenario and an adverse scenario. State which assumptions change and which stay fixed. A simple contribution calculation can reveal whether the business can cover a cost, but it is not a complete profit statement. Include overhead, capacity and cash timing in the final interpretation.


Fictional baseline: 100 services at $40, with variable cost $15 each. Revenue is $4,000 and contribution before fixed costs is $2,500. If demand falls to 80 and variable cost rises to $18, contribution becomes 80 × ($40 − $18) = $1,760. Compare options such as reducing waste, adjusting service mix or postponing borrowing. Explain tradeoffs for customers and staff.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. Baseline contribution: 100 × ($40 − $15) = $2,500. Adverse contribution: 80 × ($40 − $18) = $1,760. The difference is $740 before fixed costs.
  2. The manager tests whether improved scheduling could reduce idle time without making an unsupported promise of higher demand.
  3. The recommendation names a trigger: postpone expansion if contribution remains below the stated threshold for two comparable periods. It acknowledges that the figures are a classroom scenario, not a forecast.

MAM3030 · Business in the Global Marketplace
MAM3030 · Learning module · Advanced


Compare two possible markets for a fictional Alberta cycling-accessory business. Recommend whether and how to expand, considering customers, currency, distribution and ethics.
Learning focus: investigate customers and the operating environment; calculate landed cost and currency exposure; compare entry strategies and responsibilities.


1. Investigate customers and the operating environment2. Calculate landed cost and currency exposure3. Compare entry strategies and responsibilitiesPracticeYour work


Words to know
Exchange rate
the price of one currency in terms of another.
Tariff
a tax applied to specified traded goods.
Comparative advantage
producing at a lower opportunity cost relative to another producer.
Market entry
the method used to begin operating in a new market.
1. Investigate customers and the operating environment
International expansion is more than translating an advertisement. Investigate customer needs, competition, cultural context, distribution, regulation and payment practices. A product that succeeds locally may not suit another climate, transport pattern or price expectation. Avoid treating a country as a single uniform audience.


Compare two defined markets using the same criteria. Separate evidence from assumptions and identify what must be verified before a real decision. Trade agreements can affect access, but they do not eliminate every rule or cost. Official trade resources are a starting point for checking current requirements; this classroom task does not involve an actual shipment or transaction.
Next lesson
2. Calculate landed cost and currency exposure
A product's overseas cost can include manufacturing, freight, insurance, customs-related charges, storage and returns. State the currency for every amount. If one Canadian dollar buys 0.75 units of a fictional foreign currency, a foreign price of 30 converts to CAD 40 by dividing 30 by 0.75. Multiplying instead would answer a different question.


Use scenarios to examine currency changes rather than assuming the exchange rate remains constant. A business can earn more local revenue and still receive fewer Canadian dollars after conversion. Consider payment delays and supplier commitments. Show the calculation and describe the direction of the effect in words as a reasonableness check.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Compare entry strategies and responsibilities
Exporting through a distributor can reduce some setup demands but gives up control and margin. Selling directly may increase control while adding logistics and service responsibilities. Partnerships can provide local knowledge but require clear expectations. Compare options against the business's resources and the market's needs.


Global operations create responsibilities for working conditions, environmental claims, product quality and consumer communication. Identify stakeholders across the supply chain rather than considering only the selling business. Research the roles of trade-support organizations and international institutions without assuming that membership guarantees an ethical outcome. A defensible recommendation includes a reason not to expand if the evidence is insufficient.
Previous lessonGo to practice
Worked example
  1. Market A's fictional landed cost is CAD 18 per item; Market B's is CAD 23. Market B supports a higher price but has a longer return cycle.
  2. At a foreign selling price of 30 and a rate of 0.75 foreign units per CAD, revenue converts to CAD 40 before fees. At 0.80, the same foreign price converts to CAD 37.50.
  3. The recommendation favours a small distributor trial in Market A, with clear quality and labour expectations, rather than assuming the higher sticker price in Market B means a better return.

Prepare and independently demonstrate an ethical sales conversation for a fictional customer choosing cycling accessories. Show how the recommendation follows the customer's needs.

An independently performed sales demonstration is proposed evidence; interactive selling/teamwork outcomes require teacher validation.
Learning focus: discover before recommending; translate features into relevant benefits; close appropriately and review performance.


1. Discover before recommending2. Translate features into relevant benefits3. Close appropriately and review performancePracticeYour work


Words to know
Needs discovery
questions used to understand a customer's purpose and constraints.
Feature
a characteristic of a product or service.
Benefit
why a feature matters for a particular customer.
Objection
a concern that may prevent a customer from proceeding.
1. Discover before recommending
A sales conversation should establish whether an offer fits the customer. Begin with an appropriate introduction and ask open questions about use, priorities and constraints. Follow with focused questions to clarify details. Listening is not merely waiting to deliver a pitch: summarize what you understood and give the customer a chance to correct it.


Fictional customer: commutes after dark, has a $60 accessory budget and already owns a working lock. The record does not justify assuming the customer needs another lock or the most expensive product. Their stated need suggests investigating visibility and how the bicycle is used. Avoid pressure based on stereotypes or unsupported assumptions.
Next lesson
2. Translate features into relevant benefits
A feature has value only in context. A rechargeable light may reduce battery replacement, but a rider without convenient charging access may prefer another option. Present a small number of suitable alternatives and explain the tradeoffs. Demonstrate use accurately and identify limitations. A persuasive explanation can still recommend buying nothing if the current equipment already meets the need.


Objections may concern price, suitability, trust or timing. Clarify the concern before responding. An ethical response acknowledges a genuine limitation and offers a realistic alternative; it does not invent a guarantee. Distinguish helpful urgency based on a real constraint from pressure designed to prevent an informed choice.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Close appropriately and review performance
A close confirms the customer's decision and the agreed terms. Summarize the recommended product, price, relevant conditions and next step. If the customer declines, maintain a respectful relationship. Follow-up should support service and consent rather than repeatedly contacting someone who does not want it.


For independent practice, perform the salesperson's responses to a sequence of supplied customer statements. A solo recording with transcript or a clearly marked scripted performance can make reasoning reviewable. Keep the fictional customer's lines separate from your own. Self-evaluation should identify a missed question, a weak claim or an unclear explanation and demonstrate a revision.

MAM3060 · Setting Up a Retail Store
MAM3060 · Learning module · Advanced


Develop an original launch proposal for a small Alberta retail store using fictional site and supplier data. Show that the customer promise, floor plan, operations and costs fit together.
Learning focus: choose a concept and compare sites; design a workable space and supplier system; test feasibility and plan the launch.


1. Choose a concept and compare sites2. Design a workable space and supplier system3. Test feasibility and plan the launchPracticeYour work


Words to know
Location analysis
comparison of sites against the business's requirements.
Fixed cost
a cost that does not change directly with short-term sales volume.
Break-even
the point at which defined revenues equal defined costs.
Operating plan
the roles, procedures and resources needed to run the business.
1. Choose a concept and compare sites
Start with the customer and the problem the store will solve. Define the product range, service promise, price position and reason to choose the store. A concept should be specific enough to guide stock, layout and promotion. A store for commuter essentials will make different decisions from a specialist racing-equipment store.


Compare location options using access, visibility, nearby customers, space, deliveries, competition and cost. Fictional Site A costs $2,000 per month and has strong pedestrian access; Site B costs $1,500 but needs additional delivery arrangements. A lower rent does not automatically produce a lower total operating cost. Use an Alberta community or the supplied data; no site visit is required.
Next lesson
2. Design a workable space and supplier system
An original floor plan should show entrances, circulation, merchandise zones, service point, storage and emergency/access considerations. Describe dimensions and assumptions rather than claiming compliance with a building code you have not checked. Good layout supports both customer decisions and staff tasks such as receiving deliveries and replenishing stock.


Compare suppliers on cost, quality, reliability, minimum order, payment terms and return arrangements. A low unit price can be offset by large minimum orders or unreliable delivery. Plan receiving, storage, housekeeping and inventory controls. Identify which decisions require a qualified professional or current official guidance before a real store could open.
Previous lessonNext lesson
3. Test feasibility and plan the launch
A startup budget includes one-time setup and ongoing operating costs. Distinguish revenue, contribution and cash.
