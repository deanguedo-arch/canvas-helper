Novel Study - Unit Introduction

"The novel is a great discovery; far greater than Galileo's telescope or somebody else's wireless. The novel is the highest form of expression so far attained. Why? Because it is so incapable of the absolute"  ~D.H. Lawrence

You were given a choice of novels at the beginning of the course and should have chosen one of the following for your research in a previous assignment:

  • Into Thin Air by Jon Krakauer (non fiction)
  • 1984 by George Orwell (fiction)
  • Annabel by Kathleen Winter (fiction)
  • The Cellist of Sarajevo by Steven Galloway (fiction)
  • The Kiterunner (Khaled Hosseini)
  • A Thousand Splendid Suns (Khaled Hosseini)
  • The Glass Castle (Jeanette Walls)

Overview

The assignments in this section of the course are a culmination of the work you have done over the past few months. As such, you need to commit more time and effort to each of the assignments as the expectations are higher.

This section involves:

  • responding to a book review based on the novel you chose to study for this course
  • writing a Critical/Analytical Essay 

Throughout this section of the course, students should:

  • become actively involved with literature, using effective strategies for appreciation, understanding and critical response
  • form generalizations by integrating new information with prior knowledge
  • understand the role, variety and usefulness of literary criticism to literary study
  • seek alternative viewpoints using information technologies
  • critically assess information accessed through the use of a variety of technologies
  • improve thoughtfulness, effectiveness and correctness of communication

Lesson 2

Characteristics of a Novel

Review the major features of novels: plot, character, point of view, setting, conflict, and theme.

How can we identify a novel? Here are some characteristics of a novel:

  • A novel may contain many of the elements of fiction that we have considered when studying the short story. However, a novel is usually longer and contains more characters than a short story. A novel may cover a longer period of time than a story.
  • A novel can begin anywhere in time (the past, present, or future.)
  • The conflict of a novel can exist on many different levels.

Lesson 3

How to Read a Novel

Use active reading routines to notice patterns, questions, character change, and important passages.
  • If possible, research some biographical background on the author. Remember that writers sometimes use fiction as a tool to explore and reflect on issues in their own lives, so knowing something about the author may help extend your understanding of the themes of a novel.
  • As soon as possible, try to determine both the time and place setting of the story. You may need to do further research if the novel is set in either a time or a place with which you are unfamiliar.
  • Be aware of the central conflicts that evolve as the plotdevelops. Remember that the internal conflicts of characters may be far more significant than obvious external conflicts in helping you discover the underlying themes of the novel.
  • Determine the point of view being used to relate the plot. Then carefully consider why you feel the author chose that particular point of view to relate the story. How does the chosen point of view slant or bias what the 'truth' is? Does the point of view shift?
  • Determine who the protagonist (central character) is. Remember that the protagonist may be more than one person. Then determine what the protagonist is trying to achieve, or, in other words, what his or her goal is. Then consider what forces, including other characters, are hindering the protagonist from achieving that goal. Considering the nature of the protagonist(s) and that of the antagonist(s) and reflecting on the types of conflicts that develop provides important clues to help a reader make meaning of a novel.
  • Be aware of any changes that occur in the essential nature of any of the major characters as the plot develops. If changes do occur, reflect why a character is motivated to undergo such a change. If, on the other hand, the essential nature of a central character never changes, consider how such a static nature determines that character's fate.
  • Identify images that recur throughout the novel. Such recurring images are referred to as symbols and are often important keys to discovering the novel's themes.
  • Watch for other literary devices, particularly the use of foreshadowing (hints of what is to come) or irony. These, too, can be helpful clues to lead the reader to a more insightful understanding of the novel.
  • Be sensitive to the general tone and mood of the story, and to changes in the tone and mood. Consider the author's style of writing and how that style reinforces the mood and general tone that the author is seeking to create.
  • The title of a novel is usually carefully chosen. Speculate on the significance of the title the author chose for the novel.
  • Most good novels deserve more than a single read. Re-reading a book usually allows readers to deepen and expand their understanding of the story and its underlying themes.
  • Try to find someone else who has also studied the novel you are studying. Sharing various ideas, reactions and interpretations about a novel is an important way of expanding your perspectives.
  • Reading critical reviews and watching movie adaptations of a novel may offer new perspectives and insights. However, challenge yourself to read and interpret the novel before you consider how others have interpreted the book. Then you will be better able to critique how effectively you feel a director has adapted a novel to a movie or how perceptively a critic has reviewed a novel.

Novel Study Questions

Complete these formative questions after each third of your selected novel. Use specific evidence from the text whenever possible.

ELA 20-1 Formative Reading

Section 1 Questions

Answer after reading the first third of your novel.
Formative Progress0 of 9 answered
1. How does the novel begin?
Teacher Hint: Describe the opening situation, mood, setting, and any first conflict or question the author creates.
2. Who are the major characters and what are their relationships to each other?
Teacher Hint: Name the central characters and explain how they are connected, not just who they are.
3. What is the initial conflict?
Teacher Hint: Look for the problem, pressure, or tension that starts moving the story forward.
4. What method does the author use to create suspense?
Teacher Hint: Consider unanswered questions, danger, pacing, foreshadowing, withheld information, or unstable relationships.
5. What is the point of view? Who is telling the story?
Teacher Hint: Identify the narrator and explain how that perspective shapes what the reader knows or feels.
6. What minor characters are introduced in the novel?
Teacher Hint: Include characters who may not lead the plot but still affect conflict, setting, or the protagonist.
7. Are there any characters you particularly like or dislike? Explain.
Teacher Hint: Support your reaction with a specific action, decision, line of dialogue, or relationship.
8. What themes do you see being developed?
Teacher Hint: Move beyond one-word topics. Explain what the novel may be suggesting about people, choices, or society.
9. Make a prediction as to what you believe will happen in the next section of the book.
Teacher Hint: Base the prediction on evidence from conflict, character goals, or foreshadowing.

ELA 20-1 Formative Reading

Section 2 Questions

Answer after reading the middle third of your novel.
Formative Progress0 of 9 answered
1. Has the conflict changed? Explain.
Teacher Hint: Describe whether the original conflict has intensified, shifted, or revealed a deeper problem.
2. Are all the characters facing the same problem?
Teacher Hint: Compare at least two characters and explain how their pressures are similar or different.
3. Discuss one important decision the protagonist has made.
Teacher Hint: Name the decision, explain the motivation, and describe the consequence or likely consequence.
4. Discuss the author's style: foreshadowing, flashbacks, changing point of view, and other choices.
Teacher Hint: Choose one or two techniques and explain their effect on the reader.
5. Discuss how the author is continuing to create suspense.
Teacher Hint: Look for complications, uncertainty, secrets, delayed answers, or rising stakes.
6. Has anything happened to surprise you? Explain.
Teacher Hint: Describe the moment and explain why it challenged your expectations.
7. How is the author developing the characters? Is it by telling us about them, by telling us what others say about them, or by telling us what the characters say about themselves?
Teacher Hint: Use one character as an example and connect the technique to what you learn about that character.
8. How accurate have your predictions been so far?
Teacher Hint: Compare your earlier prediction to what actually happened and explain what you learned about the author's direction.
9. Make a final prediction for the end of the book.
Teacher Hint: Use current conflict, character choices, and repeated patterns to make a defensible prediction.

ELA 20-1 Formative Reading

Section 3 Questions

Answer after finishing your novel.
Formative Progress0 of 6 answered
1. What overall influence did the setting have on the events of the novel and the characters?
Teacher Hint: Explain how time, place, social conditions, or atmosphere shaped choices and conflict.
2. Were the characters dynamic or static? Explain.
Teacher Hint: Identify who changed or stayed the same and support your answer with specific evidence.
3. Approximately how much time elapses in the book?
Teacher Hint: Estimate the story's timeline and mention any jumps, flashbacks, or compressed time.
4. What themes were developed? Which one do you feel was the most important one?
Teacher Hint: Choose the strongest theme and explain how the novel develops it from beginning to end.
5. What will you take away from your reading of the book?
Teacher Hint: Connect your takeaway to a character, conflict, theme, or repeated idea from the novel.
6. Make a criticism or recommendation for the novel.
Teacher Hint: Evaluate the novel thoughtfully by naming a strength, limitation, audience, or reason for your recommendation.
