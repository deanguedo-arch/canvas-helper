The phase case
Alex prepares for a school volleyball tryout
Alex is a fictional Grade 10 student. The school volleyball tryout is two weeks away. Alex enjoys
playing games with friends and values contributing to a group. Repeating a serving drill is not always
enjoyable, but Alex chooses it because becoming a reliable teammate matters. Alex also says,
“Sometimes I practise because I would feel guilty if I stopped.” Those are different reasons, even
when the practice looks the same.
Alex can attend two school practices this week. A late bus is available on Tuesday, but not Thursday.
There is also a science presentation to prepare. At the last practice, Alex completed the preparation
routine on six of ten planned serves. On four attempts, Alex rushed the setup after watching another
player score. The coach offered to demonstrate the setup again. A classmate offered to practise
together during a suitable school session.
For the assignment, treat these details as case evidence, not a report about a real student. You may
add reasonable fictional details and label them as assumptions. Later, the case changes: Thursday's
gym session is cancelled. Alex also reports that the current schedule leaves too little time to recover.
Your job is to adjust the plan, not diagnose Alex or prescribe harder training.

Describe Alex’s task and two practical constraints. What information would help you choose a
realistic next action?

02 · Where effort goes
Separate the amount of effort from the job that effort is doing.
Learning goal: I can distinguish direction and intensity using evidence from one situation.
Before you begin: Think of two people who spend the same amount of time practising
different things.
Start with the task
Two students each spend twenty minutes preparing for a presentation. One practises explaining the
main idea without reading. The other changes the title slide several times. Both are working. They
are not working on the same thing. Before judging effort, ask what each action is meant to improve.
Motivation concerns the reasons and processes that get action started and keep it going. This
phase begins with two useful dimensions: direction and    intensity. Direction means where effort is
aimed. Intensity means how much effort is applied. Time spent can give one clue, but time alone
does not tell you either the quality or the purpose of the work.
Direction: choose a useful target
A volleyball player may aim to improve a reliable serving setup. A musician may aim to enter on the
correct beat. A student may aim to explain evidence clearly. Each target gives practice a job.
“Improve everything” is too broad to guide the next few minutes.
Direction also involves choosing between tasks. Repeating an already comfortable skill can feel
productive. However, it might leave the main difficulty untouched. A useful question is, “Which part of
this performance needs attention now?” The answer should come from evidence, such as feedback
or a pattern across attempts.
Intensity: how much effort is applied?
Intensity describes the effort invested after a target is chosen. Two players may practise the same
skill, but one engages carefully while the other barely attempts it. That difference may concern
intensity. Do not decide why it happened from appearance alone. The second player might need
clearer instructions, a different level of challenge, or support.
More intense work is not automatically better work. Repeating a misunderstood action with greater
force does not supply the missing explanation. A useful plan joins an appropriate target with an
amount of effort that fits the task and setting.

Discipline is not a fixed label
In this course, discipline means organizing and returning to useful actions over time. It is not a
permanent label separating good people from bad people. Reasons, skills, time, feedback and the
environment all affect what a person can do.
When a plan slips, inspect it before judging the person. Was the task clear? Was the action realistic?
Was there a way to get feedback? A plan can need adjusting even when the performer cares about
the goal. Later topics explain reasons for effort and the conditions that support it.
Worked example · fictional case
Equal time, different practice
Alex and Sam each practise for twenty minutes. Alex repeats a familiar serve. Sam checks
the starting position, tries a correction and asks whether it changed the setup. Their practice
time is equal, but their direction differs. Sam is targeting a specific part of the skill.
We cannot conclude that Sam is a better person or is trying harder. The evidence tells us
what each session targets. A useful adjustment for Alex is to identify one feature to observe,
rather than simply adding more minutes.

03 · Why we do the work
The same activity can be done for very different reasons.
Learning goal: I can distinguish reasons for action without assigning a permanent label to a
person.
Before you begin: Use the performer’s words, not their appearance, as the main evidence.
Look beneath the action
Three students attend the same rehearsal. One enjoys making music. One values contributing to the
concert. One fears getting in trouble for missing it. Attendance looks the same, but the reasons
differ.    Self-determination theory, associated with Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, helps us name
those differences.
Intrinsic motivation means doing an activity for its own interest or enjoyment. “I like playing this
piece” is an example. Extrinsic motivation means doing it for a separable result or purpose. That
purpose can be personally meaningful; extrinsic does not always mean someone is forcing the
person.
Four forms of extrinsic regulation
External regulation centres on an outside demand, reward or consequence. A learner might
practise to earn a reward or avoid punishment. The immediate reason is outside the activity itself.
Introjected regulation involves pressure that the person has taken inside. “I must practise or I am
worthless” ties action to guilt or protecting self-worth. The pressure feels internal, but the action may
still feel controlled rather than freely endorsed.
Identified regulation means accepting the importance of the action. “This drill helps a skill I value”
gives a personally accepted reason. The drill does not have to be enjoyable.

Integrated regulation means that the accepted reason fits with broader values and identity.
“Preparing reliably fits how I want to contribute as a teammate” is an example. Integrated regulation
remains extrinsic: the activity serves a valued purpose rather than being done only for enjoyment.
Lack of intention is a separate idea
Amotivation describes a lack of intention to act. A learner may not see a purpose, may not believe
the action will help, or may feel unable to influence the result. Do not describe it as the most
controlled kind of intentional motivation. A useful response begins by understanding what is missing.
The diagram organizes reasons. It is not a compulsory ladder that everyone must climb. A person
can have several reasons at once, and reasons can change between activities or situations.
Someone may enjoy games, value repetitive practice and also feel pressure about selection.
Use the categories carefully
Ask what the person says about the activity. Do not assume that smiling proves intrinsic motivation
or that effort proves integrated regulation. Rewards and feedback also depend on context.
Information that helps someone understand progress is different from pressure that makes them feel
controlled.
The practical question is not, “Which student deserves the best label?” It is, “What reason is
supported by the evidence, and what would make the next action understandable and worthwhile?”
Worked example · fictional case
Enjoyment is not the same as a valued purpose
Maya says, “I enjoy the sound we make together.” That statement supports intrinsic
motivation. Ren says, “Scales are repetitive, but they help me become the musician I want to
be.” That statement supports a valued, potentially integrated reason. It does not establish that
Ren enjoys scales.
Both reasons can support participation. We would need more information before making
claims about either learner’s long-term behaviour.

Change the conditions, not just the slogan
Imagine arriving at practice and receiving an unexplained task that feels impossible. Nobody
answers questions, and partners exclude you. “Be motivated” does not address those conditions.
Self-determination theory identifies three psychological needs that help us examine an environment:
autonomy, competence and relatedness.
These needs are not prizes that only successful performers earn. Use them to ask what support a
learner might need. A small change in the task or interaction can be more relevant than another
speech about effort.
Autonomy: a meaningful sense of choice
Autonomy concerns experiencing an action as chosen and personally endorsed. It does not mean
having no rules, ignoring safety or choosing only easy tasks. A teacher can set an appropriate
learning target while explaining its purpose and offering useful choices within it.
For example, a learner might choose which of two suitable practice tasks to try first. They might help
set a realistic review point. The choice should matter to the learning, not merely change a decorative
detail. Clear reasons can also help someone understand why a constraint is necessary.
Competence: understanding how to make progress
Competence concerns feeling able to develop skill and affect what happens through action. It is not
the claim that the learner already knows everything. Clear demonstrations, manageable steps and
specific feedback can help connect practice to progress.
A task that is far beyond current understanding may need a smaller first step. A task that is too easy
may need a suitable challenge. Feedback such as “Your setup stayed consistent on the last three
attempts” provides information. “Be better” does not explain what to keep or change.
Relatedness: being included and supported
Relatedness concerns belonging and mattering to others. In a class, it can be supported by
respectful partners, being included in a group and being able to ask for help. It does not require
being popular, agreeing with everyone or sharing private information.
A support role can be enough for a plan: a teacher, coach or practice partner. You do not need to
name a real person. The useful detail is what help the performer would request and how that request
fits the task.
One change can support more than one need
A partner demonstration may support competence and relatedness. A choice between suitable tasks
may support autonomy while allowing a better challenge level. Explain the strongest connection
rather than forcing every change into all three categories.

Supporting a need is not a promise that motivation or performance will immediately improve. Ask

A welcoming first practice
A new player does not understand the serving setup and is left without a partner. The teacher

A direction, not a trophy
A  value    is a quality or direction that matters to a person. Examples include learning, contribution,
fairness and care. A goal   is a particular target. A learner may value learning and set a goal of
explaining one difficult idea clearly. The value can continue after that goal is reached.
Values help organize choices when several things compete for time. They do not make every
decision easy. A student can value both preparation and supporting a family activity. A workable plan
needs to consider the situation rather than pretend that one value removes all conflicts.
Make the value visible in behaviour
“Contribution” is a label. “Arrive with the agreed part prepared and help a partner understand the
next step” describes behaviour. Someone can observe whether the action happened. This makes
the plan easier to explain and review.
Avoid using words such as “good” or “strong” as the only evidence. They judge the whole person
without identifying an action. A useful response states what the performer will do, when they will do it
and how it connects to the chosen value.
You do not have to prove private beliefs in this assignment. You can choose a suitable value for the
fictional performer and explain a reasonable action. A teacher can assess that explanation without
deciding whether the value is truly part of your identity.
Toward and away are about the choice
A toward-value action fits the direction in the case. An away-from-value action moves away from it. If
a learner values improving understanding, asking about unclear feedback may be a toward action.
Hiding the feedback without reading it may move away from that direction.
These descriptions are not permanent labels for people. One missed practice does not establish that
someone lacks commitment. There may be a transport problem, a competing responsibility or
missing information. Describe the decision and context before interpreting it.
Acting while uncertain
Entering an “arena” is a metaphor for beginning before feeling perfectly ready. Treat that as a
metaphor for participating in learning despite ordinary uncertainty. A learner can ask a question,
share a rough draft or try an appropriately supported new step without knowing the outcome.
It is not an instruction to ignore safety, injury or a lack of essential skill. A useful action may be to ask
for help or change the task. Courage and careful planning can belong together.

Review the action, not the identity
At a review point, ask what happened and whether the action still fits. If it did not happen, identify a
barrier and revise the plan. This produces a next step. “I am not the right kind of person” produces a
judgment but no usable instruction.
Worked example · fictional case
Contribution during a rehearsal
A fictional musician values contribution to the ensemble. One toward action is to practise the
agreed entrance and arrive ready to follow the conductor. An away action would be to ignore
a known entrance problem while blaming the group.
If transport prevents attendance, contribution might instead involve contacting the teacher
and arranging a suitable way to prepare. The value stays meaningful while the action
changes with the constraint.

06 · Responding to mistakes
Keep the useful information in an error without turning it into a judgment of the whole person.
Learning goal: I can write an accurate observation, choose an adjustment and explain
evidence-based pride.
Before you begin: A useful response does not pretend the mistake never happened.
An error can become information
A student misses an entrance and thinks, “I am useless.” The entrance was missed, but the
conclusion about the whole person goes beyond that event. Compare it with, “I lost the count while
watching the audience.” The second statement identifies something that can be investigated and
changed.
A  fixed or entity view treats ability as something largely set that must be displayed. A growth or
incremental view treats skill as something that can develop through practice, strategy, feedback
and time. These are ways of interpreting learning, not permanent types of student.
Growth is more than praising effort
“Try harder” is not a complete learning strategy. A learner may be repeating an error because the
instruction is unclear. Useful feedback identifies what happened, offers a relevant adjustment and
gives a way to check the next attempt.
For example, “Practise the first sentence more slowly and check whether the main idea is clear”
gives a task. “You are talented” may feel encouraging, but does not explain that task. Praise should
not replace information about learning.

A growth-oriented response also has limits. Practice does not guarantee every desired result. A
learner may need a different method, more instruction, time or appropriate support. Recognizing that
need is part of adjusting the process, not evidence that effort is pointless.
Behaviour and the whole self
A useful distinction separates guilt  , which can focus on an action, from shame, which can become a
global judgment about the person. “I did not prepare the agreed section” identifies behaviour. “I am a
failure” describes the whole self.
Use this distinction to make the response more specific. It is not a tool for diagnosing a classmate’s
emotions. Nor does it mean a student must feel guilty to improve. A factual observation and repair
action can be useful without making a claim about how someone should feel.
What kind of pride is supported?
Authentic pride connects achievement to evidence such as preparation, effort and skill
development. “I used the feedback and made the entrance more consistent” identifies something
worth keeping. Hubristic pride centres on a broad claim of superiority, such as “I am naturally better
than everyone.”
The practical difference is what the statement lets the performer review. Specific evidence can
identify a useful process. A superiority claim does not explain what to repeat and can make
correction seem like an attack on status.
Use a three-part response
State the observation. Choose one adjustment. Decide what would show whether it helped. Keep
both success and mistakes specific. A strong explanation can recognize progress and still identify
something to work on next.
Worked example · fictional case
A serve after feedback
Alex says, “I rushed my setup after looking at another player’s result.” The proposed
adjustment is to use the practised setup and target cue on the next planned attempt. The
review checks whether the routine was used, separately from whether the serve scored.
If the setup becomes more consistent, Alex can recognize that progress. “I used the
feedback” is evidence-based pride. It does not require claiming to be a naturally superior
player.

Put a job on the calendar
“I want to improve” gives a direction but not a plan. What will the performer do? In which setting?
How will they check it? A useful goal helps answer those questions. It should also fit the performer’s
time, current learning and wider responsibilities.
A broad priority, such as learning or contribution, can guide several goals. A specific goal chooses
one target within that direction. Do not confuse having an important priority with having a workable
action for this week.
Three goal types
An   outcome goal concerns a result relative to others, such as winning a match or earning a place in
a group. Other people and conditions influence it. Caring about the outcome is understandable, but
the performer cannot guarantee it through a thought.
A  performance goal concerns a personal standard. Examples include a target serving percentage
or explaining a topic within an agreed time. It can help track progress, but execution and conditions
still affect the result.
A  process goal concerns an action used in the task. Examples include using a preparation routine,
checking the evidence before making a claim, or following the conductor’s entrance cue. These
actions are usually more directly controllable than the final result. They are not completely
independent of circumstances.
Use SMART as a planning check
In this course, SMART means Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.
Specific names the action. Measurable explains what can be observed or recorded. Achievable
checks whether it is workable. Relevant connects it to the actual need. Time-bound sets a review
point.
For example: “During the next two school practices, Alex will use the agreed setup before ten
planned serves and record whether it was used. Alex will review the record with the coach after the
second practice.” The number of attempts is a fictional planning choice, not a training prescription for
everyone.
A measurable goal can still be a poor fit. It may ignore transport, overload the schedule or focus on a
result that depends heavily on others. SMART does not replace judgment about those conditions.
Make the next action manageable
Keep the action clear and short. A long list of simultaneous cues can make execution harder to
organize. Choose the current learning need and a suitable challenge. The plan can include a longer-
term aim while making the next step small enough to begin.

A player may follow the routine and miss. Another may skip it and score. Record the planned action
and the outcome separately. Then inspect a pattern across attempts and decide what to adjust. One
result does not prove that the routine caused success or failure.

to a reason.
The process goal gives the student an action during rehearsal. The performance standard
helps review the rehearsal. The selection outcome still depends on other presentations and
the selection process.

08 · Effort, recovery and support
A useful plan changes when the demands no longer fit the performer’s resources.
Learning goal: I can identify information, support and a reasonable planning adjustment
without making a diagnosis.
Before you begin: Treat the case as a planning problem, not a test of toughness.
Effort is only part of a plan
A performer can care deeply about a goal and still struggle with the current schedule. More practice,
homework, travel and other demands all take time. When the plan no longer fits, “push harder” may
ignore the real problem. A useful review considers both what is being asked and the resources
available.
Recovery means allowing the resources and time needed after demanding activity. In this course, it
is part of planning. It is not a reward that must be earned by reaching a score. Nor is one feeling
enough to calculate exactly how much recovery a person needs.
Read the growth model carefully
“Stress plus rest equals growth” is a shorthand for considering challenge and recovery together.
Treat it as a simplified model, not a literal equation. Appropriate challenge, learning and recovery
can support development. They do not guarantee improvement after every session or tell every
learner to follow the same schedule.
The   SAID principle refers to specific adaptations to imposed demands. At this introductory level,
the useful planning point is that the kind of task matters. Practising an explanation is different from
training a physical skill. Neither a slogan nor a graph gives a personal training dose.

Describe patterns without diagnosing
Training discussions may refer to acute fatigue, functional overreaching, nonfunctional overreaching
and overtraining syndrome. These terms describe different responses to demands and recovery.
They are not categories students should assign to themselves from a short symptom checklist.
Temporary fatigue after a demanding activity is different from a continuing pattern of difficulty.
However, a classroom scenario cannot establish the cause of persistent tiredness, changes in sleep
or falling performance. The same observation can need more context and appropriate assessment.
Do not decide that a struggling performer is lazy or that high motivation means continuing is safe.
Motivation and readiness are different questions. If a person is worried about ongoing symptoms, a
trusted adult and suitable qualified support are more appropriate than a motivational test.
Ask useful questions
Start with practical information. What changed in the schedule? Are the instructions clear? What
commitments compete for time? What opportunities for rest and support are already available? The
performer does not need to share private health details in this assignment.
Then identify a discussion or adjustment. A teacher might help reorganize deadlines. A coach might
review the planned activity. A support request should explain the problem and ask for appropriate
help, rather than prescribe a diagnosis or treatment.
Keep the review open
A plan is a starting decision, not proof that the problem is solved. Decide when to review it and what
information would matter. Continued difficulty can be a reason for more support, not a reason to
blame the performer for failing to follow the plan perfectly.
Worked example · fictional case
When the schedule stops fitting
Alex has two practices, a presentation deadline and limited transport. Alex reports struggling
to recover under the current schedule. A reasonable first step is to discuss the demands with
the teacher or coach and ask which planned activities can be adjusted.
The class task is to describe the information and support needed. It is not to diagnose
overtraining, decide whether pain is “only effort,” or prescribe a harder session.

The same person can act differently
A learner speaks freely in a small rehearsal but becomes quiet in an unfamiliar group. Another works
carefully alone but benefits from a partner when a task is unclear. These examples show why one
label, such as “motivated” or “unmotivated,” may miss important information.
A  person-by-situation view considers both the person and the conditions. Relevant details can
include experience, reasons for participating, the task, feedback, resources and social setting. The
question is how these features interact in this case, not which permanent category the person
belongs in.
Social settings do not affect everyone the same way
An audience, partner or team can change a performance situation. A familiar skill and an unfamiliar
skill may need different preparation. A supportive partner and an evaluative audience are also
different conditions.
Do not prescribe solitude or group work from a brief personality label. Instead, describe the task and
compare evidence. Does the learner understand the instruction? What does the partner contribute?
What has changed when the difficulty appears? A reasonable plan may involve both independent
preparation and a supported review.
Make constraints visible
A plan is not realistic simply because it is written neatly. Check the actual week. Time, transport,
access to equipment, other deadlines and available support can affect what happens. These are
conditions to consider, not excuses to dismiss automatically.
For Alex, a Thursday practice may not be possible without transport. A science presentation also
needs preparation. A usable plan can prioritize one suitable school practice, a short planning task
and a check-in. The exact choice should fit the case and any guidance about the activity.
Use an if–then plan
An   if–then plan connects a likely barrier to a response. “If the gym session is cancelled, then I will
ask the coach which available preparation task still fits the goal” is more useful than “I will never
miss practice.” It names both the trigger and the next action.
Not every disruption can be predicted. Leave room to pause, ask questions or choose a different
response when new information appears. An automatic response should not override safety or
ignore a changed demand.

Glossary and sources
Motivation
Reasons and processes that start, direct and sustain action.
Direction
Where effort is aimed.
Intensity
How much effort is applied.
Intrinsic motivation
Doing an activity for its own interest or enjoyment.
Extrinsic motivation
Doing an activity for a separable purpose or result.
External regulation
Acting for an outside demand, reward or consequence.
Introjected regulation
Acting under internal pressure, such as guilt or protecting self-worth.
Identified regulation
Acting because a purpose is personally accepted as important.
Integrated regulation
Acting for a purpose that fits broader values and identity; it remains extrinsic.
Amotivation
Lack of intention to act.
Autonomy
Experiencing action as chosen and endorsed, within appropriate constraints.
Competence
Experiencing opportunities to develop skill and affect progress.
Relatedness
Experiencing belonging and support.
Value
A meaningful quality or direction that guides choices.

Adapt to a new constraint
Identify the most important passage, ask for focused feedback at an available time and revise the
review point. Preserve the purpose while changing the action to fit the new time and support.
Explain a choice and a limitation
Changing the environment addresses competence and access, not merely effort. The plan cannot
guarantee selection or explain every cause of difficulty.
Written review
Review 1: Intrinsic motivation concerns enjoyment or interest in the activity itself. Integrated
regulation concerns a purpose that fits broader values and identity. It remains extrinsic. The same
person can have both reasons.
Review 2: Use the stated barrier. A clear demonstration can support competence; a respectful
partner can support relatedness. Explain what to ask or observe after the change rather than
promise immediate motivation.
Review 3: Effort does not remove constraints, unclear instructions or recovery needs. Describe the
changed demand, identify support and adapt the action. A realistic revision is part of planning, not
proof of weak character.
Review 4: Name the earlier response and the exact change. For example, separate an outcome
goal from a process action or replace a global judgment with an observation. Explain why the
revision improves the reasoning rather than copy an entire new plan.
