Artists must be able to talk about their work. Critique is not about liking or disliking — it is about analyzing decisions, meaning, and effectiveness. Comparing your work to professional artists helps you understand where your work fits and how it can grow.

You will practise using art vocabulary to reflect on your work and compare it thoughtfully to the work of established artists.

What You Are Learning

In this lesson, you will:

  • Learn how to analyze artwork using critique vocabulary
  • Compare your work to professional artists without copying
  • Identify strengths, weaknesses, and next steps in your work
  • Practise written and visual reflection for portfolio development

This lesson helps you move from making art to thinking and speaking like an artist.

Formal Art Critique

A formal art critique is a way of carefully analyzing and discussing artwork using clear visual evidence. Instead of simply saying whether you like or dislike a piece of art, a critique focuses on explaining how the artwork is constructed and how it communicates meaning. When conducting a formal critique, you examine the composition of the image and the relationships between visual elements. This includes looking at how shapes, lines, colours, and forms are arranged and how they guide the viewer’s eye through the artwork. You also consider the elements of art and principles of design, such as balance, contrast, movement, emphasis, and unity, and how these contribute to the overall structure of the piece.

A critique also considers the techniques and materials used by the artist. Different materials and processes influence the appearance and emotional impact of an artwork. For example, rough textures, layered surfaces, or expressive brushstrokes can communicate different ideas than smooth or highly controlled techniques. Finally, a formal critique considers the meaning and intent of the artwork. This involves thinking about what ideas, messages, or emotions the artist may be communicating through their visual choices. A strong critique is evidence-based, meaning that observations are supported by specific details from the artwork rather than personal opinion alone.

 

Comparative Analysis

Comparative analysis involves examining two or more artworks together in order to understand how artists approach similar ideas in different ways. When comparing artworks, the goal is not to decide which piece is better, but rather to understand the decisions each artist made and how those decisions affect the viewer’s experience. This process begins by identifying similarities and differences between the works. These comparisons might involve subject matter, composition, colour use, materials, symbolism, or overall mood.

After identifying similarities and differences, students analyze why the artists made certain choices. For example, one artist might use bold colour and expressive lines to create emotional intensity, while another might use muted colours and careful composition to create a quieter or more reflective mood. Through comparative analysis, students learn how different artistic approaches can communicate different ideas or experiences. The goal is to evaluate the effectiveness of visual decisions, rather than simply judging whether an artwork is good or bad. This type of analysis helps students understand how artistic choices influence meaning.

 

Reflective Writing

Reflective writing allows artists to think critically about their own work and creative process. Reflection connects three important ideas: what you intended to create, what you actually created, and what you learned through the process. Artists often begin with a clear idea or theme, but the final artwork may develop in unexpected ways as they experiment with materials, composition, and technique. Reflection helps artists understand how their ideas evolved during the creative process.

Strong reflection goes beyond describing the artwork and instead explains the decisions made during creation. This includes discussing choices related to colour, composition, symbolism, materials, and technique. Effective reflective writing also includes specific visual evidence, meaning that you refer directly to aspects of your artwork to explain your thinking. Through reflection, artists become more aware of their strengths, challenges, and areas for growth. This process helps artists develop stronger ideas and more intentional work in future projects.
