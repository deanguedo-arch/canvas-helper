Artists rarely create work in isolation. Throughout history, artists have learned from the ideas, techniques, materials, and imagery of artists who came before them. Studying other artists helps artists expand their own visual language and develop new ways of thinking about art.

However, artists must also understand the difference between copying, appropriation, and inspiration. These three approaches describe different ways artists use ideas from earlier artwork.

Understanding these differences helps artists create work that is ethical, respectful, and original, while still participating in the long conversation that exists throughout art history.

This lesson will help you understand how artistic influence works so that you can confidently create your own artwork that responds to historical artists later in your portfolio.

 

Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

  • Explain the difference between copying, appropriation, and inspiration
  • Identify how artists influence one another
  • Analyze how historical and cultural imagery appears in later artwork
  • Describe how an artist can respond to earlier art without simply reproducing it

 

Key Vocabulary

Copying

Copying occurs when an artist recreates another artwork as closely as possible. The goal is to duplicate the original image rather than develop a new idea.

Artists sometimes copy artwork when practicing techniques or studying art history, but copying alone is not considered original artistic work.

Example: Painting an exact replica of a famous painting.

 

Appropriation

Appropriation occurs when an artist intentionally uses recognizable images, symbols, or visual elements from another artwork and places them in a new context.

The artist is not trying to hide the source. Instead, the artist wants the viewer to recognize the reference and think about its meaning.

Appropriation often raises questions about:

  • cultural meaning
  • ownership of imagery
  • reinterpretation of historical ideas

Example: Using a famous image but changing its message or context.

 

Inspiration

Inspiration occurs when an artist studies the ideas, techniques, processes, or style of another artist and then creates something new using those influences.

The final artwork may show similarities in mood, colour, technique, or theme, but the imagery itself is original.

Most artists throughout history work through inspiration rather than copying.

 

Why Artistic Influence Matters

Artists learn from each other in many ways:

  • studying techniques
  • exploring new materials
  • responding to cultural ideas
  • continuing conversations about identity, history, and society

Art history is not a series of isolated artworks. Instead, it is a network of influences, where artists build on earlier ideas while also challenging them.

 

Case Study 1 — Picasso and African Masks

In the early 1900s, the Spanish artist Pablo Picasso encountered African masks in museums in Paris. These masks had very different visual characteristics compared to European art at the time.

African masks often emphasize:

  • simplified shapes
  • geometric features
  • exaggerated facial forms
  • symbolic rather than realistic representation

Picasso became fascinated by these forms. Instead of painting faces realistically, he began simplifying and fragmenting facial features into angular shapes.

This influence is clearly visible in his painting Les Demoiselles d’Avignon (1907).

The faces in the painting resemble the stylized forms found in African masks. However, Picasso was not copying the masks directly. Instead, he was inspired by the visual language of abstraction and simplification.

This influence helped lead to the development of Cubism, one of the most important movements in modern art.

Case Study 2 — Basquiat and Graffiti Culture

 

Jean-Michel Basquiat began his artistic career as a graffiti artist in New York City during the late 1970s.

Graffiti culture includes:

  • spontaneous mark-making
  • bold text and symbols
  • expressive line work
  • layered surfaces

When Basquiat moved into gallery spaces, he continued to incorporate the visual language of graffiti into his paintings.

His artworks often include:

  • rough lines
  • words and phrases
  • crowns and symbols
  • layered imagery

Basquiat was not copying graffiti walls directly. Instead, he used the energy, symbolism, and visual style of graffiti culture as inspiration for his paintings.

This approach helped shape the Neo-Expressionist movement, which emphasized raw emotional expression and personal imagery.

 

Case Study 3 — Contemporary Indigenous Artists Responding to History

Contemporary Indigenous artists often respond to historical artworks that were created during colonial periods.

Many historical paintings depicted Indigenous peoples through the perspective of European settlers. These images often ignored Indigenous perspectives and experiences.

Artists such as Kent Monkman reinterpret historical imagery to challenge these narratives.

Monkman frequently recreates the style of large European historical paintings but includes:

  • Indigenous perspectives
  • contemporary social commentary
  • new storytelling elements

In these works, Monkman uses appropriation intentionally. Viewers recognize the style of classical European paintings, but the imagery now communicates a different story.

This approach allows the artist to question how history has been represented and whose voices have been included or excluded.
