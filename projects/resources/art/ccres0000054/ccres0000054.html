Lesson 8: Art Movements & Influence

Artists do not work in isolation. Every artist is influenced by ideas, movements, and other artists — but strong artists transform influence into something personal.

You are not copying styles; you are learning how to analyze, adapt, and reinterpret artistic influence.

 

What You Are Learning

In this lesson, you will:

  • Understand how art movements shape artistic choices
  • Analyze stylistic characteristics and ideas behind movements
  • Learn how to borrow strategies without copying images
  • Practice transforming influence into personal expression

This lesson helps you move from inspiration to intentional adaptation.

 

Technique Focus 

1. Stylistic Analysis

Stylistic analysis asks:

  • How is the artwork made?
  • What visual rules does it follow or break?
  • What ideas or beliefs shaped it?

Understanding style helps you make informed choices.

Stylistic Analysis

Stylistic analysis is the process of examining how an artwork is made rather than just what it shows. Instead of focusing only on subject matter, you analyze the artist’s visual choices and how those choices create meaning. When you do stylistic analysis, you are asking: How did the artist use line, colour, shape, texture, space, composition, and technique? What patterns or characteristics define their style? How do those choices affect the viewer?

What You Look For in Stylistic Analysis

In stylistic analysis, you examine visual elements such as brushstrokes, colour intensity, distortion, level of realism, and overall composition. For example, an Expressionist painting might use exaggerated colour and sharp, jagged lines to communicate emotion. A Pop Art piece might use flat colour, bold outlines, and repetition inspired by advertising. An Impressionist painting might show loose brushwork and attention to light. A Cubist artwork might fragment forms into geometric shapes.  These stylistic traits are not accidents. They are intentional decisions that communicate ideas.

Why Stylistic Analysis Matters

Stylistic analysis helps you understand that artists make choices for a reason. A thick, rough brushstroke creates a different feeling than a smooth, blended one. A limited colour palette creates a different mood than a full spectrum of bright colours. A distorted figure communicates differently than a realistic portrait.  By studying style, you begin to see that technique and meaning are connected. The “how” influences the “why.”

How to Write a Stylistic Analysis 

When writing about style, you should describe what you see and explain its effect. For example, you might say that the artist uses bold complementary colours to create tension and energy, or that the composition is tightly cropped to make the viewer feel close to the subject. Strong stylistic analysis connects observation to interpretation.  Instead of saying, “The painting looks cool,” you would explain how the specific use of line, colour, and composition creates a certain mood or message.

How Stylistic Analysis Makes You a Better Artist

When you practice stylistic analysis, you become more aware of your own choices. You start asking yourself why you are using a certain colour or composition. You begin designing intentionally instead of working randomly.

Understanding style also helps you experiment. You can try working in a limited palette, exaggerating proportion, or flattening space on purpose. As a result, your artwork becomes more thoughtful and deliberate.

In short, stylistic analysis trains you to see deeply, think critically, and create with intention. It turns you from someone who simply makes images into someone who understands visual language.

2. Reinterpretation in Art

Reinterpretation is when an artist takes an existing artwork, image, idea, or style and transforms it into something new with a different meaning, context, or perspective. It is not copying. It is responding.

When you reinterpret something, you are asking:
What happens if this image is placed in a new time period?
What if it reflects a different culture, identity, or issue?
What if I change the style, materials, or message?

Reinterpretation is a conversation between artists across time.

What Reinterpretation Looks Like

An artist might take a classical portrait and reinterpret it in a Pop Art style using bold colours and repetition. Another might recreate a historical painting but replace the figures with modern characters to comment on current social issues. Some artists reinterpret famous works by changing materials, such as turning a painting into a sculpture or digital piece.

The key is transformation. The original source is recognizable, but the new version communicates something different.

Why Artists Reinterpret

Artists reinterpret to challenge ideas, question traditions, or give voice to perspectives that were previously ignored. Some reinterpretations critique power structures or historical narratives. Others celebrate or honour past artists while adding a contemporary viewpoint.

Reinterpretation allows artists to explore identity, culture, politics, and social change. It acknowledges art history while refusing to simply repeat it.

Why Reinterpretation Matters for Art Students

Reinterpretation helps you think critically instead of copying. When you reinterpret, you must understand the original work deeply before you can change it meaningfully. This strengthens both your analytical and creative skills.

It also pushes you to develop your own voice. Instead of asking, “Can I draw this well?” you begin asking, “What do I want to say about this?”

Reinterpretation encourages risk-taking, conceptual thinking, and problem-solving. You learn how meaning shifts when style, context, or materials change.

How Reinterpretation Makes You a Better Artist

When you practice reinterpretation, you become more aware of artistic choices. You begin to understand how composition, colour, symbolism, and context influence meaning. You learn that art is not fixed. It evolves as artists respond to one another and to the world around them.

Reinterpretation turns art into dialogue rather than imitation. It shows that creativity is not about starting from nothing; it is about building on what came before and reshaping it with intention.

In short, reinterpretation is how artists participate in history while creating something new.

  

3. Blending Influences

Blending influences is when an artist combines ideas, styles, techniques, or cultural references from different sources to create something new. Instead of copying one artist or one movement, you take inspiration from multiple places and merge them into your own visual voice.

Blending influences is not about mixing randomly. It is about making intentional choices that reflect what you are drawn to and why.

What Blending Influences Looks Like

You might see a realistic portrait painted with Pop Art colour choices. You might see traditional cultural patterns combined with modern digital illustration. You might see a painting that blends Expressionist brushstrokes with a Surrealist concept. The final piece feels unified, but you can recognize multiple inspirations within it.

The key is that the work does not feel divided. The influences work together instead of competing.

Why Artists Blend Influences

Most contemporary artists are influenced by many things at once: art history, social media, cultural traditions, music, fashion, politics, and personal experience. Blending influences reflects how we actually experience the world — layered and interconnected.

Blending also allows artists to push boundaries. When you combine ideas from different movements or cultures, you often create something unexpected and original.

Why This Matters for Art Students

When students begin learning about art movements, it is common to copy one style directly. That can be helpful for learning techniques, but growth happens when you start combining ideas intentionally.

Blending influences helps you move from imitation to innovation. You start asking questions such as: What do I like about this artist’s colour use? What do I like about this other artist’s composition? How can I combine those qualities in a way that reflects my own ideas?

This process builds critical thinking and self-awareness.

How Blending Influences Makes You a Better Artist

Blending influences develops flexibility and creativity. You learn to analyze style, extract specific techniques, and adapt them. You become more intentional about your choices because you are not simply copying — you are designing.

It also helps you develop a personal style. Your style begins to emerge from the combination of what inspires you and how you transform it. Over time, your work becomes recognizable not because it matches one movement, but because it reflects your unique blend of influences.

In short, blending influences is how artists grow beyond practice studies and begin creating work that feels authentically their own.

 

Art History: Minimalism

 

Minimalism emerged in the 1960s as a reaction against emotional and highly expressive art movements like Abstract Expressionism. Minimalist artists believed that art did not need to be dramatic, symbolic, or full of personal storytelling. Instead, they focused on simplicity, order, and clarity.

Minimalism reduces artwork to its most essential elements. This often means simple geometric shapes, limited colour palettes, repeated forms, and clean lines. Artists such as Donald Judd, Agnes Martin, and Frank Stella created works that appear calm, structured, and precise. The materials are often industrial, and the surfaces are clean and controlled.

In Minimalism, the artwork is not trying to tell a story or represent something else. A cube is simply a cube. A line is simply a line. The focus is on form, space, proportion, and the viewer’s physical experience of the object. Many minimalist sculptures are large and placed directly in space so that viewers become aware of how their body moves around them.

This movement shifts attention away from emotion and toward perception. Instead of asking what the artwork means symbolically, viewers are encouraged to notice scale, balance, repetition, and the relationship between the object and the surrounding environment.

For art students, Minimalism is important because it teaches restraint and intentionality. It shows that powerful artwork does not always require complexity. Sometimes removing elements makes the design stronger. By limiting colour, shape, or detail, you learn to focus on composition and structure.

Minimalism also strengthens problem-solving skills. When you reduce your choices, every decision matters more. The placement of a single line or the proportion of a single shape can change the entire composition.

In short, Minimalism demonstrates that simplicity can be powerful. It reminds artists that clarity, balance, and thoughtful reduction can create strong visual impact without excess.

Artists 

Michael Snow

Michael Snow is a Canadian artist known for his experimental work across film, sculpture, photography, and installation. He is associated with conceptual art and explores perception, time, and the act of seeing.

Snow’s work often challenges viewers to notice:

  • How images are constructed
  • How time affects experience
  • How perception shapes meaning

Key Characteristics of Snow’s Work:

  • Concept-driven experimentation
  • Minimal or reduced imagery
  • Focus on perception rather than representation

How This Connects to Your Work: Snow shows that influence can be intellectual as well as visual. You can borrow ideas and questions, not just style.

 Daphne Odjig

Daphne Odjig was an Indigenous Canadian artist whose work blended modernist influences with Indigenous forms and storytelling. She is associated with the Woodland School while also drawing from Cubism and Expressionism.

Odjig’s work demonstrates:

  • Synthesis of cultural tradition and modern art
  • Flowing forms and strong outlines
  • Narrative and emotional expression

Key Characteristics of Odjig’s Work:

  • Curvilinear forms
  • Flattened space
  • Blended cultural and modern influences

How This Connects to Your Work: Odjig shows that influence does not erase identity. Artists can engage with movements while remaining grounded in culture and personal perspective.

 

Pablo Picasso

Pablo Picasso was an international artist who helped develop Cubism. His work radically changed how space, form, and perspective could be represented.

Picasso’s practice was marked by:

  • Constant experimentation
  • Reinvention across styles
  • Breaking and rebuilding visual rules

Key Characteristics of Picasso’s Work:

  • Fragmented forms
  • Multiple viewpoints
  • Simplification and abstraction

How This Connects to Your Work: Picasso demonstrates that artists evolve by questioning rules. Influence is a starting point, not an endpoint.

Influence becomes style when you make it your own.
