Lesson 7: Experimental Media & Material Meaning
Materials are not neutral. What you choose to work with — and how you use it — carries meaning. At the Art 30 level, materials become part of the message.
 You are encouraged to experiment, test, and reflect. You do not need access to expensive supplies — thoughtful choices matter more than polish.
What You Are Learning
In this lesson, you will:
Explore how materials and processes communicate meaning
Experiment with non-traditional or combined media
Understand how artists use material choice to reinforce concept
Practice documenting experimentation and decision-making
This lesson shifts focus from what the image shows to what it is made from.
Technique Focus (What You Are Practising)
1. Mixed Media & Combination
Mixed media means combining two or more materials intentionally.
Examples:
Drawing + collage
Paint + found materials
Image + text
The goal is not decoration — it is purposeful interaction between materials.
2. Found & Everyday Materials
Found materials include:
Packaging
Fabric
Cardboard
Natural materials
Printed text or images
These materials already carry associations and meaning.
3. Process as Meaning
How something is made can be just as important as the final image.
Examples:
Repetition → labour, persistence
Destruction → loss, tension
Layering → memory, complexity
Art History
In the 20th century, artists began rejecting the idea that art must use traditional materials.
Movements such as Dada, assemblage, and contemporary installation art questioned:
What materials are “allowed” in art
Who decides value
How everyday objects can carry meaning
In contemporary practice, material choice is understood as conceptual language.
Dada MovementDada began around 1916 during World War I. It was not just an art style—it was a protest. Dada artists were frustrated and angry about the war, politics, and what they saw as a society that had lost its sense of logic and humanity. Instead of responding with traditional, beautiful art, they responded with nonsense, absurdity, and shock.
Dada rejected the idea that art had to be skilled, meaningful in a traditional way, or even visually pleasing. Artists such as Marcel Duchamp challenged the art world by presenting ordinary objects as art. His work Fountain, which was simply a urinal turned on its side and signed with a fake name, asked a powerful question: if an artist chooses it, can anything be art? This idea introduced the concept of the “readymade,” where the idea behind the artwork mattered more than craftsmanship.
Other artists, like Hannah Höch, used photomontage, cutting and rearranging images from newspapers and magazines to create chaotic, political compositions. These works often criticized government, gender roles, and mass media. The messy, fragmented look reflected a world that felt broken and unstable.
Dada artists often used humor, randomness, and chance. They might create poetry by pulling words from a hat or assemble artworks without planning. This was intentional. If the world seemed irrational, their art would be irrational too. Dada questioned authority, tradition, and even the definition of art itself.
For art students, Dada is important because it teaches that art is not only about technical skill. It can be about ideas, questions, and challenging systems. Dada encourages experimentation, risk-taking, and thinking critically about culture. It shows that sometimes the most powerful art does not try to be beautiful—it tries to make you think.
In many ways, Dada paved the way for later movements like Conceptual Art and Surrealism. It expanded the boundaries of what art could be and proved that creativity can also be a form of rebellion.
Artists
Brian Jungen
Brian Jungen is a Dane-zaa First Nations artist known for transforming everyday consumer objects into sculptural works. By reworking familiar materials, Jungen forces viewers to reconsider value, identity, and culture.
Jungen’s work emphasizes:
Transformation of materials
Concept before appearance
Critical thinking through process
Key Characteristics of Jungen’s Work:
Use of found or mass-produced materials
Strong conceptual intent
Visible transformation
How This Connects to Your Work: Jungen demonstrates that materials can question meaning. What you choose to work with can say as much as what you depict.
Beau Dick
Beau Dick was a Kwakwaka’wakw artist known for carving ceremonial masks used in performances and potlatches. His work demonstrates that materials can hold cultural, spiritual, and ceremonial meaning.
Dick’s practice highlights:
Respect for material traditions
Meaning embedded in process
Art as part of community and ceremony
Key Characteristics of Dick’s Work:
Wood carving and painting
Symbolic forms
Cultural continuity
How This Connects to Your Work: Dick’s work shows that materials are never neutral. They are chosen with intention, respect, and purpose.
Anselm Kiefer
Anselm Kiefer is an international artist known for using heavy, unconventional materials such as straw, ash, lead, and clay. His work often addresses memory, history, and trauma.
Kiefer’s materials:
Add physical weight to meaning
Create texture and tension
Emphasize history and process
Key Characteristics of Kiefer’s Work:
Thick, layered surfaces
Rough, distressed materials
Concept-driven material choice
How This Connects to Your Work: Kiefer shows that roughness and imperfection can strengthen meaning. Materials can make ideas feel physical and real.
What your work is made of is part of what it says.
