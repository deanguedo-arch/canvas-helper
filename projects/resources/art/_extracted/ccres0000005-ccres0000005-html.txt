Sketchbook Task — Learning the Visual Language of Art
Your sketchbook is not meant to be a book of perfect drawings. Instead, it is a place where artists explore ideas, experiment, and think visually. Many artists fill their sketchbooks with rough drawings, quick notes, and visual experiments.
The goal of this assignment is to begin using your sketchbook as a thinking space.
Page 1 — Exploring the Elements of Art
On one page of your sketchbook, divide the page into six sections. Each section should be labeled with one element of art.
In each section you will create a small sketch that demonstrates the element and write one sentence explaining how that element can communicate something visually.
For example, in the section labeled Line, a student might draw several different lines across the page. One might be jagged, another smooth and curved, and another thick and heavy. The student might then write a sentence such as:“Sharp jagged lines can create tension or excitement.”
In the section labeled Shape, a student might draw several overlapping circles, triangles, and organic shapes. The written note might explain that shapes simplify objects and help artists organize images.
For Value, a student might create a simple value scale that gradually changes from very dark to very light. The note might explain that strong light and dark differences create contrast and help areas stand out.
The goal is not detailed drawing but demonstrating that you understand how the element works.
Example Sketchbook Page (Elements)
A typical page might contain six boxes with small visual experiments and short notes written beside them. The drawings may be rough, but the labels and ideas should be clear.
Page 2 — Exploring the Principles of Design
On another page of your sketchbook, divide the page into four sections. Each section should be labeled with one principle of design.
In each section you will create a small diagram that shows how the principle works. You will also write one sentence describing the idea.
For example, in the section labeled Balance, a student might draw two boxes. One box could show symmetrical balance with identical shapes on each side, while the other shows asymmetrical balance with different shapes that still feel stable.
In the section labeled Emphasis, a student might draw several small circles and one large dark circle in the middle. The written explanation might say:“The largest and darkest circle becomes the focal point.”
For Movement, a student might draw curved lines that guide the eye across the page, explaining that lines can direct how viewers look at an image.
Example Sketchbook Page (Principles)
Strong sketchbook pages usually contain simple diagrams and short written explanations that demonstrate understanding.
Page 3 — Composition Thumbnail
On half a page of your sketchbook you will plan a very small composition. Artists often create thumbnail sketches before beginning a final artwork. These sketches are small and quick, allowing the artist to explore ideas.
First write which element of art you want to focus on and which principle of design you want to use.
Next create one or two small thumbnail sketches showing how you might arrange shapes or lines on the page. These sketches should be very small and quick.
Finally write one or two sentences explaining how your element and principle will work together.
For example, a student might choose line as their element and movement as their principle. Their sketches might show curved lines flowing across the page to guide the viewer’s eye.

Page 4 — Reflection
On half a page of your sketchbook you will briefly reflect on what you learned in this lesson. Your reflection should answer three questions.
Explain which element of art was easiest for you to understand and why. Describe which principle of design felt most difficult and explain what made it challenging. Finally, write a few sentences about what you learned about how art communicates ideas visually.
Your reflection does not need to be long. Honest thinking is more important than writing a perfect explanation.
What This Assignment Is Assessing
This assignment focuses on your understanding of the elements and principles of art and your willingness to experiment in your sketchbook. Your teacher is looking for evidence that you attempted the exercises, labeled your work clearly, and wrote simple explanations showing your thinking.
Your artistic skill, realism, or drawing ability are not being graded in this assignment. Perfect drawings are not expected.
The strongest sketchbook work usually includes simple drawings, clear labels, short written explanations, and evidence that all pages were completed thoughtfully.
