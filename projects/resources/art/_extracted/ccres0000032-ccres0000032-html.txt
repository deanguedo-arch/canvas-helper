Lesson 5: Colour & Emotion
Communicating Feeling with Colour
Colour is one of the most powerful tools artists use to communicate emotion and atmosphere. While colour can describe the appearance of objects, artists often use colour to express feeling rather than realism. A landscape painted with bright orange skies and purple mountains may not represent how the place truly looks, but it can communicate excitement, warmth, or intensity.
Artists rarely choose colours randomly. Instead, they consider how colours interact with one another and how those combinations influence the viewer’s emotional response. Certain colours can create calmness or energy, while others can suggest tension or mystery.
In this lesson you will explore how colour relationships influence mood. Instead of focusing on painting objects exactly as they appear, you will experiment with colour combinations that communicate a specific emotional tone. Through this process you will begin learning how artists use colour intentionally to shape how viewers experience an artwork.
This lesson builds on the value and form skills you developed earlier. While value creates structure and depth, colour introduces emotional expression and visual atmosphere.
What You Are Learning
In this lesson you will begin understanding how basic colour relationships affect how an artwork feels. You will explore how warm and cool colours interact and how complementary colours create contrast and visual energy.
You will also learn about the idea of a limited palette, which means intentionally choosing only a small group of colours for an artwork. Limiting colour choices can strengthen unity and make the emotional tone of the artwork clearer.
Through sketchbook exercises and a final painting project, you will practice selecting colours intentionally rather than automatically. By the end of the lesson you will create a painting that communicates a chosen emotion through colour relationships.
Technique Focus
Understanding Colour Relationships
Colours rarely exist alone within an artwork. Their effect often depends on the colours surrounding them. Artists study colour relationships to understand how different colours interact visually and emotionally.
One important relationship involves warm colours, which include reds, oranges, and yellows. These colours often feel energetic, intense, or lively. In contrast, cool colours, such as blues, greens, and purples, tend to feel calm, quiet, or distant.
Another important relationship involves complementary colours, which are colours located opposite each other on the colour wheel. Examples include red and green, blue and orange, and yellow and purple. When complementary colours appear next to each other they create strong visual contrast and energy.
Understanding these relationships allows artists to create more intentional emotional effects in their work.

Colour and Mood
Colour has a powerful psychological effect on viewers. Artists often use colour choices to suggest mood or emotional atmosphere.
Warm colours such as red or orange may suggest excitement, passion, or energy. Cooler colours such as blue or green may create a sense of calm or quiet reflection. Darker colours can create mystery or tension, while lighter colours may feel joyful or peaceful.
It is important to remember that colour meanings are not fixed rules. The same colour may suggest different emotions depending on how it is used. What matters most is that the colour choices remain consistent throughout the artwork so that the emotional message feels clear.
Artists often experiment with different colour combinations before deciding which ones best communicate their intended feeling.

Limited Palettes
A limited palette means intentionally choosing only a small number of colours to use in an artwork. Many artists limit themselves to two, three, or four main colours rather than using every colour available.
Limiting colour choices often creates stronger visual unity. When the same colours appear repeatedly throughout an artwork, the image feels more connected and intentional.
Working with fewer colours can also help artists focus more carefully on relationships between colours. Instead of constantly switching colours, artists explore how variations in lightness, darkness, and intensity affect the image.
Many famous paintings use surprisingly small colour palettes. Limitation often encourages creativity and stronger design choices.

Art History and Context
During the late nineteenth century, many artists began experimenting with colour in new ways. Instead of painting scenes exactly as they appeared, artists became interested in how colour could express emotion and personal experience.
This shift is often associated with Post-Impressionism, a movement that expanded on earlier Impressionist ideas. Artists such as Vincent van Gogh and Paul Gauguin used bold, expressive colour to communicate feeling rather than strict realism.
Instead of asking which colours were “correct,” these artists asked which colours best expressed the mood or meaning of the scene. This approach helped transform colour into a powerful emotional tool within art.
Today, artists continue to use colour intentionally to communicate emotion, atmosphere, and symbolic meaning.

Artists Who Use Colour Expressively
Christi Belcourt
Christi Belcourt is a Métis artist whose paintings are inspired by traditional beadwork patterns. Her work often features intricate arrangements of plants and animals painted with rich, carefully chosen colours.
Belcourt’s colour choices are not random. They often reflect connections to nature and Métis cultural traditions. The repetition of colour patterns across the artwork creates rhythm and unity.
Her work demonstrates how colour can communicate meaning, cultural identity, and respect for the natural world.

Ted Harrison
Ted Harrison was a Canadian artist known for painting landscapes of the Yukon using bold and expressive colours. Rather than painting mountains and skies realistically, Harrison used unexpected colour combinations to create emotional impact.
His paintings often include bright oranges, purples, and blues arranged in simplified shapes. These colour choices make the landscapes feel energetic and imaginative.
Harrison’s work shows how colour choices alone can transform how a place feels to the viewer.

Vincent van Gogh
Vincent van Gogh used colour as a powerful emotional language. His paintings often contain intense, saturated colours applied with energetic brushstrokes.
Instead of attempting to reproduce the exact colours of a scene, van Gogh used colour to communicate his feelings about the subject. Bright yellows, deep blues, and vibrant greens combine to create a sense of movement and emotional intensity.
His work reminds us that colour can express personal experience as much as visual reality.
