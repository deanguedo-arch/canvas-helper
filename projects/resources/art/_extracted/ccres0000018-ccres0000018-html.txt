Lesson 2: Advanced Drawing & Personal Mark-Making
Drawing at the Art 30 level is not about copying what you see — it is about how your marks communicate emotion, energy, and intention. Your line quality becomes part of your artistic voice.
You are expected to experiment, take risks, and let go of the idea that drawings must look “finished” or “correct.”
In this lesson, you will:
Push beyond accuracy into expressive mark-making
Explore how line, texture, and gesture communicate feeling
Experiment with unconventional drawing tools
Build confidence and individuality in your drawing style
This lesson helps you move from careful drawing to intentional, expressive drawing.
Technique Focus
1. Expressive Line & Mark-Making
Not all lines communicate the same feeling.
Line can be:
Fast or slow
Heavy or light
Smooth or scratchy
Controlled or chaotic
Your goal is to explore how line quality changes meaning, not how accurate the image looks.

2. Unconventional Tools
You are encouraged to draw with tools that are not traditional drawing pencils, such as:
Sticks or cardboard edges
Cotton swabs or sponges
Found objects dipped in ink or paint
Thick markers or brush pens
Different tools force different marks — and different thinking.
3. Gesture vs Sustained Drawing
Gesture drawing captures movement and energy quickly (30 seconds–5 minutes)
Sustained drawing allows for repetition, layering, and emotional depth (20+ minutes)
Both approaches are valuable and should be used intentionally.

Why artists do gesture drawing
Gesture drawing trains you to:
See the whole pose, not tiny details
Capture life and movement
Stop overthinking
Draw confidently and freely
Think of it like a warm-up or a quick note instead of a finished essay.
Why artists do sustained drawing
Sustained drawing helps you:
Improve realism
Develop patience and focus
Make intentional choices
Practice finishing work
This is where technical skill and polish grow.

Art History: Expressionism
Expressionism was an early 20th-century art movement that focused on showing inner emotion rather than outer reality. Instead of trying to paint the world exactly as it looked, Expressionist artists painted how it felt. Their goal was not realism but emotional intensity.
Artists used exaggerated colour, distorted figures, and dramatic lines to communicate anxiety, isolation, fear, excitement, or tension. In works like The Scream, Munch twisted the sky into swirling waves of red and orange to express overwhelming emotion. The landscape is not realistic; it mirrors the psychological state of the figure.
Expressionist artists often distorted faces and bodies on purpose. Proportions might be stretched, lines might feel jagged, and colours might be unnatural. These choices were intentional. The distortion makes the emotion stronger and more visible. A calm scene painted realistically might feel neutral, but a distorted scene with intense colour feels urgent and personal.
Expressionism developed partly as a reaction to a rapidly changing world filled with industrial growth, political tension, and social anxiety. Many artists wanted their work to reflect the emotional stress of modern life. Rather than painting beautiful surfaces, they painted raw human experience.
For art students, Expressionism is important because it teaches that art does not need to look “perfect” to be powerful. Technical skill matters, but emotional honesty matters just as much. Expressionism encourages you to ask what you are feeling and how you can show that visually. You might use sharp lines to show anger, dark heavy colours to show sadness, or exaggerated proportions to show vulnerability.
Expressionism shows that art can be a tool for self-expression and social commentary. It shifts the question from “Does it look accurate?” to “Does it feel real?” When you understand this, you begin to see that artistic choices are not mistakes—they are decisions that communicate meaning.

Artists
Shary Boyle
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ps5otzKxR_4
Shary Boyle is a contemporary Canadian artist known for detailed, often unsettling drawings and sculptures. Her work frequently combines myth, fairy tales, and personal narrative. Boyle uses decorative, highly intentional line to create images that feel both delicate and disturbing.
Her drawings show that:
Line can be beautiful and uncomfortable at the same time
Narrative can be suggested without being fully explained
Decorative detail can carry emotional weight
Key Characteristics of Boyle’s Work:
Intricate, expressive line work
Mythical and symbolic imagery
Emotional tension beneath decorative surfaces
How This Connects to Your Work: Boyle demonstrates that expressive drawing does not need to be messy to be powerful. Careful, repeated lines can still communicate emotion and meaning when used intentionally.
Brian Jungen
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Rv_k96SHOkA
Brian Jungen is a Dane-zaa First Nations artist best known for transforming everyday objects into powerful sculptural works. His creative process begins with experimentation, sketching, and testing ideas, rather than planning a perfect final image.
While Jungen is primarily known for sculpture, his approach to art-making emphasizes:
Exploration over precision
Concept before polish
Materials and marks as meaning-makers
Key Characteristics of Jungen’s Approach:
Experimental process
Use of found materials
Concept-driven mark-making and planning
How This Connects to Your Work: Jungen’s practice shows that drawing can be investigative. Your sketchbook drawings do not need to be beautiful — they need to help you think, test, and question ideas.
Alberto Giacometti
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8obPEldTaUs
Alberto Giacometti was an international artist known for his elongated figures and raw, searching drawing style. He often drew the same subject repeatedly, layering lines as he worked.
Giacometti believed that drawing was a process of searching, not a process of copying. His drawings show uncertainty, revision, and persistence.
Key Characteristics of Giacometti’s Work:
Repeated, overlapping lines
Elongated, fragile figures
Visible struggle and revision
How This Connects to Your Work: Giacometti’s drawings prove that emotional truth matters more than accuracy. Repetition, erasing, and reworking are signs of engagement — not failure.
