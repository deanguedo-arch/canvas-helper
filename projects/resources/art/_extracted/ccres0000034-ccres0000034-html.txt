Lesson 5: Personal Narrative & Visual Storytelling
Art can tell stories without words. Personal experiences, memories, and moments are powerful sources for visual art when they are translated through symbol, sequence, and visual metaphor.
You are not expected to share everything about your life — you are expected to make intentional choices about what and how you communicate visually.
What You Are Learning
In this lesson, you will:
Explore how personal experiences can become visual narratives
Use symbolism and sequencing to suggest stories
Understand how artists communicate identity and experience visually
Develop confidence using your own life as artistic source material
This lesson helps you move from single images to story-driven artwork.
Technique Focus
1. Visual Storytelling
Visual storytelling uses images to suggest:
A moment
A memory
A relationship
A change over time
Stories do not need a beginning–middle–end. They can be implied through mood, symbol, or repetition.

2. Symbolism in Narrative
Symbols allow you to tell personal stories without being literal.
Examples:
An empty chair → absence or loss
A path → transition or growth
A repeated object → memory or habit
Symbolism protects privacy while still communicating meaning.
3. Sequencing & Repetition
Narratives can be built through:
Multiple images
Repeated symbols
Changes in scale, colour, or composition
Even a single image can feel narrative if it suggests before or after.
Art History & Context (Why This Matters)
Artists throughout history have told stories visually:
Cave paintings recorded shared experiences
Religious art communicated belief and morality
Contemporary artists often explore personal identity and lived experience
In modern and contemporary art, personal narrative is valued as a valid and powerful subject.
In Art 30, your story matters — how you choose to tell it matters even more.
Artists
Emily Carr
Emily Carr was a Canadian artist whose work reflects a deep personal connection to landscape and place. Rather than painting landscapes as neutral scenery, Carr used them to express emotion, spirituality, and identity.
Carr’s work often:
Reflects personal experience of place
Uses landscape as emotional narrative
Suggests presence, absence, and memory
Key Characteristics of Carr’s Work:
Expressive brushwork
Simplified forms
Strong emotional atmosphere
How This Connects to Your Work: Carr demonstrates that personal narrative does not need to show people or events directly. Place, objects, and environment can carry story and meaning.

Joane Cardinal-Schubert
Joane Cardinal-Schubert was a Blackfoot artist whose work addressed themes of identity, colonialism, loss, and resilience. She often combined imagery, text, and symbolic objects to tell deeply personal and cultural stories.
Her work shows that:
Personal narrative can also be political
Art can respond to lived experience and history
Symbols can carry layered meanings
Key Characteristics of Cardinal-Schubert’s Work:
Use of text and image together
Symbolic objects and forms
Exploration of identity and history
How This Connects to Your Work: Cardinal-Schubert’s work shows that storytelling in art can be subtle and layered. You can suggest complex ideas without explaining everything directly.
Kara Walker
Kara Walker is an international artist known for using silhouette imagery to tell narratives about history, power, and identity. Her work often feels story-like, even when a clear plot is not provided.
Walker’s approach emphasizes:
Visual metaphor
Strong contrast
Viewer interpretation
Key Characteristics of Walker’s Work:
Narrative silhouettes
Symbolic figures
Ambiguity and tension
How This Connects to Your Work: Walker demonstrates that narrative does not need to be comfortable or obvious. Allowing viewers to interpret your story can make the work more powerful.
Your story does not need words — it needs intention.
