Lesson 2: Observation & Drawing Foundations 

Many people believe that drawing well is something you are either born able to do or not. In reality, drawing is a skill that improves through observation and practice. Artists do not simply draw what they imagine something looks like. Instead, they train themselves to slow down, study what is actually in front of them, and record those observations carefully.

Your brain often tries to simplify what you see into symbols. For example, when people draw an eye from memory, they often draw a simple almond shape with a circle inside it. This is a symbol for an eye, not what an eye truly looks like. Observational drawing helps you move beyond symbols and begin to draw what you actually see.

This lesson introduces several exercises that help artists improve their ability to observe. These exercises may feel awkward at first. That is normal and expected. Many of the drawings you create during this lesson are not meant to look “good.” Their purpose is to train your eyes and your hand to work together more carefully.

The skills you develop in this lesson form the foundation for everything that follows in Art 10. Observation, proportion, and value will appear again in drawing, painting, design work, and portfolio projects throughout the course.

What You Are Learning

In this lesson you will begin learning how observation improves drawing accuracy. You will practice drawing what you see rather than what your brain assumes is there. You will also learn that drawing ability develops through repetition and focused practice rather than natural talent.

As you work through the exercises in this lesson, you will explore how artists pay attention to proportions, structure, and the relationships between shapes. You will also begin to observe light and shadow, which artists describe as value.

By the end of this lesson you will create your first observational drawing that includes both accurate outlines and simple shading.

Technique Focus

Blind Contour Drawing

One of the most important exercises artists use to strengthen observation is called blind contour drawing. In this exercise you draw the outline of an object without looking at your paper. Instead, your eyes stay focused on the object you are observing.

The goal of blind contour drawing is not to create a beautiful image. Instead, the goal is to slow your eyes down so they follow the edges of the object carefully. As your eyes move slowly around the edges of the object, your hand moves across the paper at the same pace.

Because you are not looking at your paper, the drawing will likely look strange or distorted. Lines may overlap or wander across the page. This is completely normal. The value of the exercise is that it forces your brain to stop relying on simple symbols and begin truly observing the object.

Artists use blind contour drawing to improve hand–eye coordination and to break the habit of drawing from memory. Over time this exercise helps artists become more confident in observing shapes and proportions.

Observational Contour Drawing

After completing a blind contour drawing, artists often move to observational contour drawing. In this exercise you are allowed to look at both the object and your paper, but the focus remains on careful observation rather than speed.

Contour drawing focuses on the outer edges and major internal edges of an object. Instead of shading or adding details immediately, the artist first studies the structure of the object.

While drawing, artists pay attention to where shapes change direction and how one part of the object relates to another. They consider questions such as whether one section is wider or narrower than another, or whether an angle slopes upward or downward.

When artists draw slowly and carefully in this way, their drawings begin to capture accurate proportions and shapes.

Value from Observation

Another important part of observational drawing is learning to see value, which is the range from light to dark in an image.

Most objects are not one flat colour. When light shines on an object, some areas receive more light and appear brighter, while other areas fall into shadow and appear darker.

Artists observe where the light source is coming from and how that light creates highlights and shadows across the object. The area where light hits directly is often the lightest area. The side turned away from the light becomes darker.

By gradually shading from light to dark, artists create the illusion that a flat drawing surface contains a three-dimensional object.

These exercises help artists learn how light and shadow define the form of objects.

Understanding Light and Shadow

When light strikes an object, it creates a predictable pattern of brightness and shadow. Artists study this pattern carefully so that their shading matches what they observe rather than what they imagine.

The highlight is the brightest part of the object where the light hits directly. This area often appears almost white in a drawing. Surrounding the highlight is the midtone, which represents the middle range of value where the surface begins turning away from the light. As the surface turns further away, the value becomes darker and forms the shadow area, where less light reaches the object.

Objects also create cast shadows on the surfaces beneath them. These shadows show where the object blocks light from reaching the surrounding space. Cast shadows often appear darkest closest to the object and gradually soften as they extend outward.

Artists rely on careful observation to identify these value areas. Instead of guessing where shading should go, they look closely at the object and record what they actually see.

Shading with Intention

Effective shading develops slowly and intentionally. Artists rarely create dark tones immediately. Instead, they begin with light layers and gradually build darker values as needed.

Good shading often appears smooth and gradual. Sudden jumps from light to dark can make a drawing appear harsh unless the contrast is intentional. By layering pencil marks slowly and adjusting pressure, artists create subtle transitions that describe the turning surface of an object.

Observation plays an important role in shading. Instead of inventing shadows, artists continually compare the drawing with the object they are observing. This comparison helps them determine where values should become darker or lighter.

Building shading gradually allows artists to adjust their values as the drawing develops.

Art History and Context

During the Renaissance, artists began studying the natural world with much greater attention. Instead of copying earlier artwork or following strict traditional patterns, artists started observing real people, animals, and objects closely.

They studied human anatomy to understand how bodies were structured. They explored proportion to learn how different parts of the body related to one another. They examined how light created shadows and depth, and they developed mathematical systems of perspective to create the illusion of space.

This period of careful study transformed drawing and painting. Artists discovered that strong observational skills allowed them to represent the world more convincingly and communicate ideas more clearly.

Today, artists still rely on these same observational skills. Even highly stylized or abstract art often begins with careful study of real objects and forms.

Artists Who Use Observation

Carl Beam

Carl Beam was an Indigenous Canadian artist known for combining realistic imagery with symbolism and collage. His work often includes carefully observed figures and objects layered with photographs, text, and cultural imagery.

Beam’s work shows that observational drawing can be combined with deeper meaning. Realistic elements provide structure and clarity, while symbolic imagery communicates ideas about identity, history, and culture.

For students learning observational drawing, Beam’s work demonstrates how careful observation can become the foundation for more complex artistic ideas.

Alex Colville

Alex Colville was known for his extremely precise paintings and drawings. He carefully measured proportions and planned the placement of every object in his compositions.

Colville often created detailed preparatory drawings before beginning a painting. These drawings helped him ensure that the proportions and structure of the image were accurate.

His work demonstrates how slow, careful observation leads to clarity and control in an artwork.

Leonardo da Vinci

Leonardo da Vinci is one of the most famous artists of the Renaissance. He filled thousands of sketchbook pages with observational studies of anatomy, movement, plants, machines, and light.

Rather than focusing only on finished paintings, Leonardo spent much of his time sketching and studying how things worked. His drawings show careful observation of structure and form.

Leonardo reminds us that even master artists relied on practice sketches and continuous learning.
