Lesson 1: Personal Vision & Artistic Identity


Art 30 begins with you. Before mastering techniques or materials, strong artists understand what they care about, what they notice, and why they make images.

You do not need to “get it right” the first time. You do need to explore honestly and document your thinking.

In this lesson, you will:

Begin identifying personal themes, symbols, and interests

Explore symbolic drawing rather than literal illustration

Practice using your sketchbook as a thinking tool, not a finished-art book

Learn how professional artists use personal experience to shape their work

This lesson sets the foundation for your entire Art 30 portfolio.

Technique Focus 

1. Visual Brainstorming 
Visual brainstorming means drawing ideas instead of writing them. 

Visual brainstorming is a way of thinking through drawing.
Instead of trying to figure everything out in your head or writing full sentences, you use quick sketches, symbols, arrows, colour, and short words to explore ideas visually.

It’s messy on purpose. It’s not about making “good art” — it’s about getting ideas out.

 

You might see:

 

  • Small, rough drawings
  • Repeated images with changes
  • Words mixed with images
  • Crossed-out ideas
  • Arrows showing connections
  • Notes like “try darker” or “too boring”

 

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9qkxerXJ4Wk

 

The goals of visual brainstorming

1. Generate lots of ideas The goal isn’t to find the idea right away — it’s to find many possibilities. More ideas = more chances to find something interesting.

2. Explore before committing Visual brainstorming lets you test ideas without pressure. You can try something weird, dramatic, subtle, symbolic — and see what works.

3. Make thinking visible Your teacher (and you) can see your decision-making, not just the final result.

4. Take creative risks safely Because sketches are quick and unfinished, you’re more likely to: experiment, change direction and push ideas further.

Brainstorming helps you move past the obvious. It improves your final artwork by allowing you to explore composition, mood, symbols and materials, making your final piece stronger, clearer, and more intentional.

It shows growth and effort

Art isn’t just about talent — it’s about process.
Visual brainstorming proves you: thought deeply, tried alternatives and made choices on purpose

 

Better thinking → better choices → better art.

2. Symbolic Drawing 

 

Symbolic drawing is when you use images to represent ideas, emotions, or concepts, instead of drawing things only the way they literally look.

 

You’re not just drawing what you see — you’re drawing what something means.

 

For example:

 

  • A broken chain might represent freedom
  • A wilted flower might represent loss or time
  • An eye could represent awareness, fear, or identity

 

In symbolic drawing, the image stands for something bigger than itself.

 

www.youtube.com/watch?v=ep9-An5DTiU 

The goals of symbolic drawing

 

1. Communicate ideas visually. Symbolic drawing helps you say something without words.  The goal is to make viewers think, not just look.

 

2. Add meaning to your artwork. Instead of “a drawing of a hand,” it becomes: control, connection, identity and power.  The goal is depth, not decoration.

 

3. Develop personal voice.  Symbols are often personal. Two students might use the same object, but mean completely different things.

 

4. Practice visual problem-solving

 

Examples:

A locked door → fear, boundaries, privacy

A broken mirror → identity, self-image, change

A road → choices, direction, uncertainty

Symbols do not need to be universal. Personal symbols are encouraged.

Art History 

In earlier periods of art history, artists were often expected to:

Serve religious institutions

Document historical events

Follow strict rules of realism or tradition

In the 20th century, artists increasingly focused on:

Personal experience

Identity

Emotion and psychology

Cultural perspective

Originality and individual voice became essential.

In Art 30, you are expected to think like a contemporary artist:

What do I have to say, and how do I choose to say it visually?

 

Artists Focus

In this lesson, you will study three artists whose work is strongly connected to personal vision, identity, and symbolic meaning. You are not copying their work — you are learning how artists turn inner experience into visual form.

 

Alex Colville 

www.youtube.com/watch?v=qxz3p9_LgfM 

Alex Colville was a Canadian painter known for his highly controlled, realistic style. At first glance, his paintings often appear calm, ordinary, or even quiet. However, many viewers experience a strong sense of psychological tension, unease, or mystery in his work.

Colville believed that art should come from careful observation combined with personal experience. He often painted scenes from everyday life — people standing, walking, waiting — but arranged them deliberately to suggest deeper emotional meaning.

Key Characteristics of Colville’s Work:

Precise, controlled realism

Strong composition and structure

Subtle emotional or psychological tension

Ordinary moments that feel unsettling or symbolic

How This Connects to Your Work:
Colville shows that personal vision does not require dramatic imagery. Meaning can be created through composition, mood, and point of view. Even realistic drawings can communicate inner thoughts and feelings when choices are intentional.

 

Norval Morrisseau 

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2k-qZ255iOQ 

Norval Morrisseau was an Anishinaabe artist and a founder of what is now called the Woodland School of Art. His work is deeply rooted in Anishinaabe spiritual beliefs, oral storytelling, and cultural knowledge.

Morrisseau used bold black outlines, simplified forms, and symbolic imagery to communicate stories about identity, spirituality, and connection to the natural and spirit worlds. His art was not meant to be realistic; instead, it was meant to teach, remember, and communicate meaning.

Key Characteristics of Morrisseau’s Work:

Thick black contour lines

Bright, flat colour

X-ray or interior views of people and animals

Strong use of cultural and spiritual symbols

How This Connects to Your Work:
Morrisseau’s work demonstrates how symbolism can replace realism. Your symbols do not need to look realistic to be powerful. What matters is that they communicate meaning that is personal, cultural, or experiential.

 

Frida Kahlo

www.youtube.com/watch?v=B9XYtPqWLB4 

Frida Kahlo was a Mexican artist known for her deeply personal paintings, especially self-portraits. She used her own image, body, and life experiences to explore themes of identity, pain, resilience, culture, and belonging.

Rather than hiding personal struggle, Kahlo placed it at the center of her work. She often included symbolic objects, animals, clothing, and environments to represent emotional and physical experiences that could not be shown literally.

Key Characteristics of Kahlo’s Work:

Strong use of symbolism

Self-portraiture as storytelling

Personal and emotional subject matter

Cultural identity expressed visually

How This Connects to Your Work:
Kahlo’s work shows that your own experiences are valid sources for art. You do not need to explain everything directly — symbols can carry meaning for you, even if viewers interpret them differently.

 

Sketchbook Assignment

Step 1 — Create a Visual Brainstorm Page

Fill at least one full page with:

  • Words
  • Symbols
  • Small sketches
  • Arrows and connections

Do not worry about neatness.

Prompt ideas:

  • Things I think about often
  • Things that matter to me
  • Things I avoid or fear
  • Things that define my identity

Step 2 — Symbol Exploration (2–3 Pages)

Choose 3 ideas from your brainstorm and create:

  • 2–3 symbolic sketches per idea
  • Try different symbols for the same idea

Ask yourself:

  • Is this symbol too obvious?
  • Could I make it more personal?

Step 3 — Artist Influence Sketches

Create one sketch inspired by each artist studied.

This does NOT mean copying.

Instead, borrow:

  • Line style
  • Symbolic approach
  • Mood or emotional tone

Label each sketch with:

  • Artist influence
  • What you borrowed
  • What you changed

Step 4 — Written Reflection (½ Page)

Answer in point form or short paragraphs 2 of the following:

  • What themes or ideas keep reappearing?
  • Which symbols feel meaningful to you?
  • Which artist’s approach felt most natural?
  • What surprised you about this process?

What Strong Sketchbook Work Looks Like

✔ Messy but thoughtful
✔ Multiple ideas explored

✔ Notes beside drawings

✔ Visible decision-making

✔ Willingness to abandon weak ideas

✘ One perfect drawing only

✘ No written thinking

✘ Rushing to finished images

 

Common Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

Mistake: Trying to make “good art” too early
Fix: Focus on thinking, not polish

Mistake: Copying symbols from the internet
Fix: Personal meaning matters more than appearance

Mistake: Skipping reflection
Fix: Reflection is part of the artwork

Checklist — Before You Move On

☐ Visual brainstorm page complete
☐ Symbol exploration sketches (minimum 6)
☐ Artist-influenced sketches (3)
☐ Written reflection completed
☐ Sketchbook shows risk-taking and exploration
