<p><p><strong>Aim</strong>: Sherif (1935) conducted an experiment with the aim of demonstrating that people conform to group norms when they are put in an ambiguous (i.e. unclear) situation.<br></p><strong>Method</strong>: Sherif used a lab experiment to study conformity.� He used the autokinetic effect   this is where a small spot of light (projected onto a screen) in a dark room will appear to move, even though it is still (i.e. it is a visual illusion).</p>
<div id="cm-001" style="text-align: center;"><strong><img src="http://www.s-cool.co.uk/assets/learn_its/alevel/psychology/social-influence/conformity-key-studies/a-psy-socinf-dia04.gif" width="416" height="214"></strong></div>
<div><strong>Sherif conducted two versions of this experiment:</strong></div>
<ol>
<li>Individuals were asked to estimate how far they thought the light moved, then tested them together in a group. Estimates in the group converged as they established a�<strong>'group norm'</strong>. This was close to the average of estimates they gave individually.</li>
<li>A group was asked to give estimates of how far they thought the light moved then they were asked to give individual estimates, these were very close to the group estimate.</li>
</ol>
<p>Sherif claimed that he had shown conformity. The individuals were experiencing informational social influence.</p>
<p><em class="red"><strong>Criticism of Sherif's work:</strong></em></p>
<p>The 'group' used consisted of three people. They may not have considered themselves to be a group.</p>
<p>There was no right or wrong answer, it was an ambiguous task, and Sherif told them that he was going to move the light, so they were more likely to change their minds anyway.</p>

<h4><a id="aschs-visual-judgement-experiment-1951"></a>Asch's Visual judgement experiment (1951)</h4>
</div>
<p>Solomon Asch thought he would improve on Sherif's work and really demonstrate conformity. He showed just how easy it is to influence someone into saying something blatantly�<strong>wrong!</strong></p>
<p><strong>Asch showed a group of people a series of cards similar to the ones below.</strong></p>
<p style="text-align: center;"><strong><img src="http://www.s-cool.co.uk/assets/learn_its/alevel/psychology/social-influence/conformity-key-studies/a-psy-socinf-dia05.gif" width="410" height="276"></strong></p>
<div id="cm-002">Each test had only one innocent subject, the others in each group were�<strong>stooges</strong>. The correct answer was usually very obvious. When he gave the stooges a secret signal they all gave a predetermined�<strong>incorrect</strong>�answer.�</div><div id="cm-002"><br></div>
<p><em class="red"><strong>The results:</strong></em></p>
<p>74% of the innocent participants went along with the group and conformed, giving the�<strong>incorrect</strong>�answer at least once.</p>
<p><strong>If you count all the trials carried out, 34% showed conformity.</strong></p>
<p>Asch went on to test out different conditions on levels of conformity using the same set up. His method became known as�<strong>the Asch paradigm</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Asch concluded that various factors can affect the level of conformity:</strong></p>
<ol>
<li>Three 'stooges' produced maximum levels of conformity. With very large numbers, conformity levels drop dramatically (perhaps the game is then too obvious!).</li>
<li>Just one stooge not going along is enough to dramatically reduce conformity levels.</li>
<li>Difficult tasks tend to lead to more conformity.</li>
<li>Ambiguous tasks tend to lead to more conformity as people may feel less certain of their own ideas.</li>
<li>Men may try to appear more independent because of social expectations.</li>
<li>Women tend to show more conformity than men do when their answers are said out loud, publicly.</li>
<li>Low self-esteem may lead to higher conformity. This reflects low self-confidence or strong need for approval of others.</li>
<li>Conformity rates are higher when people are attracted to other members of the group.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Have you ever agreed with someone just because you fancy them?</strong></p>
<p><em class="red"><strong>Criticism of Asch's work:</strong></em></p>
<p>Some critics thought the high levels of conformity found by Asch were a reflection of American, 1950's culture:</p>
<p>'It was time-consuming and uneconomical'. (Crutchfield)</p>
<p>'Tasks set not like real-life situations'. (Crutchfield)</p>
<p>'It did not account for minority'. influences</p>

<h4><a id="zimbardos-prison-simulation-experiment-1971"></a>Zimbardo's Prison simulation experiment (1971)</h4>
<p style="text-align: center;"><img src="..\image_chapter_15845.jpeg" width="327" height="154"></p>
</div>
<p>This experiment was designed to show conformity to social roles, this is an an example of�<strong>normative influence</strong>. Volunteers were given authority and asked to act as guards over other volunteers who were prisoners.</p>
<p><em class="red"><strong>Aim:</strong></em>�to see the psychological effects of making 'normal', 'good' people into prisoners or guards.</p>
<p><em class="red"><strong>Volunteers:</strong></em>�24 middle class, male college students, mentally sound in tests and no criminal records, were paid $15 per day and divided into prisoners or guards by the flip of a coin.</p>
<p><em class="red"><strong>Procedure:</strong></em>�Prisoners were arrested at their homes at the start of the study, blindfolded and taken to Stanford University Psychology Department basement, which had been converted into a realistic prison! From then on the volunteers were treated as prisoners by the other volunteers who were guards.</p>
<p><em class="red"><strong>End of the study:</strong></em>�The planned two-week study was stopped early after six days because the guards became sadistic and the prisoners became extremely stressed. Several "prisoners" left mid-experiment due to extreme psychological distress.</p><ul>
