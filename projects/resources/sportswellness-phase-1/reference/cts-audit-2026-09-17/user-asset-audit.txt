## Updated verdict: the course still needs fixes before independent student release

With the assets supplied, I can be more specific: **the problem is no longer simply that I could not see the images. Several of the actual assets need correction, and the simulator gives incorrect instructional feedback in a reproducible situation.**

The biggest issue is **consistency across formats**. Your written lessons often make careful distinctions, but some diagrams, PDF explanations and game messages revert to stronger, more absolute claims. For example, the lesson says process goals are *usually more directly controllable*, while the goal graphic says “100% controllable.” The lesson rejects a universal activation midpoint, while the reading’s answer key describes a “shared moderate optimum.” Those differences matter when students are using these resources to figure out the correct answer.    

**Keep the main course design and most of the teaching. Do not add more content yet. Fix the contradictions, readability and completion workflow.**

### The activation continuum is genuinely incomplete

The file `arousal-continuum.svg` exists, but it references another file:

`phase1-arousal-continuum.png`

That PNG is **not in the archive**. The result is a dark panel without the teaching diagram.

This is an important packaging lesson: checking that the HTML can find an SVG is not enough. You also need to check whether that SVG depends on another file. **Make this graphic self-contained.**

### The four-model comparison is worth preserving

This figure distinguishes arousal from the state-anxiety scale used for IZOF, includes the dominant-response qualification for Drive Theory, and states the cognitive-anxiety condition beside the Catastrophe graph. It also explicitly says these are schematic predictions, not personal measurements. That is the direction I would use for the other diagrams. 

My criticism is its **display size**, not its underlying content. A student should not have to decipher four tiny graphs before understanding the first one.

### The goal ladder needs a precise correction

The graphic labels process goals as:

> “Mechanics and form — 100% controllable.” 

Your written lesson is better: it describes process goals as actions that are **usually more directly controllable than the final result**. Use that wording consistently. 

For teaching, I would also give each level an example from the same situation:

**Outcome:** win the volleyball match.
**Performance:** achieve a target serving percentage.
**Process:** use the practised preparation routine before each serve.

That lets students distinguish the categories through a concrete example rather than memorize three labels.

### The learning map overstates what interpretation can do

Its perception-of-control box says:

> “Challenge vs. threat framing changes the outcome.” 

That is stronger than the opening lesson, which correctly says the player may remain nervous and the result is not guaranteed. 

A better replacement is:

> **“How the performer interprets the situation can influence their attention and next action.”**

Also, arrows from stress and anxiety directly into **Ideal Performance State** can be read as though those inputs produce the ideal state. A centre labelled **Performance state** would make this a clearer map of influences.

### The toolkit needs less “elite operator” language

“Elite Operator Toolkit,” “reset the stress loop,” “sends a safety signal” and “process goals reduce anxiety” make the graphic sound more certain and technical than the surrounding lesson. 

For this course, I would call it **Performance Regulation Tools** and organize it around the question students actually need to answer:

> **What do I notice, what adjustment fits, and what will I do next?**

That also fits the breadth of REC1050 better than an exclusively elite-athlete framing: the official course description includes sport, artistic and academic performance. ([Learn Alberta Curriculum][1])

## 2. The simulator should not be released in its current form

**This is the most consequential new finding.**

### It can tell students the wrong reason they failed

I started the simulator and deliberately left the pointer stationary. The round ended after approximately **3.4 seconds** because the cursor did not track the moving target.

At the end, the game displayed **60% activation** and still labelled that state **“Ideal Performance State.”** Nevertheless, its teaching feedback said the learner’s activation had drifted outside the ideal state and caused execution to break down. The screenshot shows those statements together. 

**That is not a minor wording issue. The explanation contradicts the simulation’s own state.**

A correct message for that particular result would be:

> “This round ended because the cursor did not stay near the target. Your activation remained inside this game’s practice band. The tracking result does not tell us whether you regulated a real performance situation well.”

The game needs separate feedback for **tracking loss**, **the upper activation limit** and **the lower activation limit**.

### It names a model that its mechanics do not fully represent

The game’s high-activation ending is labelled **“Catastrophe Phenomenon (Panic / Cognitive Overload).”** However, the inspected application logic triggers that ending from the activation variable reaching its upper limit; it does not separately model cognitive anxiety.

Your written lesson explicitly teaches that the Catastrophe model concerns the combination of **high cognitive anxiety and high physiological arousal**. 

**Choose one of two approaches:** make the game represent those separate conditions, or stop presenting its activation-limit ending as a demonstration of that model. For Grade 10, I would choose the simpler approach: describe it as a **fictional activation-and-attention exercise**.

### The game currently rewards survival more clearly than understanding

The main end screen emphasizes **Regulation Failure**, **Simulation Terminated**, survival time and score. Meanwhile, the course’s actual learning goal is to justify a response from the signs in a situation. Those are different tasks.

My recommendation is to make the **guided scenario lab the default practice**. Retain the target-tracking game only as an optional activity after its feedback is repaired.

A stronger required activity would use short rounds where students identify the signs, choose a response, explain their choice and interpret what happened. The moving target can add interest, but it should not become the main obstacle to demonstrating understanding.

### Navigation and evidence handling also need work

In the component test, a round continued and ended after I navigated to another course topic. After an ended round, the parent course’s game-attempt record was still empty. The separately typed debrief is saved, but that is not the same as collecting the game attempt itself.

Add explicit **pause/resume and stop-on-topic-exit behaviour**, and decide exactly what game evidence should appear in My Work. Do not imply that activity records are collected unless the integration actually records them.

## 3. The PDFs are not ready to serve as equivalent student explanations

Both PDFs are linked in Resources as ways to revisit the lesson. That makes their content part of the student learning experience—not just background material for you. 

I found several specific reasons to reconcile them before release.

**The reading’s answer key retains an older Inverted-U explanation.** Page 12 says it assumes a shared moderate optimum. Your revised lesson deliberately warns students not to treat the drawing as one fixed midpoint for everybody. A student using the PDF to check their answer receives a different explanation.  

**Slide 10 contains a visible graph-axis error.** The state-anxiety scale reads **30, 40, 40, 50, 60**. The duplicated 40 needs correction. I would also question whether this schematic needs numerical ticks at all when the lesson is not teaching a defined measurement scale. 

**Slide 14 makes breathing sound like a literal override mechanism.** It uses “the only physiological function,” “manual override switch” and a direct safety signal that intercepts the stress response. That needs reconciliation with the lesson’s more restrained explanation of a comfortable routine followed by a task cue.  

**My release recommendation: temporarily remove these PDFs from the student Resources page, or revise them against the approved lesson.** Keep the originals in your development materials. Do not solve this by telling students that the sources sometimes disagree and expecting them to sort it out.

## 4. Styling: keep the theme, fix the reading widths

The main school-branded layout does not need another redesign. However, **fitting on the screen is not the same as being readable**.

In the rendering tests, the opening infographic displayed at approximately **495 pixels wide on a desktop layout** and **334 pixels wide on a phone layout**. Its source image is 1440 pixels wide, with substantial explanatory text inside it. That leaves much of the figure’s text noticeably smaller than the lesson text beside it.

The four-model diagram has a similar problem. On the phone-sized view, selecting **Enlarge** initially produced a roughly **344-pixel-wide** fit-to-screen image. Students then have to select **Actual size** and scroll around it to read the details.

**Zoom should help with details; it should not be required to read the main explanation.**

The tablet-sized layout was worse in one important respect. At **768 pixels wide**, the desktop sidebar remained visible, and accumulated margins reduced the toolkit figure to approximately **162 pixels wide**. The page technically fit, but the figure was not a useful normal-size reading resource.

I would change the sidebar breakpoint, remove repeated margins around nested figures, and place text-heavy diagrams in wider or stacked layouts. Where possible, keep explanatory sentences as live webpage text and let the image show the relationship.

### The phone simulator needs a separate layout pass

The mobile game stacks its meters, controls and playfield vertically. Its introduction is inside a fixed-height, clipped playfield, and the normal full-document rendering cuts off parts of the instructions and the start button.

An automated click could reach the start button by scrolling containers, so I am **not** claiming it is impossible to start. I am saying the ordinary touch-facing presentation is not clear or comfortable enough for release.

Move the introduction outside the clipped arena, keep Start visible, and keep the regulation controls close to the playfield.

## 5. What this changes—and what it does not

**The new upload resolves most of the earlier missing-file uncertainty.** The main HTML’s direct image, logo, PDF and game references now have matching files. The continuum’s missing nested image is the remaining confirmed asset dependency problem.

**The earlier workflow findings remain.** In this pass, I reconfirmed that a response could exist in the course state while My Work remained blank after normal navigation. Supplying assets does not repair the main HTML’s saving, collection, checkpoint-reset or completion logic.

**The video audit is unchanged.** This archive does not supply a replacement video collection. My earlier recommendations about curating the overlapping selections, avoiding unnecessarily adult-facing support material and checking playback in the real student environment still apply. I have not newly certified every external video’s playback or captions.

## My priority order

1. **Fix the blank continuum and incorrect simulator feedback.** Withhold the unreconciled PDFs from the student resource route.
2. **Repair the work-saving, My Work, checkpoint and submission workflow** identified in the first audit.
3. **Revise the five inconsistent figures and improve the presentation of the three stronger ones.**
4. **Fix tablet and phone layouts**, make guided practice the default, then pilot in Brightspace with student accounts.

**The course is closer in its written teaching than in its supporting media and software behaviour.** The next improvement should be one consistent, dependable learning experience—not more diagrams, another visual theme or a more elaborate game.
