Phase 1: The Engine

Understand pressure, regulate your performance state and build a plan you can use in sport, school and life.

ReadReflect

You have practised the skill. You know what to do. Then the moment arrives—and it feels different.

Imagine a hockey player waiting to take a penalty shot. The rink feels loud. The player notices a fast heartbeat and a tight grip. One thought appears: “Everyone is counting on me.” The skill has not suddenly disappeared, but the situation now feels more important.

Now imagine the player thinking, “This matters. I have practised my approach. I can choose a target and commit to the shot.” The player may still feel nervous. What changes is where their attention goes and what they do next.

This phase is about understanding that difference. You will learn to name what is happening, choose a response and check whether that response helps the task.

By the end of this phase, I can:

  • I can distinguish stress, arousal and anxiety in a performance situation.
  • I can explain how pressure can change attention, muscle tension and performance.
  • I can compare major arousal-performance models without treating one model as a rule for every athlete.
  • I can choose and explain regulation tools that fit the athlete, task and situation.
  • I can build a Regulation Engine that gives me a practical plan before, during and after pressure.

What are we actually trying to improve?

The goal is not to feel calm all the time. A sprinter needs energy at the start. A dancer needs to notice the music and the next count. A basketball player needs enough energy to move, but not so much tension that a familiar free throw becomes rushed.

Your aim is to find a useful level of activation—how switched on you feel—and direct your attention toward what the task needs. Sometimes that means settling your energy. Sometimes it means raising it. Sometimes the best choice is to keep your routine steady.

Mental fitness is a skill, not a personality test

In this course, mental fitness means using skills to manage your thoughts, feelings, attention and body responses when you face a demand. The word psychological refers to your mental experience. Physiological refers to what is happening in your body.

A useful comparison is learning a physical skill. First you notice what you are doing. Then you practise one change. Finally you review it. A mental skill deserves that same practice; it is not a last-minute instruction to “be tougher.”

You do not need to play on a team to use the ideas. A school presentation, musical performance, recreational game or new fitness skill can provide a suitable example. You can use a fictional performer throughout.

What does “in the zone” mean?

This is called the Ideal Performance State (IPS). An athlete is deeply involved in the task, execution feels smooth rather than forced, and attention stays with useful information. Smooth does not mean easy: the athlete may still be working very hard.

You cannot demand this feeling on command. Instead, practise routines that help you prepare and respond. A strong plan gives you something useful to do even on a day when you do not feel at your best.

Where motivation fits

Intrinsic motivation comes from the activity itself, such as enjoying movement or learning a skill. Extrinsic motivation involves outcomes outside the activity, such as a medal or recognition. These help explain why you participate. Here, we focus on how you respond while participating. Phase 2, The Drive, develops motivation further.

Arousal, stress & anxiety

Three related terms. Three different parts of the performance picture.

Learning goal

I can distinguish stress, arousal and anxiety and identify each one in a sport example.

Before you begin

Begin with a situation you can picture. Focus on what the athlete faces, what the body does and what the athlete thinks or feels.

ReadPractice

A fast heartbeat does not tell you the whole story. One swimmer might feel excited and ready. Another might be worried about a false start. To explain the difference, separate the demand, the activation and the athlete’s experience.

Arousal: how switched on are you?

Arousal is your level of physical and mental activation. It can range from sleepy and unengaged to highly alert and energized. It is not automatically good or bad. The useful level depends on the person and the task.

A swimmer who feels alert and notices a quicker heartbeat is describing activation. That description alone does not tell us that the swimmer is anxious. Ask what they are thinking and how they experience those sensations.

Anxiety: worry and the way the body feels

Anxiety involves nervousness, worry or apprehension. Apprehension means concern about what might happen. The course separates anxiety into a thought component and a body component.

Cognitive anxiety

Cognitive means thinking. “I will let my team down” is an example of worry. So are self-doubt and repeatedly imagining a mistake.

Somatic anxiety

Somatic means bodily. An athlete might describe butterflies, a tight chest or shaky hands as part of feeling nervous. The athlete’s experience matters.

Stressor and stress are not the same thing

A stressor is the demand or situation: a selection trial, a difficult opponent, a deadline or a judged routine. Stress, as used in this course, involves a meaningful gap between the demand and the resources you believe you have to meet it.

Those resources might include practice, a clear role, useful information, time or support. A task can feel more stressful when it matters to you and you do not believe you have enough resources to handle it.

Worked example · fictional scenario

One tryout, four descriptions

A student is asked to demonstrate a new volleyball skill in front of the coach.

Stressor
The public skill demonstration.
Stress appraisal
“This is important, but I do not feel prepared for this skill.”
Arousal
The student feels more alert and notices faster breathing.
Anxiety
The student worries about looking foolish and describes nervous butterflies.

The same event can involve all four. Naming the parts helps you choose a better response than simply saying, “They are stressed.”

What might you notice in the body?

During a pressure response, the heart may beat faster, breathing may speed up and muscles may feel tense. An athlete may also notice sweating or an unsettled stomach. These changes can happen before a performance has even started.

Think of these as information to investigate. “My jaw is tight and my routine is rushed” is more useful than “My body is failing.” The first description identifies something the athlete can notice and respond to.

These signals have other possible causes too. A fast heartbeat after warming up is not proof of anxiety. A photograph cannot tell you how someone feels. Ask about the task, the person’s thoughts and what has changed from their usual pattern.

Further reading: American Psychological Association: stress and the body ↗

State or trait: now, or a pattern over time?

State anxiety describes anxiety in the current moment. It can change between a warm-up, a first attempt and the end of an event.

Trait anxiety describes a more lasting tendency to interpret situations as threatening. It is about a pattern, not one nervous afternoon. You cannot identify a teammate’s trait anxiety just by watching one performance.

For example, “I feel nervous before today’s routine” describes a state. A repeated tendency across many situations is different evidence. Neither statement, by itself, is a diagnosis.

How pressure becomes a loop

The demand matters. So does the meaning you give it.

Learning goal

I can trace how a demand is interpreted and how that interpretation can influence body responses and performance.

Before you begin

You should be able to tell stress, arousal and anxiety apart before following the full process.

ReadTry itPractice

Imagine a basketball player taking two free throws with little time left. “Two free throws” is the situation. “I have practised this” and “I must not miss” are two ways of interpreting it. The four-stage stress process helps separate those parts.

1. Environmental demand

State what the performer is being asked to do. A penalty kick, a routine or a presentation is a demand. Describe the facts first: “I am serving at 24–24” is more precise than “Everything is going wrong.”

2. Perception of demand

The athlete considers what the task means and whether they have the resources to meet it. This is called appraisal. Does the task feel like a manageable challenge, a threat, or a mixture of both?

Think of appraisal as the pivot point. The situation may stay the same while the meaning changes. “One mistake proves I am useless” and “One attempt gives me information” can lead attention in different directions.

3. Stress response

Thoughts, feelings, activation, muscle tension and attention may change. An athlete might become energized and focused, or worried and rushed. These are possible responses—not inevitable outcomes of an event.

4. Behavioural consequences

The athlete acts. They may use their usual routine, rush it, hesitate or change their technique. A result follows. That experience can affect the way the next demand is interpreted.

Why call it a loop?

Suppose a player misses, decides that the miss proves they cannot handle pressure, and rushes the next attempt. The first result has become part of the next appraisal. The player is responding to both the new task and the meaning attached to the earlier result.

A different loop is possible. The player notices the miss, identifies a rushed setup, uses a cue and returns to their routine. The next shot might still miss. However, the player now has a specific action to review instead of a judgement about their whole ability.

Worked example · fictional scenario

Where would you intervene?

A cheer athlete loses a count and thinks, “Now I will ruin the whole routine.” Their shoulders tighten. They stare at the audience instead of picking up the next agreed cue.

Name the parts: the lost count is an event; the prediction about ruining the routine is an appraisal; tension is a response; looking away from useful cues is a behaviour.

A possible response: return to the next rehearsed cue agreed with the coach. The athlete does not need to solve the entire routine in one thought. They need a safe, familiar next action.

Remember: Changing an appraisal does not mean pretending a real problem is harmless. An unclear role may need a question. An unsafe demand needs support or a change to the task.

Finding your performance zone

Why “more energy” is not always the right answer.

Learning goal

I can compare Drive Theory, the Inverted-U and IZOF and explain what each model predicts.

Before you begin

Remember that arousal is activation. A model is a way to organize evidence—not a guarantee of what every athlete will do.

ReadPractice

“Get more fired up” and “calm down” cannot both be right for every athlete in every moment. The arousal models give us different ways to think about the relationship between activation and performance.

A model is a simplified explanation. It highlights one relationship while leaving out other details. Use a model to explain a possible pattern—not to predict someone’s exact score.

Start by reading the axes

On the Drive and Inverted-U graphs, the horizontal axis shows increasing arousal. The vertical axis shows better performance. A line that rises means the model predicts improvement. A line that falls means it predicts a decline. The diagrams do not assign you a personal heart rate or target number.

Drive Theory: which response becomes stronger?

A simplified Drive Theory diagram uses a rising line: more arousal predicts better performance. To use that idea carefully, add the idea of a dominant response—the response that is most likely to occur, often a well-practised habit.

In the drive account, increased arousal strengthens that response. If the habit is useful, performance may improve. If the habit is an error, a stronger response is not necessarily a better one. That is why “more energy always helps” is too simple.

Worked example · fictional scenario

A stronger habit is not always a better habit

An experienced player has a reliable shooting routine. A learner is still practising a new release and often pushes the ball too hard. The drive account asks which response is strongest for each player. More activation does not teach the learner a missing skill.

Limit: skill, attention and worry also matter. A straight line cannot explain every high-pressure performance.

Further reading: Platania and Moran: dominant responses in the drive account ↗

Inverted-U: when does more stop helping?

The Inverted-U Hypothesis predicts that performance improves as arousal rises toward a useful level, then declines as arousal goes beyond it. The graph looks like an upside-down U. Unlike the straight-line version of Drive Theory, it predicts that more activation can eventually stop helping.

A player who is barely engaged may react late. As they become more alert, they may notice the play sooner. If activation rises further and their movement becomes rushed and tense, execution may get worse. This illustrates the predicted rise and fall.

Do not turn the drawing into a universal midpoint. A useful range can differ with the person, skill and situation. The diagram teaches a pattern; it does not tell every athlete to aim for exactly the same level.

IZOF: what works for this person?

Individualized Zones of Optimal Functioning (IZOF) emphasizes an athlete’s personal best-performance range. The introductory version used here describes a range of state anxiety linked to their best performances.

One athlete may describe that range as settled and steady. Another may report more nervous energy and still perform well. The key is a personal range, not one ideal point shared by everyone.

Be accurate with the terms. Arousal means activation. State anxiety means anxiety right now. They are related, but they are not interchangeable labels.

Find evidence, not a permanent label

Compare several performances. What was the task? How did the athlete describe their energy and worry? What did they attend to? What did their movement look or feel like? A pattern across attempts is more useful than deciding, “I am a high-arousal athlete,” after one good day.

Keep the task specific. The same person may need a different preparation for a powerful start than for a precise aiming task. A routine is something to test and adjust, not an identity to defend.

The fourth graph, Catastrophe, asks a different question: could performance drop sharply rather than gradually? You will use it in the next topic.

When nerves help—or hurt

Worry, bodily activation and perceived control do not tell the same story.

Learning goal

I can explain how worry, body activation and perceived control can change how nerves affect performance.

Before you begin

Bring forward the idea that two athletes can have similar activation but interpret it differently.

ReadWatchPractice

Two athletes can feel equally activated and have different experiences. One says, “I feel ready.” Another says, “I cannot stop thinking about mistakes.” The intensity of a feeling is only part of the story. Its meaning matters too.

When a decline is sudden: the Catastrophe model

The Catastrophe model describes a possible sharp drop in performance when high cognitive anxiety combines with high physiological arousal. In plain language: a lot of worry and a lot of bodily activation occur together.

Compare that with the Inverted-U. Its decline is gradual. Catastrophe allows a sudden breakdown rather than just a small drop as activation rises. In the course, “catastrophe” is the name of a performance model. It is not a label for a person or a mental-health diagnosis.

Worked example · fictional scenario

Rushing one mistake into another

A basketball player is worried about being taken out of the game. After a mistake, they feel very activated, rush the next decision and suddenly struggle with passes that are usually reliable.

You could use the Catastrophe model to explain a possible sharp change because the scenario includes both high worry and high bodily activation. You could not prove that model just by seeing one bad pass. Other explanations would need consideration.

Perceived control: what resources can I use?

Jones’ model focuses on the athlete’s perception of control: whether they believe they have resources and actions that can help them meet the demand. Resources may include practice, a routine, a clear plan and support.

Facilitative anxiety is anxiety the athlete interprets as helpful or energizing. Debilitative anxiety is interpreted as harmful or disruptive. These terms describe the meaning and perceived effect of the nerves, not simply how fast the heart is beating.

“I feel nervous, but I have rehearsed my opening and know my first cue” connects the feeling to preparation. “Being nervous means I am going to fail” treats a feeling as a certain prediction. That prediction may pull attention away from the task.

A reframe must be believable

Reframing means considering a different, useful interpretation. It is not pretending you will definitely win. Try a statement that includes both the real demand and a resource: “This is difficult, and I can use the routine I practised.”

If you are unsure of a rule, ask. If you do not know the skill yet, seek help and practise it. A useful reframe points to a real action instead of covering up a gap in preparation.

Two more ways the source explains anxiety

Multidimensional Anxiety Theory

Multidimensional means considering more than one part. This theory separates cognitive anxiety from somatic anxiety. It proposes a negative relationship between cognitive anxiety and performance, and an Inverted-U relationship for somatic anxiety.

Support for this model is mixed, especially for the claim that worry always harms performance. Write “the theory proposes” rather than treating it as a rule for every athlete.

Reversal Theory

This theory emphasizes how the meaning of arousal can change. High activation can be experienced as exciting or unpleasant. “My body feels energized” and “These sensations are dangerous” are different experiences even when activation feels similarly strong.

The name does not mean that people can instantly reverse every feeling by choosing a thought. The teaching point is to consider interpretation as well as intensity.

Where pressure comes from

Separate features of the situation from personal factors.

Learning goal

I can identify situational and personal sources of pressure without treating every difficulty as the same problem.

Before you begin

Use examples you are comfortable discussing. A fictional athlete is always acceptable.

ReadPractice

Before choosing a tool, ask what is creating the pressure. Two students may both say, “I am stressed,” while facing very different problems. One does not understand the task. Another understands it but believes a mistake will lower their worth.

Situational sources: what is happening around you?

Event importance concerns how much the result matters. A final, an audition or a tryout may feel important because something is at stake. Importance can come from a selection decision, a personal goal or wanting to contribute to a team.

Uncertainty concerns what is unclear. An athlete might not know their role, the judging criteria or when they will perform. These sources can overlap, but they ask different questions: “How much does this matter?” and “What is going to happen?”

Personal factors: what meaning do you bring?

Trait anxiety is a more stable tendency to see threat. Self-esteem concerns your sense of personal worth. A performer who treats every result as a judgement of their worth may experience a mistake differently from someone who treats it as information about a skill.

Social physique anxiety is worry about how other people evaluate your body. A student may be comfortable learning a movement but uncomfortable being watched in a particular uniform. That is not the same as being unsure of the movement instructions.

These concepts help describe possible influences. They are not excuses to label a classmate. A fictional case can be used for every question in this topic.

Match the response to what can change

Start with the practical issue. Unclear instructions may call for a question. Too many commitments may call for a discussion about the schedule. A hurtful comment may require support from a trusted adult. A tension signal may be a reason to try a familiar regulation routine.

Some things are outside the athlete’s control, such as another competitor’s score. Other things can be influenced, such as preparation and asking for clarification. Learning to regulate yourself does not mean taking responsibility for every part of the environment.

What pressure changes

Two pathways to performance: coordination and attention.

Learning goal

I can explain how high or low activation can change attention, muscle tension and coordination.

Before you begin

Connect the athlete’s internal state to something observable in the actual skill.

ReadPractice

An athlete can know a skill and still find it hard to execute under pressure. Two useful questions are: What has changed in the movement? and What information is the athlete paying attention to?

Muscle tension and coordination

Coordination means organizing movements so that the parts work together. Extra tension can make a familiar movement feel stiff, rushed or less smooth. The athlete may start trying to control every small part instead of following a practised rhythm.

Consider a grappler who squeezes every grip as hard as possible, even while waiting for an opening. “Try harder” does not address the unnecessary tension. Noticing the grip, releasing what is not needed and returning to a familiar cue gives them a more specific response.

Attention: what gets noticed?

You cannot give equal attention to everything at once. Selective attention means choosing useful information and letting less useful information stay in the background.

A soccer player may need to notice the ball, nearby defenders and an available teammate. Crowd noise may not help the next pass. A free-throw shooter has a different task, so a narrower focus on a target and routine may be useful.

Broad, narrow or off target?

The diagram illustrates three possibilities. At low arousal, attention may drift toward unrelated information. In a useful state, the athlete notices what matters. At very high arousal, attention may become too narrow and miss an important cue.

This last pattern is often called tunnel vision. A player might stare at the opponent with the ball and miss a teammate moving into space. The problem is not simply “narrow focus.” It is missing information the task needs.

These are possible patterns, not a rule that attention always changes in exactly this way. Ask what the task requires before deciding whether the focus is useful.

Internal and external distractions

An internal distraction comes from your thoughts or sensations: replaying an error, worrying about a result or repeatedly checking how nervous you feel. An external distraction comes from around you: a spectator, a phone or an unrelated conversation.

Internal attention is not always bad, and external attention is not always good. Noticing shoulder tension during a pause may help a reset. Watching a spectator’s reaction during a play may not. The key question is whether the information helps the current task.

At the free-throw line, a player uses a familiar cue and looks at their target. That narrow focus fits the task.

During open play, the same player stares only at the basket and misses an unguarded teammate. Now a wider scan is needed. The best focus changed because the job changed.

Turning energy up or down

Choose the tool that fits the state—not the tool you always use.

Learning goal

I can choose a regulation strategy that matches what the athlete needs: settle, activate or maintain.

Before you begin

The goal is useful regulation, not automatic relaxation. First identify what is happening.

ReadWatchPractice

Regulation means making a deliberate adjustment to your state. Start by noticing the signs. Then ask: do I need to lower activation, raise it, or keep it steady and direct my attention?

A tool is useful when it fits a need. More relaxation is not automatically better. More energy is not automatically better. The point is to return to an action you can perform safely and deliberately.

01

Too tense or rushed?

Consider down-regulation: settle excess tension, slow a rushed routine and return to a task cue.

02

Flat or disengaged?

Check the cause. When appropriate, use familiar warm-up movement, rhythm or an effort cue.

03

Ready and focused?

Maintain your routine. You do not need to change your state just because a competition is starting.

A short reset has a beginning and an ending

The course’s sequence is notice → regulate → return to the task. A reset should not leave you endlessly checking your feelings. It should help you identify what needs changing, use one appropriate tool, then act.

“Take a breath” is incomplete advice unless the performer knows when to use it and what comes next. “When I notice tight shoulders before the serve, I will settle my breathing, release the extra tension, choose the target and use my cue” is a plan.

Explore a reset · choose a step

Notice. Adjust. Refocus. Act. Review.

1. Notice a specific signal

“My shoulders are tight and I am rushing my setup.” Describe the signal without judging your whole ability.

Tool 1 · Comfortable breathing and centering

Use it when: you notice a rushed rhythm or extra tension and there is a safe pause in the task. Purpose: give yourself a steady point of attention before returning to action.

Diaphragmatic breathing refers to breathing involving the diaphragm, a major breathing muscle. For this introductory practice, do not force a huge breath or try to hit an exact number. A comfortable, gentle rhythm is enough to explore the idea.

  1. Choose a safe, still position and notice your normal breath.
  2. Let the next few breaths feel gentle and unforced. Do not hold your breath.
  3. Notice whether your rhythm feels less rushed. No particular feeling is required.
  4. Return your eyes and attention to one useful task cue.

You can describe the steps in writing instead of practising them. Stop if you feel light-headed or uncomfortable, and return to normal breathing. Use any individual guidance you have been given. Do not practise a breathing exercise while swimming or doing an activity that requires your full attention.

A centering routine links that pause to a next action. The breath is one part of the routine, not a promise to switch stress off.

Tool 2 · Notice and release excess muscle tension

Use it when: the athlete notices unnecessary tightness, such as a clenched jaw or raised shoulders. Purpose: reduce tension that is not helping the task.

Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR) is the source’s term for systematically tensing and releasing muscle groups to notice the contrast. It is different from a quick in-performance reminder to let go of extra tension.

For a simple written reset, identify one area the athlete tends to tense. Plan how they would notice it during a safe pause and allow it to relax, then return to the skill. For example: “Between attempts, I will check my shoulders, let them settle and find my target.”

Do not force a stretch, tense an injured area or ignore pain. Formal PMR practice should follow appropriate guidance. You can explain its purpose without doing it physically.

Tool 3 · Raise activation when it is appropriate

Use it when: the athlete describes low engagement and their usual warm-up or preparation is still needed. Purpose: become ready for the task—not exhausted before it begins.

  1. Check why the athlete feels flat. Unclear instructions need clarification; pain, illness or significant fatigue should not be treated as a motivation failure.
  2. Choose familiar, appropriate preparation: teacher-approved warm-up movement, a rhythm or a brief motivational cue.
  3. Keep the adjustment proportionate. More is not always better.
  4. Check whether the athlete is now engaged with the task and able to use the next cue.

For example, a volleyball substitute may use their normal on-court preparation and the cue “ready feet.” A student giving a presentation may stand in a comfortable, upright position and rehearse the first sentence. The activity and setting determine the tool.

Music and imagery are two other possible preparation tools. Their use depends on the athlete and the setting. An imagined successful start may help one performer prepare; another may prefer a simple movement cue.

Tool 4 · Maintain a state that is already working

Use it when: the athlete is alert, coordinated and attending to useful cues. Purpose: avoid changing a helpful routine for no clear reason.

Stay with the preparation you know. Use a brief cue, commit to the next action and review at the next sensible break. “Do nothing” and “maintain” are not the same: maintaining involves continuing useful actions without chasing a different feeling.

Some nervousness may remain. The practical question is whether the athlete can perform the task, not whether every sensation has disappeared.

Cues and controllable goals

Give attention a job it can actually do.

Learning goal

I can create short cue words and controllable goals that direct attention to the next useful action.

Before you begin

A good cue is specific enough to guide the task and simple enough to use under pressure.

ReadPractice

Once you have adjusted your activation, your attention needs somewhere useful to go. A cue is a short word or phrase that reminds you of an action. A goal describes what you are working toward. Together they help answer, “What am I doing next?”

Instructional cues: guide the action

Instructional cue words point attention toward technique, timing or another useful feature of the skill. Examples include “smooth” and “follow through.” The cue must mean something specific to the athlete.

“Be better” gives little direction. “See the target” can give a performer a clear next action. A cue should connect to what the athlete has practised; it should not introduce a complicated new technical instruction during an important attempt.

Motivational cues: support effort

Motivational cue words support effort and commitment. Examples include “explode” and “dig deep”. A more everyday cue might be “keep moving” or “commit.”

Choose language that fits the person and situation. A phrase that energizes one athlete may make another feel rushed. The difference between instructional and motivational cues is their purpose, not how impressive they sound.

Worked example · fictional scenario

Make a cue usable

Too vague: “Do not mess up.” It names a feared result, not a useful action.

Too much at once: “Fix your shoulders, feet, head, follow-through and timing.” The performer now has several instructions competing for attention.

More usable: “Target, then smooth.” In this example, the player has already practised what those words mean. Their cue supports a familiar sequence.

Three kinds of goals

An outcome goal is a result, such as winning a match or making a team. Other people and conditions affect it, so you have less direct control.

A performance goal is a personal standard, such as a target time or shooting percentage. It provides useful feedback, but it can still be affected by conditions and normal variation.

A process goal describes the action used to perform: completing a setup, choosing a target or returning to a cue after an error. These are usually more directly controllable than the final result.

All three can matter. The point is not to stop caring about winning. During an attempt, direct attention toward the process that gives you a useful job.

Make the process goal SMART

SMART means Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. It turns a vague intention into a plan you can review.

Start with “I will manage pressure better.” What would that look like? Add the action, the setting, a way to check it and a review point.

A modelled goal · adapt the structure, not the answers

“During my next three volleyball practices, I will use my breath–target–cue routine before ten planned practice serves. I will record whether I used the routine and review the pattern after the third practice.”

Specific
The action is a breath–target–cue routine before serving.
Measurable
The athlete records whether the routine was used for each planned attempt.
Achievable
The athlete and teacher can adjust the number of attempts to suit the setting.
Relevant
The routine targets a rushed setup, the problem identified in the scenario.
Time-bound
There is a review point after three practices.

Track the action and the outcome separately

Using the routine does not guarantee an ace. A player can use the routine well and miss, or skip it and score. Record both what they did and what happened.

After several attempts, ask: was the routine usable? Did the athlete notice a change in attention or pace? What should be adjusted? Avoid claiming that one good result proves the routine caused it.

    Phase 1 checkpoint

    Ten questions on arousal, anxiety, appraisal, attention and regulation.

    1. An athlete describes a pre-competition state as neutral energy: a high heart rate and mental activation without worry or apprehension. Which concept is being described?

    Arousal describes activation and is not automatically anxiety. Anxiety includes worry, apprehension, or tension.

    2. Over several months and across different situations, an athlete often interprets demands as threatening. Which course term describes this relatively lasting tendency?

    Trait anxiety describes a relatively lasting tendency across situations. State anxiety describes anxiety in a particular moment. One nervous performance would not establish a lasting tendency.

    3. A golfer reports butterflies, sweating, and muscle tension immediately before a decisive putt. According to the model used in this course, these are indicators of:

    Somatic state anxiety refers to physical sensations experienced with anxiety in the moment; bodily signs alone still need context.

    4. In the four-stage stress process taught here, at which stage does an athlete interpret what a demand means and whether they have the resources to manage it?

    Stage 2 is appraisal. That interpretation can influence the response and next action; it does not guarantee a controlled response or successful performance.

    5. Which hypothesis predicts that performance rises toward a useful arousal level and then gradually declines as arousal continues to increase?

    The Inverted-U Hypothesis describes a curved relationship. Its diagram is a general model rather than one fixed target for every person and task.

    6. Across several performances, two athletes describe different ranges of state anxiety during their best performances. Which model emphasizes these individual best-performance ranges?

    IZOF emphasizes a personal range of state anxiety associated with good performance. State anxiety and arousal are related concepts, but they are not interchangeable.

    7. According to the Catastrophe Model, what is predicted when high cognitive state anxiety combines with physiological arousal beyond the athlete's useful range?

    This model predicts a sudden decline when high worry and high physiological arousal combine. It is a model, not a diagnosis or guaranteed outcome.

    8. During open play, a player watches the ball so closely that they miss an unmarked teammate signalling for a pass. Which term best describes this loss of awareness of useful peripheral cues?

    Attention has narrowed enough to miss information needed for this task. Narrow focus can be useful for other tasks; the problem here is losing a relevant cue.

    9. During a safe pause before serving, an athlete notices tense shoulders and a rushed routine. Which plan best matches the breathing-and-centering routine taught here?

    A centering routine connects a comfortable pause with a useful next action. It does not guarantee that worry disappears or that the next attempt succeeds.

    10. According to the source reading, which two factors are primary situational sources of stress in competitive environments?

    The source identifies event importance and uncertainty as situational stressors. Personal variables can matter too, but they are not the situational sources asked for here.

    Glossary & resources

    25 terms

    Arousal
    How activated your body and mind are, from very low activation to high energy and alertness.
    Anxiety
    Nervousness, worry or apprehension, sometimes accompanied by bodily sensations.
    Catastrophe Model
    A model proposing that performance can suddenly decline when high cognitive anxiety combines with high physiological arousal.
    Cognitive State Anxiety
    The degree to which a person experiences negative thoughts and worry in the moment.
    Debilitative Anxiety
    Anxiety interpreted as harmful to performance.
    Facilitative Anxiety
    Anxiety interpreted as helpful or energizing for performance.
    Ideal Performance State (IPS)
    The course’s term for being absorbed in the task with execution that feels smooth rather than forced.
    Individualized Zones of Optimal Functioning (IZOF)
    The idea that each athlete has a personal range of state anxiety linked to best performance.
    Inverted-U Hypothesis
    A model predicting that performance rises toward a useful arousal level, then falls as arousal rises further. The peak is not a universal target number.
    Mental Fitness
    Skills used to manage thoughts, feelings, attention and body responses when meeting a demand.
    Perception of Control
    The degree to which a person believes they have the resources and ability to meet a challenge.
    Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)
    A structured technique that contrasts tension with release in different muscle groups.
    SMART Goals
    Goals that are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound.
    Social Physique Anxiety
    Anxiety caused by the perception that others are evaluating one's body or physique.
    Somatic State Anxiety
    Bodily sensations experienced as part of feeling anxious in the current moment. A bodily sign alone does not prove anxiety.
    Stress
    In this course, a meaningful imbalance between a demand and the resources the performer believes they have to meet it.
    Trait Anxiety
    A more lasting tendency to interpret situations as threatening; not something established from one nervous moment.
    Stressor
    The demand or situation that may contribute to a stress response.
    Appraisal
    How a person interprets a demand and the resources available to meet it.
    Dominant response
    The response most likely to occur, often a well-practised habit. It is not always the correct response.
    Regulation
    A deliberate adjustment to activation, tension or attention.
    Process goal
    A goal about an action the performer can usually control more directly.
    Performance goal
    A goal about a personal standard or measurable performance result.
    Outcome goal
    A goal about a final result, such as winning or making a team.
    Reframe
    A different, believable interpretation connected to a real resource or useful action.
