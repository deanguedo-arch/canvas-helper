Humans in Research
Humans can be cruel.  There is no doubt about it.  Cruel and inhumane research has been conducted on all types of animals (including humans) in all fields of science.  Some of the more infamous experimentation on humans was conducted on prisoners interned in concentration camps during the World War II.
The crimes committed in World War II made evident the need to apply ethical thought to applied social research.  The Nuremberg War Crimes Trial following the war identified the ways German researchers had used humans in gruesome experiments.  Even decades after the war ended, questionable practices remained.  For example, in one study (the Tuskegee Syphilis Study), African Americans were denied a treatment known to be effective.  In essence, these individuals were used as guinea pigs.  Today, ethical researchers try to protect the rights of test subjects by adhering to the following principles.
Voluntary Participation.  With this principal, test subjects are not to be coerced into participation.  In the past, researchers would approach people who were in a confined location (such as prisons or hospitals) and intimidate, bribe, or coerce them to "volunteer."
Informed Consent.  Participants must be fully informed about the procedures involved and the associated risks.  Ethical standards also require researchers not put participants at any risk or harm as a result of participation.  Harm cannot be physical or psychological.
Confidentiality and Anonymity.  Participants in a study must be assured that identifying information will not be shared with anyone who is not involved in the research.  When possible, researchers should be blinded to the identifying information of the participants.
