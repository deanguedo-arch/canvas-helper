Case Study 13
Brain Damage and Ethical Behaviour

Do we have a choice regarding how to behave or are we products of our genetic coding?
The nature-nurture debate is classic in the field of psychology.  Many people believe
behaviour results from a blend of genetic coding (nature), and familial upbringing (how we
were raised as children) -- nurture.  A study published in the journal Nature suggests that
ethical behaviour may be, at least in part, hardwired in the brain.

The Dilemma
American researchers presented a controversial scenario to 30 test subjects.  The
participants were told that if they do harm to one person, they could prevent future harm to
many others.  For example, if you knew that someone with a deadly infectious disease was
planning to infect others (some of whom would die), would you kill the person or would
you let the person intentionally infect others?

The Results

Many of the participants struggled in making a decision and
most said they would not kill the infected individual.  Six
participants, however, stated a willingness to harm one
individual to protect others.  These six participants had
damage to a part of their brains called the ventromedial
prefrontal cortex (VMPC).  The VMPC is part of the frontal
lobe and the frontal lobe is linked to higher cognitive
functioning.

2
Ralph Adolphs, professor of psychology and neuroscience at the California Institute of
Technology, believes that VMPC brain damage results in abnormal social emotions.  The
six participants, he believed, lacked empathy and compassion.  Of the remaining 24
participants, 12 had no brain damage and 12 had brain damage in areas other than the
VMPC.

Although correlation is not causation, this study implies that aversion to hurting others
may be hard-wired in the frontal lobe of the brain.  This leaves researchers with an
important question:  Are social emotions necessary for moral judgments?  The research
continues!
