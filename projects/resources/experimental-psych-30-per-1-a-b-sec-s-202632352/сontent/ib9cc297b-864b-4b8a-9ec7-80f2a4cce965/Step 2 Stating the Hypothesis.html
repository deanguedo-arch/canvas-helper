Step 2: Stating the Hypothesis

After researching a topic and choosing a problem, a scientist makes a prediction about the expected results of his or her experiment.  This prediction is called a hypothesis.   Hypotheses must be worded in such a way that measurements can be readily obtained to prove or disprove each hypothesis.

Using the example from Step 1, you want to know if being extremely full (satiated) affects the response time of drivers.  You predict that if a person has just finished a large meal and is quite full, he or she will have a slower response rate compared to being neither full nor hungry.  The manipulated and responding variables should be clearly outlined in a hypothesis.  In this case, the manipulated variable is the level of satiety (fullness) and the responding variable is response time.

Often a hypothesis is written in the form of an if-then statement.  Your prediction might read like this:  If a person is extremely full, then he or she will have a slower response time compared to a person who is neither full nor hungry.  Someone else might have a hypothesis that states that, if a person is extremely full, then he or she will have a faster response time compared to a person who is neither full nor hungry.  Both hypotheses are acceptable.  

Caution:  Even if your experiment showed exactly the opposite of what you predicted (or no effect at all), your experiment was not a failure.  The information is still useful and increases the knowledge base of the area under investigation.
