The Re-interpretation Effect and Observer Bias


The Re-interpretation Effect
Although the placebo effect actually results in a tangible benefit to the patient, the re-interpretation effect is more of a perceptual illusion than a real benefit.  For example, even when a treatment does not improve symptoms, people may re-interpret their symptoms and experience them as less severe.
For example, if you purchase a new brand of syrup that convincingly claims you will cough less frequently, you might experience yourself coughing less frequently even if there is no actual change in your coughing behaviour.  In this case, you re-interpreted your symptoms to perceive them as less severe.

 

Observer Bias
Researchers and doctors rather than subjects and patients are affected by observer bias, and it relates, once again, to belief systems.  For example, if researchers believe that they are giving participants an effective drug, and they then interview those participants, they will observe improvements even if there are none.  Also known as confirmation bias, observer bias really refers to "selective noticing" whereby researchers tend to notice and to look for what confirms their beliefs and to ignore or overlook the relevance of what contradicts their belief systems.
In one example summarized by Dr. Bratman, the effectiveness of a new treatment for multiple sclerosis was compared to a placebo treatment.  This example was a double-blind study with a "twist."  Most doctors who were to evaluate the results did not know which participants received the real treatment and which received the fake treatment (i.e., they were "blinded").  However, the experimenters introduced an interesting variation: they allowed a few physicians to know which patients were receiving the real treatment (i.e., they were "unblinded").  The results were a bit surprising.  The unblinded physicians were much more likely to "observe" that the treatment worked compared to the impartial blinded physicians.  In other words, the unblinded physicians imagined a benefit because they expected to see one.
