Unit Introduction

As you have seen in the previous unit, for some nations, the economic, political, or security interests of a nation may be considered of greater importance than the interests of the international community.

Other nations may take the perspective that achieving global interests is beneficial to meeting their national interests. These global interests become their national interests. In this unit you will explore the following:

  • Nations choose whether or not to pursue internationalism for a variety of motives.
  • Relationships among countries can be complex and dynamic
  • Foreign policy can affect you both directly and indirectly
  • The ways nations interact in international affairs has an impact on individual and collective identities.

What is Internationalism?

What is Internationalism? Essential Questions: What is internationalism? How is internationalism different from globalization? How do national perspectives affect moti...

Essential Questions:

  • What is internationalism?

  • How is internationalism different from globalization?

  • How do national perspectives affect motives?

Have you ever had two friends that were fighting with each other?  They both come to you and want you to choose sides – what do you do?  Do you pick one friend over another, do you stay out of it, or do you try to get them to talk to each other and solve the problem so you can all be friends again?

Just like you, leaders of nations have to decide when and how they will get involved with other nations. There is a fine line between assisting and interfering, and there are consequences for involvement, both positive and negative.  When you get involved in an argument between two friends, are you taking a positive step to helping those friends, or are you interfering in a situation, maybe even making things worse? It can be difficult to predict what your efforts will do to the situation.

What is Internationalism?

Internationalism is a sense of common interests among nations, a recognition that the interests of your nation-state often extend beyond domestic concerns.  For example, if the environment is a priority in your nation, it only makes sense to join with other nations to work on reducing pollution in the atmosphere because pollution does not recognize national borders - one nation acting alone cannot solve global warming. It is different from globalization because globalization doesn’t necessarily mean cooperation, while internationalism encourages nations to cooperate.

Internationalism does NOT include setting up colonial empires or spheres of influence because these policies are only self-serving (think back to imperialism). Internationalism is not about controlling others. It is about cooperating with others. This ideology involves placing global concerns before a country's own national interests. Internationalists avoid isolationist policies, that "isolate" a country from being involved in the international community. Internationalists often cite the American policy of isolationism and refusal to particpate in the League of Nations during the interwar period as one of the reasons the League failed to prevent World War II.

What is Internationalism?

Essential question:

  • What is foreign policy?

Internationalism affects how one nation acts towards other nations. The decision a nation or nation-state makes with regard to other nations and nation-states is called foreign policy. In Canada, foreign policy is the responsibility of the Global Affairs Canada. Who is the current Minister of Global Affairs?

When it comes to foreign policy, a nation must decide what their priorities are.  A nation will evaluate their motives, and how these motives change in response to various conditions.  For example, do you want to build your own economy, or focus on humanitarian assistance? That could depend on the current situation; was there just a natural disaster that would increase the need for humanitarian assistance?

Motives and conditions can also affect internationalism because there is that fine line of helping versus interfering. A nation needs to decide when they should become involved in the affairs of another nation or nation-state. What is the boundary between self-determination and the need for international cooperation? Why get involved?

Sovereignty

For a nation to pursue a policy of internationalism successfully, it must be willing to sacrifice some amount of national sovereignty to be a part of the international community. But there are degrees of internationalism; a nation-state would not be willing to let the international community control everything in their country, as that would threaten their sovereignty.  Yet that same nation-state cannot expect to control the international organizations they belong to.  Each nation or nation-state must decide how much sovereignty they want to sacrifice in order to be a member of the international community.

For example, member countries of the United Nations need to put their own national interests aside when discussing policies regarding peacekeeping efforts, humanitarian aid, or the environment.  By being willing to sacrifice your own economy or your soldiers in order to help others, you are taking an internationalist approach.  Often, nations enter into multilateral ("many nation") agreements to address a particular issue of concern common to all nations, like the signing of the Kyoto Protocol to improve air quality.

Introduction to Motives

Essential Questions:

  • What are some common motives of nations and states?

  • How do national perspectives affect motives?

  • How do the motives of nations and states shape their responses to the world?

Every day we make choices.  For example, the clothing you are wearing right now, what motivated you to make that choice? Was it more comfortable, is it appropriate for work, does it make you look good? Another choice you made today - did you eat breakfast this morning?  If you are motivated to have more energy throughout the day and have a healthy lifestyle, you would be motivated to eat the healthy breakfast instead of grabbing coffee and a donut at Tim Horton’s, or skipping breakfast altogether.

Nations are a lot like people; they each have choices to make that are motivated by their own interests.  What motivated the Siksika nation of Alberta to build a world-class museum at Blackfoot Crossing?  What motivated groups around the world to work together to fight the AIDS crisis in Africa? What motivated the people of Kosovo to declare independence? What motivated Canada to send troops to Afghanistan in 2002?

The different perspectives people have can affect their motives.  Our perspectives affect the way we see and interpret our world.  In this lesson, you will be exploring various perspectives on global issues.  You will be able to identify how different stakeholders hold a variety of opinions on international issues.  In addition, you will be able to analyze these perspectives and evaluate how these perspectives are related to the motives of nations regarding their involvement (or non-involvement) in international affairs.

  • The Future of Food - Vandana Shiva 1 hour (Recipident of the Calgary Peace Prize 2011)
  • Rethinking Food Crisis Solutions—Al Jazeer 2.5 minutes - video embedded below:

A nation-state like Canada may have a humanitarian motive in the food crisis, like Global Affairs' "Central Emergency Response Fund," providing rapid response assistance to the UN agencies after natural disasters that might affect a nation's food supply, like drought or floods. And motives may overlap: Canada may also be motivated to solve the food crisis because of the desire for international peace and security. Competition for resources - in this case food - is the most common cause of violent conflicts and war.

But then again, Canada may want to be involved in the global discussion on the food crisis because Canada is a major exporter of food, such as wheat, which means the high price of these goods helps our citizens who are growing and selling these crops. Thus Canada has an economic stability motive that may or may not be in conflict with humanitarian and peace and security motives.

The United Nations, a supranational organization, would be motivated by humanitarian reasons as well, but may also have motives related to keeping peace and stability in the world by preventing riots and conflicts over food.  The UN would turn to organizations they have already created, like the FAO (Food and Agriculture Organization) and WFP (World Food Programme), to assist them in providing people the basic necessities in order to try to prevent the crisis from spiraling into violence.

There may be individuals and communities right here at home that are working to assist those that are lacking food. Can you think of one? What would be their motivation? Even transnational corporations are working to reduce the impact of this crisis. What would motivate a company created to make profits to want to help people who cannot afford to purchase the company’s products?

Essential Questions:

  • What motivates nations to pursue internationalism?

  • How do national perspectives affect motives?

It can be difficult to determine what the motives really are, as there is often more than one motive influencing someone to act.  In order to help organize the various motives, they have been summarized into the following categories (click the following links):

  • Humanitarianism
  • Economic stability
  • Self-determination
  • Peace and Security

Reading

Please read Chapter 9: pages 226 - 227 in the Exploring Nationalism textbook.

The difficulty with international relations is that some goals can conflict with other goals; for example, knowing when to violate the sovereignty of a nation in order to promote peace and security for its citizens.  The crisis in Darfur in the early twenty-first century has proven to be a difficult case. Nation-states around the world debate what to do for the people of Sudan who are experiencing acts of genocide.  The government of Sudan is reluctant to allow UN peacekeepers into their country because they do not want to give up their sovereignty.  And yet the humanitarian motive of wanting to help these people who cannot help themselves could override the sovereignty of a nation that is hurting its people.

Sample Opinions: Foreign Policy Motives for Canada

Which are the most compelling motives for Canada? Are any?

Read the opinions of the following speakers (If you are still able to play Flash, you can listen to the opinions here.)

Speaker 1 - Peace and Security:

"In Canada, there is a Charter of Rights and Freedoms that guarantees the government will provide each citizen with a certain level of security so that citizens can live fairly and safely. Nation states also function at a better level when they have fewer concerns about peace and security. Examples of some 21st century issues that add stress to countries' levels of security include civil wars, terrorism, conflicts between states, environmental destruction and poverty. I am proud to say that Canada is recognized for its role in safeguarding world peace; taking leadership roles in UN peacekeeping efforts worldwide. Unlike countries that wish to unilaterally interact in world affairs, Canada recognizes that it is difficult to deal with today's international threats alone and prefers to use the UN as a diplomatic tool.

This is one reason why Canada did not join the coalition of countries that invaded Iraq in 2003. International opinion was divided and all channels of diplomacy suggested by the UN had not been attempted. Look at the issues in Iraq today! Canada's mission in Afghanistan is an example of where there is a consensus of the more than 25 NATO member countries that are offering their help in rebuilding and with security issues. It may not be an easy task but Canada strives to work collaboratively with the international community to provide a secure future for all Afghan citizens. Peace and security are vital human needs without which societies cannot develop socially or economically. Who can attend school on a regular basis when life in your country is unsafe? Canada must continue to maintain its leadership in international organizations, such as the United Nations."

Speaker 2 - Self-Determination

"Just like people want to feel like they are in control of their lives, nations strive to fulfill their own peoples' economic, social and political needs. Though Canada became a country in 1867, it did not have complete control over its own destiny until the Statute of Westminster of 1932. For example, when Britain entered World War One, Canada was automatically at war. But by 1939, Canada independently made the decision to enter the Second World War. Most nations want to control their own fate. But what happens when this control affects other nations? What happens when building one's nations' economy creates negative environmental issues in another country? There is a responsibility Canada has to other nations as well.

The whole world is affected when economic decisions caused Brazil to cut down more of its rainforest, since it is 1/3 of the world's remaining rainforest. If the US decides to develop the Arctic Wildlife Refuge in Alaska, it spills over to Canada's part of the refuge in the Yukon, and will affect Canada's environment and wildlife patterns. I think Canada also has a responsibility to help other groups maintain their self-determination. When the human rights of Kosovars were threated in the Greater Yugoslavia of the 1990s, Canada joined other NATO allies and contributed in the international peacekeeping force there. Canada is now one of about 50 countries that recognize the right of Kosovo to be independent."

Speaker 3 - Economic Stability

"I think everyone will agree that money makes the world go around. People are highly motivated to act in ways that best support their family. Jobs and economic stability are extremely important. Countries are not that much different from ordinary people, so decisions that affect employment and economic growth are very high on the federal government's list of concerns. Canada must be economically engaged with the international community if it hopes to keep pace with countries that are members of the European Union and Asian countries that are active members of economic trade blocs. International trade helps Canada create employment and technological exchange; it keeps Canada on the leading edge.

It would be quite foolish for Canada to leave NAFTA, or approve high tariffs or other trade restrictions, just because Canadians are concerned about protecting resources, such as protecting water or jobs for Canadians. These concerns are valid but solutions need to be developed bilaterally, multilaterally and internationally, not by imposing protectionist policies. So Canada's concern for economic policy should remain the highest priority for government action. Canadians cannot be of much help to themselves or others if they don't have the means to make a difference."

Speaker 4 - Humanitarianism

"Sometimes people say that a chain is only as strong as its weakest link. If this chain is the global community, then everyone has the responsibility to help countries that face challenges. This does not mean Canada should ignore Canadians; there are many groups within Canada whose situations also need to be addressed. Helping those countries in need should more than provide relief when disaster strikes. Human suffering is very evident when nations are victims of famine, hurricanes, or tsunami. The relief effort provided officially through government agencies, and privately through church groups and organizations such as the International Red Cross, is vital.

Canada, as a strong member of the G8, needs to play a larger role in providing humanitarian assistance and relieving suffering and protecting the innocent. Yes, Canada's acceptance of refugees and response to natural disasters, disease and war are significant and commendable. But the bottom line is that Canada's contribution to foreign aid through official channels is inadequate and tends to shrink when the country is faced with economic recession. Humanitarianism should be one of Canada's strongest motives for government projects. Support for agencies that promote social justice should but provided on a consistent basis that clearly speaks to the commitment to make the world a better place for the greatest number of people."

    Multilateralism: many nations working together to accomplish goals such as negotiating trade or defense agreements, work on assisting a humanitarian effort, or creating international law.

    Several countries working together can be a powerful force for change.  This is why a multilateral approach is often taken to solve major international issues. For example, the proliferation (spread) of nuclear weapons is a global concern, so the United Nations International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) was created to try to monitor and control the number of nuclear weapons available.

    Multilateralism can affect your life in many ways. Because of improvements in transportation, nation-states must work together in order to facilitate international travel.  Think about air travel; if you fly from Vancouver to Beijing China, you will likely fly over the United States (Alaska) and Russia (the flight from Calgary to China goes far north in order to save time as the earth is not as wide as you get closer to the poles).  There needs to be multilateral agreements in place to allow a Canadian plane to invade the airspace of those nation-states.

    Canada often supports multilateral approaches because we are known as a middle power: we are not a superpower (like the USA), or even a former great power (like Britain, France or Germany) but we are not a weak nation either.  Canada can have a stronger voice by joining together with other like-minded middle powers, such as Australia and South Africa, in organizations like the Commonwealth.

    Some of the benefits that come with multilateralism include support from a variety of nations and nation-states. By having many voices express their opinion on an issue, chances are an effective solution can be found. Wide support can also lend an action legitimacy. The downside to having all of these voices is the time that it takes to listen to everyone.  It can take weeks, months or years for everyone to agree. While a multilateral organization is discussing what should be done, thousands of people could be starving. A nation acting unilaterally might be quicker to act. Then again, acting unilaterally may result in choosing a less effective action because not all perspectives were considered.

    Supranationalism

    Supranationalism Supranationalism: the idea of supporting an organization which brings several nations together with an independent governing body, to go above the aut...

    Supranationalism: the idea of supporting an organization which brings several nations together with an independent governing body, to go above the authority of a nation-state. Member nations agree to abide by the decisions of the supranational organization.

    The United Nations strongly believes in a multilateral approach to solving world problems.  The UN is a supranational multilateral organization that includes almost every nation from around the world working together. The organization is directed by an independent administration (national governments get to vote on issues but they must abide by the final decision.  In a supranational organization, the priorities of the group must come before the priorities of individual nations in order to be successful.

    Loss of sovereignty

    The concern with multilateralism or supranationalism is that it often means a loss of sovereignty. For example, the European Union demands that when a nation-state, joins the EU, it must follow a set of guidelines in regards to their economy and human rights.  This means that the nation-state must follow the decisions of the EU. Sometimes citizens do not like the demands of the EU. Nation-states in Europe are often willing to follow the EU rules because it is such a powerful organization; the benefits of belonging to such an influential organization outweigh the loss of sovereignty.

    In 2019, the nation-state of Serbia extradited a man to the world court to face a war crimes tribunal.  Many citizens of Serbia saw Karadzic as a hero and did not want him extradited, but the government of Serbia wants to join the EU and this was one of the conditions.

    This need to balance sovereignty and internationalism can be seen with the issue of peacekeeping.  The United Nations created peacekeeping forces to help nation-states manage their conflicts; however the UN also recognizes national sovereignty so peacekeepers must be invited by the government of the affected nation-state to monitor a ceasefire or peace settlement. But what happens when the ruling government is committing atrocities? They will not want to invite the UN to come in and stop them.

    The UN Charter bans the use of force for all but two circumstances:

    • self-defence against an actual or imminent attack
    • to prevent or stop acts of genocide or crimes against humanity

    In these cases, the UN can authorize peace-enforcement missions (instead of keeping the peace, troops from member nations are sent in to impose peace, by force if necessary). But the UN has been very reluctant to use peace-enforcement. This is because many members of the UN do not want to violate the principle of national sovereignty (all members of the UN are nation-states, so they are reluctant to set a precedent that might interfere with their own sovereignty).

    In the first 50 years of the UN, it authorized the use of force only twice; both times in matters of national self-defence - North and South Korea in 1950 and Iraq-Kuwait in 1991. Both of these cases involved one nation-state invading another. So the UN was defending the national sovereignty of the nations attacked; South Korea and Kuwait.

    More recently, the UN has authorized peace-enforcement in the Democratic Republic of Congo and Darfur, Sudan. The UN mission in Congo and the African Union (African multilateral organization) mission in Sudan are both authorized to use force to protect civilians and to prevent armed attacks and violations of the peace agreement. However, in both cases the UN has been reluctant to provide the resources to really allow the troops deployed to carry out the peace-enforcement part of the mission.

    Canada's Foreign Policy Goals

    Although priorities may change for various reasons, Canada has essentially six foreign policy goals:

    • Peace and security:  Canada works to encourage world peace through defense arrangements, peacekeeping efforts and by maintaining friendly relations with other nations.
    • Social justice:  Canada provides foreign aid, loans and other support to nations in need. 
    • Economic growth:  Canada wants to maintain a high standard of living* for its citizens.  It pursues policies such as trade agreements to improve the economic well-being of its citizens.
    • Sovereignty: Canada needs to ensure its own existence by preserving control over our own affairs and not allowing other nation-states to control or excessively  influence us.
    • Safe environment:  The world is becoming increasingly aware of the need to protect the earth’s environment.  Canada cooperates with other countries in attempting to control pollution and other environmental hazards.
    • Quality of Life:  Canada believes quality of life* is an important consideration.  Canada participates in cultural, technological, and scientific exchanges.  These can improve the quality of life for Canadians and citizens of other nations.
    *Quality of Life versus Standard of Living: Quality of Life refers to how satisfied people are with their lives and can include friends and family, the environment and employment.  Standard of Living refers to the amount of material goods one has (how much money and “stuff” they have). Standard of living does not necessarily create a good quality of life.

    There are a variety of methods Canada can use to implement those foreign policy goals.  We have diplomats that represent our country in other nation-states around the world.  We have signed on to international trade and military agreements for our economic growth and security.  Canada is often asked to play a role as a negotiator, or to observe events like an election to encourage peace between or within nation-states.  If we disagree with how a nation-state is behaving we can use diplomacy, economic sanctions, or the threat of war to try to change their behaviour.

    Understand the Difference

    Review the definitions by clicking on the words: peacekeeping, peace-enforcement and peacemaking.

    These terms mean different things - yet even experts get them confused* (see below).

    One way to help remember the difference is to reverse the combined words:

    Peacekeeping becomes "keeping the peace". This implies that there is a peace to keep - there has to be a peace agreement or ceasefire in place and the previously warring sides invite peacekeepers to monitor the peace or ceasefire they agreed to - for as long as it takes until a permanent reconciliation is reached. Peacekeepers are lightly armed neutral observers and only allowed to use armed force to defend themselves. All these criteria need to be fullfilled for it to be a true peacekeeping mission. To avoid mistakes by the warring sides, and to reassure civilians who may not trust one or both of the warring sides, UN peacekeepers wear distinctive blue helmets to let everyone know they are neutral.

    Peace-enforcement becomes "enforcing peace", or just "forcing peace". A third party (individual country, alliance or temporary coalition) can enter a conflict to impose peace between two warring sides. Military force can be used to remove an army that has invaded a neighbouring country. Peace-enforcement can also be used for humanitarian reasons, such as to stop or prevent acts of genocide or crimes against humanity by one or both sides in a conflict. Peace-enforcement does NOT require an invitation by the warring sides. It typically comes from the third party alliance or an international organization. If peace-enforcement is authorized by the UN, it falls under "Chapter VII" of the UN Charter: Specifically Articles 42 and 51 - the authorization to use military force to enforce the UN's decisions. For example, a Security Council resolution's mandate may stipulate that third party peace enforcers may use armed force to protect civilians, infrastructure, themselves or even go on the offensive against combatants in the conflict (the Korean War was a Chapter VII mission). Note the differences between this and peacekeeping.

    Peacemaking* becomes "making peace". This implies that there is no peace, or a violent conflict is looming. When two friends are arguing and about to start swinging fists, you may not be able to use force to stop them, even if just to avoid injury to yourself. But you might be able to use rational (or emotional) appeals to persuade your fighting friends to "make peace". Diplomacy is the tool used at the international level to "make peace" between warring sides or before a serious conflict erupts into violence.

    Peacebuilding, becomes "building peace". This can occur to prevent violence from occurring in the first place, or after a conflict has ended to avoid a resumption of hostilities. This involves international assistance to build the infrastructure required for lasting peace, including economic and development assistance, training police and security forces and developing institutions to promote civil society. While it is typically done before or after a conflict, peacebuilding has also been attempted during violent conflicts (eg. Afghanistan and Iraq: 2001-today).

    UN Peacekeeping

    While unarmed peacekeepers were deployed to monitor the ceasefire after the Arab-Israeli War, you discovered in your textbook reading that armed peacekeeping started in 1956, when nation-states fought over control of the Suez Canal. A Canadian diplomat, Lester Pearson, came up with the idea of having the United Nations send in neutral forces that would keep the two sides apart until peaceful negotiations create a long-term solution to the problem.

    The troops of member nations serving as United Nations peacekeepers go into areas of conflict wearing their distinctive blue helmets - a symbol to identify them as neutral peacekeepers.  Although they are sent in as soldiers to keep the peace, they are often given peacebuilding tasks in order to help the country they have been invited into, like monitoring elections, assisting the local police, training the army and rebuilding local communities. Canadian police officers often serve alongside soldiers as part of UN peacekeeping missions. This could be considered a combination of peacekeeping and peacebuilding in their mission mandate.

    Problems with UN peacekeepers

    Some people question whether or not peacekeepers are in fact neutral.  If Canada is allowed to decide when and where to send our peacekeeping troops, are we promoting our own ideology by getting involved only in missions we agree with? Are we expecting nations to accept our values, even if those values conflict with the values of that country’s people?  For example, if Canada sends in troops and then starts pressuring the government to give more rights to the women in the region, this could be seen as Canada trying to promote an ideology which we think is right in a region that may not support this ideology. The same could be said about economic ideology. Do we choose to participate in the missions that will benefit us economically in the long run? And ignore those that will have no economic impact on Canada?

    A bigger issue is the reputation of the United Nations.  In the 1990s, the United Nations sent peacekeeping missions to Somalia and Bosnia.  Both of these missions failed to keep civilians safe.  In 1994 a genocide occurred in Rwanda while UN peacekeepers looked on.  Why?  Because when peacekeepers are sent in, they are not there to get involved in the fighting, they cannot fire their weapons unless they are directly threatened.  Romeo Dallaire, the Canadian commander of UN peacekeeping forces in Rwanda, wanted to intervene to stop the genocide but the UN Security Council would not authorize him to do so. Many would see this as a failure of UN peacekeeping. Yet UN forces can only do what the Security Council authorizes. Why do you think members of the Security Council were reluctant to transition from peacekeeping to peace-enforcement?

    At the end of the 20th century, rebels increasingly ignored the neutral reputation of the UN blue helmets and would attack civilians in front of peacekeepers, knowing that the peacekeepers had a limited ability to stop what was happening. And parties to the conflict were not recongizing UN peacekeepers as neutral. In Bosnia, Somalia and Rwanda, UN Peacekeepers themselves came under attack by parties to those conflicts. It is not that peacekeepers are inneffective; they have been successful in keeping hostile sides apart until tempers cool. They are particularly effective when both sides want peace. Less effective when one or both sides are not committed to the peace process. The problem is that peacekeepers are not authorized to intervene with the use of force. That compromises their neutrality. To intervene with force, you need something other than neutral peacekeepers. That is when peace-enforcement comes into play.

    Peace-enforcement

    One solution proposed to solve these problems is to have the United Nations create it’s own army.  Each member nation would be required to have a certain number of troops dedicated to this army.  That way the United Nations would always know how many troops they have available to them, and will be able to control the actions of these troops. The United Nations would multilaterally decide when and where to get involved. So if 1,000 Canadian troops were assigned to the UN army, they would have to deploy where the UN decides rather than Canada picking and choosing which missions to participate in. A permanent UN standby force was envisioned in the UN Charter in 1946 but resistance to the idea by UN Security Council members, especially the Soviet Union, and Cold War (1947 - 1991) politics meant it was never implemented. The Cold War and the Soviet Union are long gone. Should the UN reconsider this idea?

    Another solution is to create an atmosphere where UN members or other international organizations are more willing to particiapte in peace-enforcement.  The usual rules of engagement do not allow UN peacekeepers to use force except in their own self-defence. By authorizing peace-enforcement, more heavily armed soldiers could intervene to bring a swift end to the conflict or crimes against humanity. And since it would be a multilateral effort, the offending nation would know that this effort had wide support among the international community, lending the action legitimacy.

    The United Nations has authorized peace-enforcement in the past.  In 1990 Iraq invaded Kuwait, allegedly because of a dispute over a shared oil field.  The decision by the United Nations to authorize a multi-nation force to intervene was relatively easy to make, as one nation had invaded another (a clear violation of the UN Charter). The 1991 U.S. led counter-invasion successfully removed Iraqi forces from Kuwait in one of the swiftest wars in history.  But it can be much more difficult to decide whether to send in troops when the events are occurring within a single sovereign nation.

    Problems with Peace-enforcement

    Peace-enforcement is typically not a neutral force. They usually take sides, such as in the case of the Iraqi invasion of Kuwait above. Furthermore, peace-enforcement typically requires more resources than peacekeeping because it is about war-fighting and not just observation of a ceasefire. The UN does not have a standing army so peace-enforcement operations may be delegated to multilateral organizations, like NATO. However, NATO is an exclusive military alliance primarily concerned with the interests of its member nations (Europeans and North-Americans). It is therefore not neutral. Non-members have no say in NATO decision making. Another problem with peace-enforcement is that it can violate national sovereignty - a sovereign state controls the use of force on its own territory, not foreign armed forces. Another downside to peace-enforcement is that it does not address the underlying problems that created the conflict in the first place. The Korean War of the 1950s was a UN operation, authorized under Chapter VII of the UN Charter. While they succeeded in restoring the pre-existing border between N. and S. Korea, the N. Korean dictatorship remains a threat to international security.

    The United Nations has declared that it must get involved to protect the people living in a state that is no longer protecting the welfare of their citizens, or actively harming them. It is legally authorized to intervene, or authorize multilateral organizations to intervene, in cases of genocide or crimes against humanity. This interventionist approach was designed to prevent a recurrence of events like the genocide of World War Two. The methods of intervention can range from humanitarian support for the victims, to diplomatic efforts, or even the use of force (peace-enforcement). Any of these efforts can be seen to violate the sovereignty of the nation involved. Therefore, unlike peace enforcement, peacekeepers are invited by the parties to a conflict and therefore do not violate national sovereignty.

    Canadian Foreign Policy - Water

    Canadian Foreign Policy - Water The high cost of food, as a result of the global food crisis, led Argentina to impose a tariff on the export of essential goods, such a...

    The high cost of food, as a result of the global food crisis, led Argentina to impose a tariff on the export of essential goods, such as wheat, to keep the supply of food in the country high and the prices low for Argentinian consumers. This policy encourages farmers to sell locally instead of exporting to foreign markets made more expensive by the export tariff. It also means nations that normally import Argentinian wheat are unable to get as much, causing a shortage in other countries. This is a unilateral trade policy - the focus is on national interests over international interests.

    Canada, on the other hand, supports participation in international trade agreements. Belonging to organizations like the WTO and NAFTA help us to sell our goods to other nation-states. Under the rules of NAFTA, Canada cannot do to other NAFTA members (USA and Mexico) what Argentina has done with wheat; we cannot keep a resource for ourselves. The upside to this policy is that the United States and Mexico are also unable to hoard the goods that Canadians may need or want. But what if we don’t want to sell something?

    Canada is blessed with abundant water resources; it has almost 10% of the world's supply of renewable freshwater, and the relatively small population means that there is less of a demand on our water supplies than there is in the United States (Freshwater, Atlas of Canada).  Many U.S. rivers, like the Colorado River, become a trickle in some areas as growing communities, agriculture and industries take more out of the rivers and water table. It is feared that continued droughts will cause conflict between regions as the demands for water increase, including a potential future conflict between Canada and the United States.

    Does a war over water sound a bit far fetched?  Well, let’s look at a region that is already facing conflict over water. The Nile River provides almost all of the water for the nation-state of Egypt. But the Nile doesn’t start in Egypt, it ends there. The Nile travels through 10 other countries before it ends up in Egypt, but because of Egypt’s relative power, they are able to demand control of this important waterway.

    In 1929, Egypt signed an agreement with the other nations that essentially gave it the final say on what could be done with the waterway - if dams could be built in other countries, or how much water could be diverted for irrigating crops. Egypt sees the control of the Nile as their right, and as necessary to protect their people and economy. But nations like Sudan are experiencing severe droughts, and have threatened to go to war with Egypt if the rules established in 1929 are not adjusted to help the people of Sudan. This region of Africa is already unstable, and a conflict over water between these two countries could turn into a global conflict, as many Western nations, like the United States, are allied with Egypt, and some enemies of the United States are allied with Sudan. (Cathcart, 2007)

    Of course conditions in North America are different than conditions in Africa.  The Canadian and American governments have not gone to war with each other for almost two centuries – we use diplomacy to solve our conflicts.  And right now Canadian water is classified as an essential resource (like air) and is not seen as a commodity.  Although we can bottle it and sell it, companies cannot put large amounts of water up for sale (like filling up a ship the size of an oil tanker or sending it through pipelines to the USA and Mexico).

    Could an internationalist approach to water solve these issues? Could there be an international organization that would control access to all water around the world, to ensure that this precious commodity is protected and shared?  Do you think Canadians would want to take an internationalist approach to our water resources?

    Understand the Difference

     

    International organizations = organizations "between nations"

    Our focus, therefore, is on states working through international "government" organizations. The United Nations is an international government organization, because it includes representatives of governments from almost every nation-state in the world. It is an international organization because it is organized around the principle of relations between nations (inter = between). International organizations can promote internationalism - that is, putting global concerns above national concerns.

    Non-government Organizations (NGOs) or Transnational Organizations = "not governments" and "transcend nations"

    Non-government organizations (NGOs), like the International Red Cross, Amnesty International or Medicins Sans Frontieres/Doctors Without Borders (MSF) also promote internationalism by addressing global concerns. But these are not technically "international" organizations because they operate without the direct support of governments (even though they may have "international" in their official name). Rather, NGOs that operate on the world stage are known as transnational organizations. They transcend - or work beyond - national boundaries and governments. They may sometimes work with national governments and international organizations, but they are not themselves government organizations.

    Tied Aid

    Tied aid is foreign aid that has conditions or “strings attached” because it’s like some condition tied to it with strings.  For example, Canada may donate a million dollars to a region, but request that all of the money be spent on Canadian goods; that way Canadian producers benefit while the country receiving the donation can still have some choice in what to spend the money on but not where to spend the money. Or Canada may request that there be improvements in human rights in the region, which helps us to promote our foreign policy goal of social justice.

    Multilateral vs unilateral aid

    The best approach to donating foreign aid is bilateral, or multilateral.  When foreign aid is unilateral, it often means that a good intention can be wasted because the donation is being made without consulting the regional leaders.  In a small village of Ethiopia, a Canadian group donated a school building, desks, books and pens.  The school was built strong, with reinforced concrete, and the desks were similar to the desks many students sit in at schools across Alberta.  But over time the climate took its toll on the building and the desks.  The concrete began to break away, revealing the steel rebar which began to bend, making the building unstable; and the desks’ metal frames began to bend and break.  The people in this small village did not have a welding torch to fix the rebar or the desks, they did not have concrete to mend the walls – they did not even have a water source nearby that would have been needed to mix the concrete!  Now the children read from their books outside under a tree and write in the sand (the pens have all run out of ink).  If this Canadian organization had just asked the locals what they needed, instead of donating this fantastic school, the locals may have been able to tell them what would have lasted more than a decade. Multilateral international organizations can help coordinate aid efforts.

    Last year Canada donated more than four billion dollars to other nation-states. However, there are many Canadians who feel we should not donate this much money to other nations. “We give too much money to other people!  We need to solve our own problems at home.”

    The United Nations goal: 0.7% of GDP for development assistance

    Did you know that Canada spent only 0.33% of our GDP on foreign aid in 2006 and 0.30% in 2019? Despite this small percentage of national wealth, only a handful of nations have met this target - Denmark (0.81%), (Netherlands (0.82%), Luxembourg (0.87%), Sweden (0.92%), Norway (0.93%).     Even though many see foreign aid as a positive humanitarian action, there are some drawbacks to foreign aid.  Often foreign aid is used to promote an ideology, and the aid is withheld if that ideology is not accepted (like the example of tied aid being linked to human rights).  Also, there is a concern with the donation of goods like food.       Political Organizations

      The European Union

      • Created initially as an economic trading bloc in 1951, has now evolved into a major political organization
      • Main objectives:
        • promote social and economic progress (e.g. the single currency was launched in 1999 - Euro)
        • strengthen European identity by creating a European citizenship that doesn’t replace national citizenship (e.g. a European passport to make it easier to travel)
        • build on established EU law
      • Institutions include the European Parliament, Investment Bank and Court
      • One major advantage of the EU is its ability to encourage economic and political stability in member nations, which reduces conflicts (potential wars)

      Arctic Council

      • Officially established in 1996 to promote environmental, social and economic sustainable development in the Arctic region.
      • Includes representatives from indigenous nations of the regions, as well as representatives from the nation-states of Canada, United States, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Sweden, Russia.

      African Union (AU)

      • Established in 2002, almost all African nation-states belong
      • Main objectives:
        • Overcome negative affects of colonialism and apartheid
        • Promote unity among AU members, while protecting territorial sovereignty
        • Coordinate and promote economic development
        • Promote international cooperation
      • Organizations include a parliament, court and economic council

      G-7 – The Group of Seven

      • Made up of the eight major developed nations 
        • USA, Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Canada (previously G8 - Russia was suspended after the annexation of Crimea in 2019).
      • Not a formal organization, instead they meet on a regular basis to discuss policy to help coordinate the foreign policies of member nations
        • In 2019 they dealt with issues as diverse as what to do about the failure of the Kyoto environmental protocol, and the fraudulent elections in Zimbabwe

       

      Military Organizations

      NATO - North Atlantic Treaty Organization

      • A regional defense alliance whose purpose is to enhance the stability, well-being, and freedom of it’s members through collective defence.
      • Initially created to help protect the weakened from World War II Western European democracies from the communist Soviet Union.
      • USA and Canada pledged to support the war-torn nations militarily, against the perceived threat of communist expansion.
      • With the collapse of communism in the early 1990s, some questioned the need for NATO, but it has now taken on a peace-enforcement role beyond its original European and the North Atlantic area of responsibility.

      NORAD - North American Aerospace Defense Command

      • During the Cold War the threat of nuclear bombers and missiles heading from the Soviet Union over the Arctic Circle to Canada and the United States was real.
        • U.S. and Canada created a bilateral organization to coordinate radar stations and military action in case of attack, specifically an air attack.
      • Like NATO, the role of NORAD has changed with the collapse of communism, but the alliance still plays an important role.  For example, on 9/11, NORAD allowed the governments of Canada and the United States to work together to deal with all of the air traffic already in the air when the attacks happened, and to be prepared for a counter-attack if necessary.

      Both NATO and NORAD are examples of collective defence: members joining together (collective) in order to defend each other from threats outside the alliance.

      Introduction to Internationalism and Contemporary Global Issues

      Internationalism is based on the belief that increased interaction between nation-states will make the world a better place. Governments attempt to address global issues by working together in international organizations. Yet even the best intentions can sometimes go awry.

      Donating food to a region struck by a natural disaster can actually make things worse for the local economy. The free food donated by international organizations could undercut local farmers' ability to sell their produce. After all, would you pay for food if it was available for free? This does not mean aid should be stopped. A more targeted approach might work better.

      After the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, aid organizations asked for money instead of food aid so that food could be bought from local producers - thereby supporting the local economy of the surrounding and stricken area. Sending aid in the form of Canadian wheat would have solved the immediate food shortage and benefitted Canadian producers, but it would have added further economic damage to that caused by the tsunami itself.

      Unit 3 Question: Should Internationalism Be Pursued?

      In this unit, you looked at the different perspectives of internationalism, and the ways in which nations can interact with each other.  That interaction can affect many things, including worldwide economic stability and international peace.  Many feel that in today’s globalized society, internationalism is required to solve global problems.

      With that focus on internationalism, there can be a loss of sovereignty.  Nations must decide how they want to balance internationalism with their national identity.  Does a nation want to have the reputation of being internationalist, like Canada is with the promotion of multiculturalism?  Or is it better to encourage a more isolationist approach in order to strengthen the patriotism of your people, which is seen in North Korea, or unilateralist, like the US was under George W. Bush

      The following key ideas should shape your response to the unit question:

      • The resolution of global issues may demand the participation of nations because of the impact these issues have on the nation itself.
      • International cooperation, involvement, or intervention may be necessary to maintain the economic, political, or security interests of the nation.

      • Nations such as Canada, the United States, and China and members of the European Union may hold the perspective that national citizens should come to the aid of global citizens. A variety of motives may lead a nation to include global interests as national interests.
      • It may be in the national interests of Canada to support global interests in the world economy, global security, and human rights’ issues in order to guarantee peace and stability in Canada.

      • The reaction of nations towards global issues may range from isolationism to active participation.
      • Depending on the extent of the issue, nations may or may not pursue internationalism to resolve the challenges created by the issue. It may be perceived as a local or a global issue. Internationalism may be applied if citizens and nations believe the issue has the risk of becoming a global issue and will impact citizens beyond the borders.

      • A sense of collective responsibility for the sustainability of this planet and the prosperity of all citizens may also lead a nation to come to the aid of another nation.

      • In a variety of situations, internationalism is viewed as the most effective tool. Immediately affected nations may not have the tools to respond quickly or effectively. Cooperation and involvement by other nations may require the sacrifice of nationalism in the pursuit of internationalism.

      • The cost of pursuing internationalism may make nations reluctant to sacrifice nationalism (for example, when national interests are ignored in favour of international interests or the national interests of another nation).
