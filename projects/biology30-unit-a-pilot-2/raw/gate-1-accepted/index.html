Neuron Structure

How does a message travel from a touch receptor to a muscle?

Before you begin

In the textbook

Words for this lesson

Meet the words before the mechanism

neuron
a cell specialized to receive and send information
dendrite and axon
receiving branches and the long conducting fibre
glial cell
a support cell that helps neurons function
myelin
an insulating covering around parts of some axons

Learn · Part 1

A neuron has regions with different jobs

A neuron is a cell that handles information. Its shape supports four jobs: receive a message, combine information, carry an electrical signal, and pass information to another cell.

Dendrites receive much of the input. The cell body contains the nucleus and keeps the cell working. The axon carries an electrical signal away from the cell body. At the far end, axon terminals communicate with another neuron, muscle, or gland.

These labels describe a typical pattern. Neurons vary in shape, so follow the direction of information instead of expecting every neuron to look identical.

Word lens:

Stop and Check

Follow the direction

If an electrical signal is moving toward the axon terminals, which structure did it travel along?

Check your thinking

It travelled along the axon. The axon is the long conducting region between the cell body and terminals.

Learn · Part 2

Glial cells and myelin help the signal arrive quickly

Neurons do not work alone. Glial cells support, protect, and maintain the conditions neurons need. Some glial cells wrap axons with layers of membrane called myelin.

Myelin acts as electrical insulation. It limits current loss from the axon between the nodes of Ranvier, which are small gaps in the covering. The action potential is regenerated at each node. This pattern, called saltatory conduction, is faster than regenerating the signal continuously along an unmyelinated axon of similar size.

Schwann cells form myelin around axons in the peripheral nervous system. Oligodendrocytes form myelin in the central nervous system. Damage can slow or interrupt signalling, but the amount of recovery depends on the tissue and the kind of injury.

Learn · Part 3

Three neuron roles form a pathway

A sensory neuron carries information from a receptor toward the central nervous system. An interneuron connects and processes information within the brain or spinal cord. A motor neuron carries a command from the central nervous system to an effector such as a muscle or gland.

In a simple withdrawal reflex, a receptor detects a harmful stimulus. A sensory neuron enters the spinal cord, where one or more connections activate a motor neuron. The motor neuron carries a signal to a muscle. The response can begin before conscious awareness, but information also travels to the brain.

Word lens:

Worked example

A hand touches a hot pan

  1. Heat and tissue-damage receptors create sensory information.
  2. A sensory neuron carries that information toward the spinal cord.
  3. Connections in the spinal cord pass the message into a motor pathway.
  4. A motor neuron carries the command to an arm muscle.
  5. The muscle contracts and moves the hand away. Information also travels to the brain, producing awareness of pain.

Reasoning: the pathway is identified by direction and job. It is not necessary for every neuron to have the same shape.

Advanced LearningWhy recovery differs in the PNS and CNS

Peripheral axons may regrow when the cell body survives and the pathway remains supportive. Schwann cells can help guide this process. Recovery is still limited and is not guaranteed.

Words for this lesson

Meet the words before the mechanism

neurotransmitter
a chemical messenger released by a neuron
synapse
the communication junction between a signalling cell and a target
receptor
a target-cell protein that binds a compatible messenger
cholinesterase

Learn · Part 1

Define the messenger before following the message

A neurotransmitter is a chemical messenger released by a neuron. It travels only a very short distance across a synaptic cleft and binds to matching receptor proteins on a target cell.

The complete junction is called a synapse. The cell that releases the messenger is presynaptic. The cell that receives it is postsynaptic. The target may be another neuron, a muscle cell, or a gland cell.

A neurotransmitter does not carry the electrical action potential through the empty space. Instead, the arriving electrical signal causes chemical release. Receptor binding then changes the target cell.

  1. ArriveAction potential reaches terminal.
  2. OpenVoltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open.
  3. ReleaseVesicles release neurotransmitter.
  4. DiffuseMessenger crosses the cleft.
  5. BindMessenger binds target receptors.
  6. EndBreakdown, reuptake, or diffusion limits the signal.

Stop and Check

Electrical, then chemical, then cellular

What kind of signal crosses the synaptic cleft in a typical chemical synapse?

Check your thinking

A chemical neurotransmitter crosses the cleft. Receptor binding then changes ion flow or activity in the target cell.

Learn · Part 2

The messenger, receptor, and ending mechanism all matter

Acetylcholine is a neurotransmitter used at many synapses, including the junction between a motor neuron and skeletal muscle. When it binds to receptors there, it helps depolarize the muscle membrane. Cholinesterase breaks acetylcholine down so the stimulation does not continue without limit.

Norepinephrine is another neurotransmitter. It is used by many sympathetic pathways and can change activity in target tissues that have the right receptor. The effect depends on the receptor and cell, not only the messenger's name.

Worked example

What changes if cholinesterase is blocked?

Observation: acetylcholine release is normal, but the receiving cell remains stimulated longer than expected.

  1. Cholinesterase normally breaks acetylcholine down.
  2. Blocking the enzyme leaves more acetylcholine in the cleft for longer.
  3. More receptor binding can continue.
  4. The postsynaptic effect lasts longer than it should.

Conclusion: the problem is signal ending, not neurotransmitter release or myelin.

Advanced LearningExcitation, inhibition, and combined input

A postsynaptic neuron receives many inputs. Some receptor pathways move its membrane potential toward threshold; others move it away. The cell combines these changes across location and time.

An agonist increases a signalling effect by activating a receptor or extending messenger action. An antagonist reduces a signalling effect by blocking a receptor or another required step. These labels describe a mechanism, not whether a substance is safe.

Pancreas and Adrenal Glands

How do the pancreas and adrenal glands coordinate fuel, stress, and salt balance?

Required time
95 minutes
Advanced Learning
25 optional minutes

Before you begin

You should already know negative feedback, target cells, the sympathetic nervous system, and the difference between a gland and a hormone.

In the textbook

Chapter 13 · pp. 453–455 Q18–23, 1, 2; use pp. 456–462 for pancreas and diabetes follow-up

Words for this lesson

Meet the words before the mechanism

pancreatic islet
a cluster of endocrine cells inside the pancreas
insulin and glucagon
hormones with coordinated opposing effects on blood glucose
adrenal cortex and medulla
the outer and inner regions of each adrenal gland
cortisol, epinephrine, aldosterone
adrenal hormones with different targets and timing

Learn · Part 1

The pancreas responds to changing blood glucose

The pancreas contains endocrine clusters called pancreatic islets. Beta cells in the islets release insulin when blood glucose rises. Alpha cells release glucagon when blood glucose falls.

Insulin binds to receptors on specific target cells. It supports glucose uptake in some tissues, glycogen formation in liver and muscle, and reduced glucose output by the liver. Not every tissue responds in the same way. Glucagon acts mainly on the liver, where it supports glycogen breakdown and glucose release.

Concept summary

After glucose rises, beta cells increase insulin release and target tissues change uptake, storage, and production. When glucose falls, alpha cells increase glucagon and the liver increases glucose output. Not every tissue responds to insulin in the same way.

Learn · Part 2

Diabetes changes glucose regulation

Diabetes mellitus is a group of disorders in which blood glucose remains too high. In type 1 diabetes, an autoimmune process destroys pancreatic beta cells, so the body produces little or no insulin. In type 2 diabetes, tissues may respond less effectively to insulin and insulin secretion may also change.

Long-term high blood glucose can damage blood vessels, nerves, kidneys, and eyes. Glucose may appear in urine when blood levels exceed kidney reabsorption capacity. A classroom data set can show patterns, but it cannot diagnose a person. Treatment is individualized and may include insulin, other medications, nutrition, activity, monitoring, and clinical support.

Learn · Part 3

The adrenal gland has two regions with different jobs

Each adrenal gland sits above a kidney. The inner adrenal medulla releases epinephrine and norepinephrine during a rapid sympathetic response. These signals can increase cardiac output, redirect blood flow, and make fuel available for immediate action.

The outer adrenal cortex releases steroid hormones. Cortisol supports a longer stress response and changes fuel metabolism. Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption in the kidneys; water tends to follow sodium, which supports blood volume and pressure. ACTH from the anterior pituitary stimulates cortisol release, while aldosterone is controlled mainly by kidney-linked pressure and ion signals.

Worked example

A meal is followed by a long, stressful afternoon

  2. Target tissues change glucose uptake, storage, and output.

Homeostasis

This concept is taught in Lesson 9. Continue through the learning sequence before using the full explanation.

Meaning

The active maintenance of internal conditions within ranges that allow cells and organs to function, even while conditions inside or outside the body change.

Word structure · morpheme

homeo-
similar or steady
-stasis
standing or condition

Use with care: The word suggests steadiness, but living systems remain dynamic and variable.

What it does

A regulated variable is detected, information is integrated, and effectors adjust the variable. The value may fluctuate; it is not held perfectly still.

Related ideas

regulated variable · set point · negative feedback · dynamic equilibrium

Common confusion

Homeostasis does not mean that every internal value is constant or that a response always restores one exact number.

This concept is taught in Lesson 2. Continue through the learning sequence before using the full explanation.

Meaning

A brief, regenerated reversal and restoration of membrane voltage that begins after threshold and carries information along an axon.
