Start with something familiar

Why are these things made from different materials?

Picture getting ready for a wet autumn day. Your rain jacket needs to shed water. The towel you use afterwards needs to absorb it. Your kitchen spoon needs to hold its shape and withstand repeated washing. A material that is excellent for one job can be a poor choice for another.

Chemistry helps us explain those choices. A property is a characteristic such as strength, flexibility, water resistance or resistance to corrosion. Instead of choosing a material only because it is familiar, we can connect its properties to the job it needs to do.

Over time, people have learned to make substances with useful properties. means made through human-controlled processes. Nylon and polyester are synthetic fibres. Cotton and wool are natural fibres. Both groups contain chemical substances: natural does not mean “chemical-free.”

From small units to useful products

A has large molecules built from repeating smaller units. Think of the repeating links in a chain. The analogy helps you picture repetition; real polymer molecules are much smaller than a visible chain and can have different shapes and arrangements.

Not all polymers are synthetic. Cellulose in cotton and proteins in wool are natural polymers. Polyethylene and nylon are synthetic polymers. Some polymers become fibres that can be spun into fabrics; others become moulded plastics. The word polymer describes a structure, not a promise that every polymer behaves the same way.

An is a metallic mixture. Stainless steel, for example, contains iron along with other elements that change its properties. Chromium, nickel and carbon are examples of elements used to change the properties of steel. The useful idea is not that “mixing makes everything stronger.” It is that changing a material's composition can change which tasks it can perform.

Worked example

Make a choice from evidence, not a label

A school needs fabric for reusable outdoor flags. In this practice comparison, Fabric A absorbs much water and dries slowly. Fabric B absorbs less water, dries quickly and remains strong when wet. Both meet the school's colour requirement.

Before choosing, compare what water does on each fabric. Which behaviour would help an outdoor flag?

  1. Identify the job.The flags will be left outdoors. Their ability to deal with rain matters more here than their ability to absorb water.
  2. Select the relevant evidence.Fabric B's low water absorption and fast drying fit that job. Colour alone cannot distinguish the two choices.
  3. Link property to benefit.Less retained water means the wet fabric is not weighed down as much. Faster drying means it stays wet for less time.
  4. State the limit.These data support choosing B for wet conditions. They do not compare cost, lifespan, manufacturing impacts or end-of-life waste.

Complete explanation: “Fabric B is the better fit for these flags because it retains less rainwater and dries faster. I would still compare lifespan and cost before making the final purchase.” Notice that the explanation names the task, the evidence and a limit.

Spot the error before continuing

Diagnose the reasoning

Chemistry on the farm and in the home

A controls unwanted organisms. It is the broad category. A targets unwanted plants; an targets insects. Those terms are related, but they are not interchangeable. A product appropriate for weeds is not automatically appropriate for an insect problem.

Fertilizers supply nutrients needed by plants. They do not perform the same job as pesticides. The textbook also contrasts synthetic fertilizer with manure, which returns organic matter to soil. You should connect the product to the specific need rather than describe every agricultural chemical as “helping crops grow.”

At home, manufactured soaps and detergents can be designed for particular cleaning tasks. The textbook describes older soap-making using animal fats and ashes, then contrasts this with controlled manufacturing. What changes is the ability to choose ingredients and produce a more consistent product—not the presence or absence of chemistry.

Second worked example

The same property can help one task and hinder another

A fibre that absorbs water well can make a useful towel. That same property can make an outdoor flag heavy in rain. There is no contradiction: the tasks are different.

Start with something familiar

A familiar bottle is not enough information

You begin a part-time job and find a spray bottle beside the sink. Its label is missing. It could contain water, a cleaner or something more hazardous. Recognizing the bottle's shape does not identify its contents.

The useful first decision is to stop and ask the supervisor, not open it to smell it, mix it with another product or test it on your skin. Chemical safety starts by identifying the product and obtaining the right information before exposure occurs.

A is a potential source of harm. Risk also depends on how exposure could happen and how it is controlled. A hazardous product does not become harmless because it is common or sold for household use.

Read the symbol, the words and the instructions

Household hazard symbols and workplace WHMIS pictograms communicate hazards, but they are not the same system. On household chemical labels, the symbol identifies the hazard. The signal words help describe its seriousness. Read the product instructions as well; a symbol alone cannot specify every safe action.

stands for Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System. It combines labels, safety data sheets and worker education and training. Learning a pictogram is useful, but it is only one part of using a product safely.

A product can damage skin, eyes or materials. A flame pictogram warns of specified fire hazards. The flame-over-circle pictogram identifies an oxidizing hazard, which is not the same thing as a flammable fuel. Always use the label's actual hazard statements rather than guess from the picture.

Read the older textbook alongside current terminology. The textbook uses , or Material Safety Data Sheet. Current WHMIS uses , or Safety Data Sheet, and updated pictograms. The historical page remains in the reader; use the current CCOHS reference below for workplace labelling.

Worked example

Turn safety information into a decision

In this fictional training case, a cleaner's label warns of serious eye damage. The workplace procedure says trained staff must use specified eye protection and follow the product SDS. You have not yet been trained.

  1. Identify the harm.The label identifies eye damage. “It is just a cleaner” is not a reason to dismiss that warning.
  2. Find the detailed information.The SDS and workplace procedure identify appropriate controls, handling and first-aid information.
  3. Check readiness.You do not yet have the required training. Reading one hazard symbol does not supply the missing training.
  4. Choose the action.Do not begin. Ask the supervisor for the correct procedure and training.

The answer is a reasoned action, not a list of protective equipment guessed from memory. Different products and tasks can require different controls.

Second worked example

When two labels are not the same system

A household product has a hazard symbol in a frame; a workplace product uses a red diamond WHMIS pictogram. The products may warn of a similar hazard, but their labels follow different systems. Do not transfer the meaning of a frame from one system to the other.

For current Canadian household chemical labels, a triangular frame indicates a container hazard and an octagonal frame indicates a contents hazard. The signal word indicates the degree of risk. This is why “look only at the outline” is an incomplete strategy.

A sound decision: identify which system you are reading, interpret the hazard symbol, read the words, and follow the product and workplace instructions. Never mix products merely because both are cleaners.

Start with something familiar

What makes dough rise?

Before baking, bread dough can increase in volume even though no extra flour is added. Yeast uses sugars and produces new substances, including carbon dioxide gas. Gas becomes trapped in the dough and helps it expand.

To explain this, “the dough gets bigger” is an observation, not the full chemical story. We need to identify the starting substances, the new substances and how a new substance produces the visible effect.

A changes substances into other substances. The starting substances are s. The substances formed are s. A product can have very different properties from the reactants that produced it.

Follow matter across the reaction arrow

A reaction arrow points from reactants to products. Read it as “react to form” or “produce.” It is not an equals sign saying that the substances on the two sides are identical.

reactants → productsBefore the reaction → substances formed by the reaction

In a simple representation of yeast fermentation, sugar produces ethanol and carbon dioxide. This is a simplified chemical account of one process involved in making bread, not a complete list of everything in dough. Flour, water, yeast and salt have roles, but a recipe's ingredient list is not automatically a balanced chemical equation.

Likewise, a baker, pan and oven can affect how the process is carried out, but they are not automatically consumed reactants. Ask what substance is chemically transformed, rather than listing every object near the reaction.

Worked example

Connect the product to what you observe

Consider the explanation: “Yeast helps dough rise because it makes gas from sugars.” We can make that explanation more precise.

  1. Identify the chemical change.Yeast carries out reactions involving sugars. New substances form.
  2. Name the relevant product.Carbon dioxide is the gas important to expansion.
  3. Link the product to the physical effect.The gas becomes trapped in the dough, increasing the volume of its gas pockets.
  4. Keep the claim within the evidence.This explains an important contribution to rising. It does not mean all dough volume is carbon dioxide or that every ingredient has turned into gas.

Complete explanation: “Yeast converts sugars into products that include carbon dioxide. The gas is trapped in the dough, so the dough expands.” This has a cause-and-effect connection instead of only naming a reaction.

Spot the error before continuing

Diagnose the reasoning

Useful reactions do not all look alike

involves rapid reaction with oxygen and release of energy. In complete methane combustion, methane and oxygen are reactants; carbon dioxide and water are products. Oxygen matters even though it comes from the air rather than a fuel bottle.

A reaction involves an acid and a base. An antacid reacts with excess stomach acid. Later lessons will distinguish acid–hydroxide reactions, which form salt and water, from carbonate reactions, which can also form carbon dioxide.

A reaction's usefulness depends on what its products and energy changes do. Carbon dioxide helps bread rise, while excess carbon dioxide added to the atmosphere changes Earth's energy balance. The same substance can have different consequences in different situations.

Supported practice

Separate ingredients, reactants and products

Look for the substance that is transformed or formed. Do not answer from whether a substance sounds useful.

Second worked example

Explain a change that is not dramatic

An enzyme breaks a large food molecule into smaller molecules during digestion. There may be no flame, loud sound or visible colour change. Yet it is a chemical reaction because the molecules formed are different from the starting molecule.

Compare this with cutting food into smaller pieces. Cutting changes the size of the pieces, but does not by itself establish that new chemical substances formed. “Smaller” can describe either pieces or molecules; the distinction matters.

When you explain a reaction, use this sequence: name what starts, name what forms, then connect that change to the observed or useful effect.

Start with something familiar

Two beakers bubble. Did both react?

One beaker contains water being heated to boiling. A second contains two solutions that produce a gas when mixed. Both show bubbles. Only one description clearly involves the formation of a new substance.

Boiling changes liquid water into water vapour; both are H₂O. Gas formed by a reaction can be a new substance. An observation is a clue. An explanation tells you why the clue supports a chemical change.

You cannot reliably classify every change from a single visual feature. Ask whether the evidence supports formation of new substances, and consider physical explanations that might account for the same observation.

Worked example

Make a claim that matches the evidence

Practice observations: two clear solutions are mixed in a clean container. No heating or evaporation occurs. A cloudy white solid appears and settles. The starting solutions did not contain visible suspended solids.

  1. State what was observed.A new solid appeared after the solutions were mixed.
  2. Explain why it matters.The result is consistent with an insoluble product forming from dissolved reactants.
  3. Name the process.The solid is a precipitate. Its formation is evidence of a chemical reaction.
  4. Do not claim more than you know.Colour alone does not identify the exact compound. Further information is needed to name it.

Complete explanation: “The appearance of a solid from the two clear solutions supports a chemical change because a new insoluble product formed. These observations do not identify the product's chemical name.”

Spot the error before continuing

Diagnose the reasoning

Observation, inference and a useful comparison

“Bubbles formed” is an observation. “Carbon dioxide formed” is an inference unless an appropriate identification test or other evidence is provided. Keep those two levels separate in your explanation.

The textbook describes standard classroom gas tests: a glowing splint can relight in oxygen, hydrogen can produce a characteristic pop under a trained teacher's test, and carbon dioxide can make limewater cloudy. You need to interpret these supplied results; you are not being asked to collect gases or perform flame tests at home.

A comparison can strengthen the interpretation. If the same amount of water under the same conditions does not show the change, that helps rule out some alternatives. It does not automatically eliminate every possible explanation.

Supported practice

Second worked example

When a change of colour is not enough

Adding water to blue food colouring produces a paler blue solution. No other observation is supplied. A student concludes that the colouring reacted to make a new pale-blue chemical.

The missing step is evidence of a new substance. The same particles can simply be spread through more water. A careful answer is: “The paler colour can be explained by dilution, so this observation alone does not establish a chemical reaction.”

Being cautious is not the same as refusing to decide. In the precipitation example, several observations supported a chemical change. In the dilution example, a straightforward physical explanation fits the evidence.

Start with something familiar

Why can a reaction warm your hand?

An air-activated hand warmer feels warm because energy transfers from the reacting material to its surroundings, including your hand. The warmth is not a new substance. It is evidence of energy moving.

To classify an energy change, first decide what you call the . Here, it is the reacting material. The are everything outside that chosen system. Without that boundary, “energy goes in” is ambiguous: into what?

An reaction transfers energy from the reacting system to the surroundings. An reaction takes energy into the reacting system from the surroundings or another energy source.

Interpret the energy pathway

Use the reacting system as your reference

The words describe the overall transfer. A reaction can require energy to start and still release more energy overall. A match starts a fuel burning; that does not make the continuing combustion endothermic.

Also, a temperature change does not prove that the change is chemical. Some physical processes, including dissolving, absorb or release energy. Use evidence about new substances as well as energy direction.

Read a temperature change carefully

Spot the error before continuing

Diagnose the reasoning

Why do rearrangements transfer energy?

During reactions, bonds in reactants are broken and new bonds form in products. Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases energy. The overall result depends on the balance between these contributions.

You do not need bond-energy calculations in this lesson. Use the idea to avoid a common mistake: breaking a bond is not, by itself, the step that releases energy. In an exothermic reaction, formation of the new arrangement releases more energy than is needed for the changes that precede it.

Start with something familiar

What changes when energy leaves more slowly?

A warm room cools when energy transfers to colder surroundings. Insulation changes the rate of transfer; it does not create a new energy source. This is a useful starting comparison for understanding Earth's energy balance, although an atmosphere does not work exactly like house insulation.

Sunlight supplies energy to Earth. Earth's surface absorbs some of it and emits energy as infrared radiation. es absorb and emit some of that infrared radiation. Their presence changes how readily energy escapes to space.

The is a natural process. Without it, Earth's surface would be much colder. The question about present-day change is not whether the natural process exists; it is what happens when the amounts of greenhouse gases change.

Trace the energy in the right order

  1. Incoming sunlight reaches Earth's atmosphere and surface; some is reflected.
  2. The surface absorbs energy and warms.
  3. The surface emits infrared radiation.
  4. Greenhouse gases absorb and emit infrared radiation, including toward the surface.
  5. Energy still escapes to space. The surface and atmosphere adjust toward an energy balance.

Do not picture an airtight lid trapping every ray forever. Greenhouse gases interact with radiation. The textbook uses a glass-greenhouse comparison; that comparison is limited because a glass greenhouse also limits air movement.

Carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour are greenhouse gases. Burning carbon-containing fuels adds carbon dioxide. A substance can be naturally present and still cause changes when additional amounts enter the system.

Worked example

Use an energy-balance model without inventing a measurement

These are illustrative model units, not measured Earth data. Imagine 100 units of energy are absorbed during an interval and 100 units escape. The model is balanced.

  1. Identify the starting balance.100 units in − 100 units out = 0 net gain.
  2. Change the outgoing transfer initially.Suppose a change in the atmosphere initially reduces outgoing energy to 98 units while 100 still enter.
  3. Find the imbalance.100 − 98 = 2 units gained during that interval. The system's stored energy increases.
  4. Explain the adjustment.A warmer Earth emits more infrared radiation. Temperature changes until outgoing energy can again balance incoming energy under the changed conditions.

This explains why changing the atmosphere can affect temperature without changing the energy supplied by the Sun. It does not calculate a real warming rate or a temperature from these invented units.

Start with something familiar

A pollutant can travel before its effects appear

Rain falls on a lake far from a power plant. That distance does not prove that the air above the lake is unaffected by emissions. Gases and particles can move through the atmosphere, react, and later reach the surface.

includes acid-forming substances reaching land or water through wet deposition, such as rain or snow, and through dry deposition. “Acid rain” describes only the wet part.

The textbook focuses on sulfur oxides and nitrogen oxides. They can react in the atmosphere and contribute to acids. The important chain is source → transport and reaction → deposition → change in soil or water, not simply “smoke turns into rain.”

Use pH to describe the water—not to name the acid

The scale describes how acidic or basic an aqueous solution is. In the classroom examples here, values below 7 are acidic, 7 is neutral at about room temperature, and values above 7 are basic. Moving from pH 6 to pH 4 means the solution is becoming more acidic.

pH does not tell you the identity of the acid. Two solutions can have the same pH while containing different substances. Nor can you decide whether an unknown chemical is safe merely from a familiar pH example.

Worked example

Explain a pH change without overclaiming

Practice data: comparable samples from a small water system change from pH 6.2 before an input to pH 4.8 afterwards. No chemical identity test is supplied.

Complete explanation: “The water became more acidic. Acidic material entering the system could explain this, but pH alone does not identify the substance or its source.”

Spot the error before continuing

Diagnose the reasoning

Neutralization uses a reaction, not a disappearance

An acid reacting with a hydroxide base can form a salt and water. For example, hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water. The acid and base are transformed; matter does not disappear.

A carbonate or bicarbonate base can also produce carbon dioxide. Limestone, containing calcium carbonate, can react with acidic material. The textbook calls the addition of limestone to acidic environments .

In either case, the final pH depends on the substances and their relative amounts. Adding “some base” does not guarantee exactly pH 7. Too little may leave excess acid; excess base can make the final mixture basic. These are explanations of supplied cases, not instructions for treating a lake or mixing household products.

Treatment is not the same as prevention

A lake's acidity is reduced by liming. The upstream emissions remain unchanged. The treatment has addressed a consequence in the lake, but it has not removed the source of future acid-forming material.

Trace the original chain. Emissions still enter the atmosphere; transport and reactions can still occur; new deposition can reach the lake. This is why an explanation should distinguish a treatment acting after deposition from a technology acting before release.

Worked example

Choose a mechanism that actually targets sulfur emissions

Spot the error before continuing

Diagnose the reasoning

Measure a reduction with a fair denominator

If a question provides before-and-after emission data, the reduction is the starting amount minus the ending amount. A percentage reduction compares that difference with the starting amount:

percentage reduction = (before − after) ÷ before × 100%

This is a practice tool for interpreting supplied data, not a claim that every real facility has the same performance. Use the same units and operating conditions in both measurements. If a plant produces half as much output after the change, a lower total emission does not by itself identify how much improvement came from the device.

Always keep the measured pollutant in your conclusion. “Sulfur dioxide fell by 75% under these test conditions” is more precise than “pollution fell by 75%.”

Start with something familiar

Why does a scratch matter?

A painted steel railing can look intact for years, then develop rust around a scratch. The paint did not turn into rust. Damage to the coating allowed conditions at the steel surface to change.

is chemical deterioration of a material through reactions with its surroundings. is the corrosion of iron that produces iron-containing oxides and hydroxides. Other metals can corrode, but their corrosion products are not all called rust.

Rusting requires access to oxygen and water. Dissolved salts can accelerate the process. That is why a wet, salty environment can be more damaging to exposed steel than a dry environment.

Protect the metal by interrupting the process

A , such as paint or suitable grease, reduces contact between the metal and oxygen or water. A continuous coating can work well. A scratch or gap can expose the underlying iron.

Another approach changes the material. Alloying iron with elements such as chromium can improve corrosion resistance. Stainless steel resists corrosion under many conditions, but its name is not a promise that no chemical damage is ever possible.

coats steel or iron with zinc. Zinc can act as a barrier and can also corrode preferentially, protecting the underlying iron. A separate attached can work on the same general principle. Suitable contact and conditions are necessary; placing any random metal nearby is not enough.

Worked example

Explain a failed barrier

Practice observations: two identical steel plates are painted. Plate A's coating is intact. Plate B has a deep scratch exposing steel. Both are left under the same wet conditions. Rust develops first along B's scratch.

  1. Identify the changed condition.The scratch exposes steel in B. The metal and external conditions are otherwise stated to be the same.
  2. Name the coating's job.Paint acts as a barrier that reduces oxygen and water reaching the surface.
  3. Link the damage to the reaction.The exposed area can contact oxygen and water, allowing rusting.
  4. Keep the claim bounded.The result supports a protective role for an intact barrier. It does not show that every paint or all coatings last equally long.

Complete explanation: “Rust first forms at the scratch because the protective barrier is broken there. Oxygen and water can contact the exposed iron.”

Spot the error before continuing

Diagnose the reasoning

A sacrificial metal is meant to react

The word sacrificial helps explain the design. The protecting metal reacts preferentially and is gradually consumed. Its deterioration can mean that it is doing its intended job, not that it is useless.

This differs from an ordinary paint barrier. Some protection can remain when a zinc coating has a small scratch, because zinc can protect exposed iron under suitable conditions. A paint coating has no equivalent supply of sacrificial metal.

Because the sacrificial material is used up, maintenance and replacement matter. No protection should be described as permanent merely because it initially works.

Second worked example

Why can the corrosion product have more mass?

A cleaned sample of iron starts at 12 g. Later, 15 g of corrosion product is recovered. In this simplified accounting example, no material has been lost and all the gain comes from matter taken in from the surroundings.

The gain is 15 − 12 = 3 g. It is not new matter created by rusting. Oxygen and other material involved in the corrosion process have entered the measured sample.

Do not confuse “metal is being damaged” with “the collected product must weigh less.” Damage is a change in material properties and structure. Conservation concerns the total matter, including matter supplied from outside the original piece of metal.

This connects corrosion back to the balancing lesson: choose the system boundary before interpreting a mass change.
