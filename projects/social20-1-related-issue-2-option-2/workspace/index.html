Unit 2: Should nations pursue national interests?

Big Ideas:

  • Nations and nation-states pursue national interests in various ways.
  • Nationalism can affect the decision to pursue national interests.
  • Nationalism can affect how national interests are pursued.

Introduction

Remember that in Social Studies you must base opinions on careful research, evidence and thought.  Sometimes, this is uncomfortable because your beliefs or opinions may be challenged by the evidence as you examine history. 

Don’t worry!  This is a common state of the strong Social Studies student. John F. Kennedy said, “too often we … enjoy the comfort of opinion without the discomfort of thought.”  

This unit will challenge you to explore the relationship between nationalism, ultrantionalism, national interest and foreign policy in order to understand the issue question:

Should nations pursue national interests?

As you work through the unit and complete the assignments you will formulate a position based on research, evidence and reflection in your examination of the question above. To answer this question we need to know what national interest is, how it is pursued, and how it relates to foreign policy, nationalism and ultranationalism.  Therefore, the following essential questions will guide you through your inquiry and provide the background knowledge and understandings to help you answer the related issue question above:

  • What are interests?
  • What are national interests?
  • What is foreign policy?
  • How have nations pursued national interests?
  • How does nationalism shape foreign policy?
  • Is nationalism a negative force?
  • Is all foreign policy positive?
  • What is ultrantionalism?
  • What is national self determination?

We will explore these questions in the context of Imperialism, World War I, the interwar years, World War II and the contemporary world.

By the end of this unit, you will be able to discuss the degree to which you think national interests should be pursued.  Your position on this issue should reflect your study of historical and contemporary pursuits of national interest. Another key question for you to consider in forming your position is whether national interest should be controlled and, if so, by what or whom.

As a citizen of any country, you are or should be aware that conflicts are occurring at many locations in the world today (see multimedia below). National identities have determined how nations choose to pursue their national interests, and their pursuit of national interests may or may not cause them to have conflicts with other nations.  Canada is involved in many of these conflicts, and Canada’s involvement today affects the Canada and world you will live in tomorrow.

Competing Personal interests 

Imagine your parents have decided to take a trip to a small town to visit the statue of a musician you have never heard of, and they are demanding you go. They believe this experience is of great value. In order for you to go on the trip, though, you have to take time off work, and this means you are not going to have enough money at the end of the month to pay your cell phone bill.

  • Do you go?
  • If you don’t go, what types of problems might this cause in your family?
  • Do you feel confident that your interest in keeping your phone is worth the problems?

Nations and nation-states sometimes act like parents; they have goals that work for the betterment of their children, or their citizens. Think about the following:

  • Just as with people and their competing goals, what do nations do when their goals come into conflict with another nation’s goals?
  • What are their options—peaceful relations or tension and conflict?

In this section you will explore how national interests led to conflict, the interests held by nations, and the reasons why nations held these interests.

Personal Interest

The rewards and/or punishments you receive will determine your decisions. This focuses you on what is in the situation or action for you.  You might do things your peers like, but your parents dislike.  You might do things that you would not normally have done in the past to be part of the group you belong to now.  You might choose family over friends and become isolated from your peers.  You might even develop a strong dislike for others based on your experiences with them.  Still another possibility is that you will need to choose to belong to one group over another. You might choose to be part of a sports team or part of a group that prefers not to compete in sports. 

Teens often wonder “What should I be when I leave school?"  This decision is formed by the experiences you have and the decisions you make. Whatever you decide, you will learn that every decision has consequences and that people around you will react to you based on their history with you.  You will also learn, much to your dismay, that there is often no easy solution, no clear direction on which way to go. This is called a dilemma and dilemmas follow you as you move into and through life.  However, do not worry—confusion is normal and a sign of reflection!

What are your personal interests and how do you intend to pursue them?

Personal interests can be the things you are interested in, such as shopping, dancing, sports or listening to your favourite music. But there is more to it than that. What are your hopes and dreams for the future? Where do you want to be in 5-10 years? How do you plan to make that happen? Pursuing your interests is about making and following a plan to realise those interests. Will you step on anybody who you think is competing with you or will you work cooperatively with people who have similar interests so that you can achieve your goals?

Tip - understand the terms

Review the definitions by clicking on the words: peacekeeping, peace-enforcement and peacemaking.

These terms mean different things - yet even experts get them confused* (see below).

One way to help remember the difference is to reverse the combined words:

Peacekeeping becomes "keeping the peace". This implies that there is a peace to keep - there has to be a peace agreement or ceasefire in place; and the previously warring sides invite peacekeepers to monitor the peace they agreed to - for as long as it takes until a permanent reconciliation is reached; and peacekeeping soldiers are only authorized to use force to defend themselves.

Peacemaking* becomes "making peace". This implies that there is no peace, or a violent conflict is looming. When two friends are fighting, you may not be able to use force to stop them but you might be able to use rational (or emotional) appeals to persuade your fighting friends to "make peace". Diplomacy is the tool used at the international level to "make peace" between warring sides or before a serious conflict erupts into violence.

Peace-enforcement becomes "enforcing peace", or just "forcing peace". A neutral or third party can enter a conflict to impose peace between two warring sides. No invitation is required but some kind of authorization from an international organization, like the UN, is expected. Peace-enforcement allows for the use of offensive military operations to protect civilians or to destroy the military capabilities of one or both sides in a conflict; it can be used to stop or prevent acts of genocide or crimes against humanity by one or both sides in a conflict or to stop a government from persecuting its own people.

*Please note: Your textbook and Alberta Education use the term "peacemaking" when describing peace-enforcement. These two terms have their own meaning in international organizations, such as the UN (search the UN website) and academia. Therefore, the international definitions above are used in this course to prepare you for university and/or careers in the international field. However, if you encounter or need to use these terms in an Ablerta Diploma exam, explain what you mean when you use either of these terms because the Provincial Examiner marking your Diploma Exam may be informed by the Alberta Education Programme of Studies rather than international and specialized academic norms. But in this course, I expect you to use the terms used by professionals. Know the difference.

Note: Many Canadians claim to be proud of Canada's peacekeeping role in the world. This pride is based on past glories, not present realities. Since the 1990's, Canada is no longer a significant contributor of Canadian soldiers to UN peacekeeping missions. Since the 1990s, the focus of Canadian governments has changed to peace-enforcement missions (Kosovo; Libya) and war (Afghanistan from 2002-2019; Iraq - present). Prime Minister Justin Trudeau has said Canada should return to peacekeeping but, at the time of writing, has yet to commit to a UN peacekeeping operation.

What is national interest?

Required Reading

Please read Chapter 5 pages 116-119 in the Exploring Nationalism textbook.

 

National interest

Like you, nations (actually, the governments of nations) have an idea about who they are and what they need to be content.  Nations pursue their national interests through foreign policy, which has been formed by its interaction with other nations of the world. 

Past events may have caused a nation to isolate itself from other nations, or to pursue, in the most extreme cases, a xenophobic approach to the rest of the world.  Other nations, such as Canada, have been involved in peacekeeping and active partnerships with other nations in the global community. 

As in your own interests, national interests can change over time. Canada has an international reputation as a peacekeeping nation but today it is no longer a leading contributor of troops to UN peacekeeping operations. Canada's emphasis in recent years has shifted to peacemaking and peace-enforcement operations because governments have decided that is what is in the national interest. Should Canada abandon UN peacekeeping in favour of NATO peace-enforcement? By the end of this course, you should be prepared to answer that question.

Please click here to better understand the difference between these terms. You'll be expected to demonstrate this understanding in an upcoming assignment.

Something to Consider

You may find it useful to take the concepts of national interest and international relationships to an individual level.

For example, your personal goals and motivations affect the way you interact with the world around you.

If you are playing a game of soccer and want the ball to be passed to you, you place yourself in a position to take control of it from an opposing player. This can be compared to the competition for markets and resources that occur between countries.

Your personal beliefs and ideals affect your perspective and the choices you make. These can include your religous and political ideologies. Just as your personal ideologies contribute to how you live your life, the dominant ideologies of a country shape their national interests.

If you feel pressure to receive "straight A's" on your report card, or to be the "star" of the basketball team, this will affect how you spend your day. You may choose studying and practicing rather than spending time with friends. Pressure and prestige can also be an integral aspect of a country's national interest which affects their relationships with other countries.

If a friend supports you only when it is beneficial to them, the way you perceive and interact with them is affected. Developing countries can find themselves in similar situations when accepting tied aid from developed countries.

Let's say you and a friend are afraid of a bully in your class. To feel safer, you both decide to stick together to protect eachother. This changes the nature of your relationship. One or both of you may become more aggressive toward the bully knowing that you are stronger with your friend. On an international level, countries also come to the defense of one another. The national security interest can create defensive alliances which then affect international relations. Countries may become more aggressive if they know they have an alliance to back them up.

So you see how your interests affect your interaction with others and how national interests affect international relationships.

Foreign policy

Foreign policy is the methods used by a nation-state to pursue its interests. These methods can include diplomacy, agreements and treaties, participation in international organisations, cultural exchanges, foreign aid, trade policies, and military force.

How do I know what the national interests of a country are?  A good question!  National interests are pursued through a nation-state’s foreign policy.  In Canada, the government agency responsible for handling foreign policy is the Department of Global Affairs (formerly Foreign Affairs, Trade and Development Canada).

The Global Affairs website indicates it's responsibilities. Can you tie national interests in to the 4 headings on the website? Statements of intention are one thing but the best indicator of national interest is the way a nation state interacts with other nation states. Actions speak louder than words!

Here's an example of how Canadian foreign policy relates to national interests:

Canada had a trade agreement with the USA and Mexico: the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), now CUSMA for Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement. This is an example of Canada pursuing one national interest - securing markets for Canadian products.  A second national interest, good relationships with the countries that share our continent, is another reason trade agreements have been signed with the USA and Mexico. A third interest is encouraging an accountable and consistent multilateral system.

National interest shapes foreign policy:

Are you likely to read in a newspaper that Canada has attacked another country to secure more land?  No!  Canada has long pursued a policy of cooperation and, until the 1990s, involvement in peacekeeping actions around the world.  To secure more land is not in our national interests, and Canada is fortunate to have an abundance of mineral and natural resources.  Canadian identity is not based on a history of aggression towards other countries. Canada is a member of the NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organization) military alliance and was involved in such missions as ISAF (International Security Assistance Force) in Afghanistan (see the reading assignment above).

Would Canada go to war if the USA or Britain was attacked by another nation-state? We did in the case of Afghanistan as you'll see below.  Canada has a long history with both the USA and Britain. All three nation-states share the belief that democracy is the best form of government.  Another reason we might become involved is that Canada has supported USA and Britain in past wars, and showing continued loyalty to allies is considered by many to be in Canada’s national interests, especially if they still share our values.  Also, Canada has strong economic ties with the USA.  Some political leaders in Canada believe that supporting the USA in wartime is in Canada’s best interest because Canada benefits from the goodwill of the world’s most powerful military nation with whom we share national borders.

Alliance committments

Canada, USA, Britain and 23 other European countries belong to the NATO military alliance which compels member nations to come to the aid of other member nations if attacked. The terrorist organisation responsible for 9-11 was allegedly based in Afghanistan (indicated in red on the map). The Afghan government of the day (the Taliban) allowed the terrorists to operate freely in Afghanistan. As such, the Afghan government was seen as partly responsible for the 9-11 attacks and NATO members were therefore obligated to act, including Canada.

In the case of the US-led Iraq war, however, NATO (hence Canada) was not obligated to join the American invasion of Iraq because that country did not attack the USA. So Canada stayed out of that war. The NATO alliance is meant to be defensive in nature. Some NATO members - and non-members - did participate in the Iraq war but this was not under NATO command. This may change now with the rise of the ISIL (ISIS) insurgency in Iraq, with Canada sending Canadian soldiers as "advisors", trainers and combat support to the Kurds in the north resisting ISIL.

Big idea:  Canada’s national interests are often connected with the national interests of other nation-states.  As you will learn, sometimes the connections or alliances a country forms can draw it into major conflicts, such as World War One, World War Two and Afghanistan today.  You fight me, you fight all my friends! Is there a better way?

Do all nation-states pursue national interests in the same way Canada does?  No. Nation states in other parts of the world have different national interests.  Sometimes these interests are based on historical events; sometimes they evolve when a new leader takes power.  Unlike Canada, some nation states want more land or resources and are willing to use force to get them.  Just as Canada is a unique nation state, so are the other nation-states of the world.

Is one way of pursuing national interests better than another?  That’s a very difficult question to answer and one that you need to think about in this unit and for the Canada and world you want for yourself and your children.  You will be examining the ways nation-states in the early 1900s pursued their national interests and the outcome of those methods.  You need to keep an open mind, and you need to be prepared to put yourself in a time period quite different from the world you now live in.

The Age of Imperialism in Africa (1880-1914)

In other parts of the world, European imperialism had been a fact of life for hundreds of years. In 1870, however, the African interior was still an unmapped part of the world. There were several attempts by Europeans to explore Africa's interior prior to the Age of Imperialism, but these were fraught with danger and few explorers survived. Exploration was hindered by unfamiliar peoples and conditions. Africans resisted the European advance into their territories. Malaria, the mosquito-borne virus, took European lives. As a result, prior to the 1880s, the European presence was limited to just the coasts of Africa.

With the discovery of quinine to treat the symptoms of malaria, and the development of modern firearms, the European exploration and conquest of Africa became possible. Weapons such as breech-loading rifles and hand-cranked machine guns gave Europeans military superiority over the relatively poorly-armed Africans.

The years 1880-1914 marked the rapid colonization of Africa by European powers. The rush to claim African territory by Europeans became known as the Scramble for Africa.

European countries vied for African territory and divided the continent into colonies and protectorates. As unoccupied territories grew more scarce, tension between the imperial powers grew. The Fashoda Crisis in Sudan is an example of this. When the imperialist armies of Britain and France met in Sudan in 1898, both laid claim to the same territory. In their dispute over ownership, the two empires nearly went to war. Imperialist competition for colonies increased tensions between the European powers in the years leading up to the war.

Ultranationalism and Imperialism

One of the dangers of nationalism is that it can easily develop into ultranationalism and give rise to empire-building. Nationalism is defined as pride in one's nation or the desire to serve and benefit one's country. Ultranationalism is defined as excessive pride or devotion to one's nation. The imperialist, by definition, wants to increase the power and wealth of his or her nation and has little regard for the national or human rights of the peoples conquered or exploited. To varying degrees, all of Europe's powerful countries had come under the sway of ultranationalism and imperialism by 1914.

Nineteenth-century imperialism was driven by nationalism. With the exceptions of South Africa and Egypt, Europe's African colonies were generally not profitable. The cost of exploration, conquest, development, and defence of the colonial territories usually exceeded the revenue from resources, taxation, and trade. In the Age of Imperialism, European nations measured their greatness by the size of their overseas empires.

The British considered themselves the greatest nation on earth because, as was often said, "the sun never set on the British empire." That is, when the sun was setting in one colony, it was rising in British colonies on the other side of the world. British imperial holdings were greater than any of the other European states and this stoked a sense of British superiority over the other nations of Europe.

Europeans at this time also had a sense of racial superiority toward their non-white colonial subjects. Supporters of British imperialism believed it was their responsibility to impose European culture and values on their "uncivilized" colonial subjects - as if they were children incapable of looking after their own interests.

The instability in Africa today is partly a legacy of European imperialism.

European imperialists in the late nineteenth century determined the borders of African countries. Borders were drawn with little regard for natural or traditional boundaries between African tribes. The nationalism of an imperialist power gives an advantage over people who do not yet have a fully-developed sense of national consciousness. In the long term, however, subject people may develop their own brand of nationalism and demand independence from their imperial masters. By the late 1960s, most African countries had been decolonized (see the interactive map several pages ago). Some European powers, however, continued to exert a powerful influence over their former colonies well into the late twentieth century.

Long after African countries achieved independence from Europe, the legacy of the continent's colonization can still be seen. Most African nations have unresolved border disputes. In many countries the remnants of European divide-and-rule policies are still felt in the domination of majority groups by powerful minorities. The European powers, as they imposed their administrations on a continent, carved out states where none had existed. Militarism is the pursuit of national interests through military means. This implies building up a country's armed forces. Military officers also have greater influence in government.

Competing interests on the continent and in overseas territories were creating tensions between Europe's most powerful countries. And the Industrial Revolution enabled the European powers to manufacture more destructive weaponry and in greater numbers than ever before possible. It was against this background that an arms race began.

German Militarism

Until 1871, Germany was a loose confederation of independent kingdoms and principalities (like Prussia, Bavaria and Saxony). Otto von Bismarck had unified Germany into a single nation-state in 1871. As the Emperor's appointed President/Chancellor of Germany, he led the new country into the twentieth century.  He believed that a combination of coercion and alliances would allow Germany to remain strong and at peace. "The great questions of the day will not be decided by speeches and the resolutions of majorities — that was the great mistake from 1848 to 1849 — but by iron and blood.” 

He conquered those who opposed him (in 1870 he defeated France - in 6 months!), he maintained close ties with those he did not want to fight (Russia), or he entered into alliances so the group could control any would-be aggressors. He also exerted tight control over his ally Austria.

By 1914, the Germany that had earlier been managed by Bismarck was under new leadership.When Wilhelm II became the new Emperor of Germany and King of Prussia, he wanted to pursue his own policies.  He dismissed Bismarck and set Germany on a course of rapid industrial and military expansion, distanced himself from from Russia and gave Austria-Hungary a 'blank cheque' to deal with Serbia.

What did this mean in the years leading up to WWI?

  • Germany and Britain became involved in an arms race. Germany’s militarism could not be ignored by Britain.
  • Germany entered into alliances with Italy and Austria-Hungary when its relationship with Russia weakened.
  • Germany’s increased militarism led to a weakened relationship with Russia, France and Britain.
  • Germany was more willing to use force to resolve conflicts because of its strong military power and past successes with the use of military force (ie. quickly defeating France in 1870).

Ironically, Bismarck was accurate in the warnings he delivered in his last meeting with Wilhelm II.  He said: "Bulgaria, that little country between the Danube and the Balkans, is far from being an object of adequate importance… for which to plunge Europe from Moscow to the Pyrenees, and from the North Sea to Palermo, into a war whose issue no man can foresee. At the end of the conflict we should scarcely know why we had fought."

And…"If there is ever another war in Europe, it will come out of some damned foolish thing in the Balkans."

 

 

British Militarism

Britain, meanwhile, had maintained a policy of isolation from European affairs throughout much of the 19th century (1800s). This began to change with the defeat of France in the Franco-Prussian war of 1870 and the emergence of Germany as a growing industrial and trading power to rival Britain. The changing balance of power led Britain to change its policy of isolation and to increase the size of the Navy and to seek military alliances.

For most of the nineteenth century (1800s), Britain's navy was more powerful than the navies of any two nations combined. In 1898, Germany began a program of naval expansion designed to challenge British naval strength. Between 1900 and 1914, Germany tripled its spending on warships, making the new German nation-state the second strongest naval power in the world and the most efficient army. Britain saw this as a threat and responded by increasing its own naval spending and building new heavily armed battleships. Germany then started building similar battleships. This arms race added to the growing tensions between the European powers.

As in any race, you don't want your opponent to get ahead of you. Note in the graph which country produced more dreadnoughts in 1908-1909. What was the response of the other country in the following years?

French Militarism

France meanwhile increased the size of its army in the hope of regaining the provinces of Alsace and Lorraine which it had lost to Germany in 1870.

Serbian militarism

Serbian nationalism was at its height.  Revolt against the Ottoman Empire in the previous decades had resulted in independence for Serbia. Based on this past experience, aggressive action had the potential to fulfill the Serbian goal of a larger Panslavic state (a nation-state for all the South Slavic peoples, including Croatia, Bosnia and Slovenia - all part of the Austro-Hungarian empire - and independent Montenegro). Achieving this by any means was seen to be in Serbia's national interest. Past experience suggested that a successful way to achieve their interests was through military means so Serbia built up a relatively large army for such a small nation.

Balance of Power

Military strength, or fear of it, kept Europe in a "balance of power" since the defeat of Napoleon.  With the rise of the new German nation-state, the balance was changing. Like the arms race, the alliance system was an attempt to maintain that balance. The problem is that such balances are very delicate things.  As with a circus performer walking a tightrope, one careless step or moment of inattention can be fatal. Any crisis could upset the uneasy balance of power and lead to war. Militarism meant that everyone was preparing for war. War was therefore expected and countries expecting war tend to spend even more on the military.

What were they thinking?!?!

Wars in Europe for the preceding 40 years had been short. Protagonists from all sides were confident that any war would be quick and decisive. War was expected, but the war they got was not the quick and decisive war they had envisioned. It is not uncommon in history for countries to prepare to fight the last war and then find themselves unprepared for a new different kind of conflict.

Serbian nationalism

Many states of the nineteenth century did not conform to the strict definition of a nation-state. Their borders often contained more than one nationality and/or they did not contain all of the dominant nationality. This pattern re-emerged in the Balkans in the 1990s during the wars in the former Yugoslavia. The nationalism in 1914 aimed to unify all the southern Slavic peoples in one nation-state ("Yugo" translates to "south"). After WWI, in 1918, the independent nation-state of Yugoslavia was created. In 1991, Yugoslavia was torn apart by even more narrow divisions. It was religious/linquistic/cultural nationalism that was used more recently to divide the southern Slavic people as each group tried to gain or secure territory where Slavic people of the same religion lived (Croatians tended to be Catholic; Serbs Orthodox; and Bosnia more mixed, including a large Muslim population). Nationalism and the European powers

In their rush to annex new territories, the major imperialist powers of Europe came into conflict with one another. Russia, Austria, and Italy, for example, all wanted to take advantage of the recent retreat of the Ottoman empire from the Balkan peninsula.

This 1908 French cartoon shows the Austria-Hungarian Emperor and Prince Ferdinand of Bulgaria tearing away parts of the decaying Ottoman Empire.

Austria-Hungary was a multi-nation empire. It contained many different nationalities, each living in their own geographical regions of the empire, which increasingly wanted their own independent nation-states. If the Austrians allowed Serb nationalism to go unchecked, where would it have ended? Hungarians, Poles, Czechs, Slovaks, Ukrainians, Croats, Slovenes, and other ethnic/linquistic groups within the country would press their nationalistic demands too. Nationalism threatened to break apart the multi-national Austro-Hungarian Empire.

Nationalists in other countries (including Serbia) incited their fellow nationals inside the Austria-Hungarian Empire to rebel against their overlords. In this climate of agitation, Austria was waiting for an excuse to invade Serbia and end the problem of Serbian nationalism.

Russia too was a multi-nation empire containing more than one hundred different nationalities. Nationalism was challenging Russia's domination of its largest subject nationality - the Ukrainians. Ukraine, like Serbia, was divided between two states. Most of the Ukrainian nation, about 27 million, lived within the borders of the Russian Empire, while another three million were across the border in the Austrian provinces of Galicia and Bukovina. Austria suppressed ethnic nationalism in many parts of its empire but allowed the Ukrainian nationalists of Bukovina and Galicia to operate freely in order to antagonize Russia. Russian strategy held that the annexation of Galicia and Bukovina would enable Russia to defeat the growing Ukrainian nationalism. Russia also wanted access to the Mediteranean. Finally, Russians are ethnic Slavs, so they felt a degree of loyalty to their Southern Slav cousins.

These nationalist and imperialist tensions, competing national interests plus increased militarism, and the alliance system meant the Balkan Peninsula was like a keg of gunpowder ready to explode. All it needed was a spark. That spark came with the assassination of the heir to the Austria-Hungarian throne by a Serbian nationalist.

The Alliance System

The alliance system in Europe made it more likely that a local conflict would drag all the powers into a wider war. In the twenty years prior to World War I, the relatively new German nation-state's efforts to expand its territory, armed forces and global influence brought the country into conflict with the imperialist ambitions of Britain and France.

In a bid to improve security in an increasingly hostile neighbourhood, the European powers formed alliances - like two rival street gangs - the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy) and the Triple Entente (France, Russia and Britain). Members of these gangs swore to defend eachother against one or all members of the other gang. These alliances would turn a small, localized dispute between Austria-Hungary and Serbia into the world's first global war.

Wilhelm II wrote to the Austrian ambassador at Berlin, July 5, 1914, and asked him to forward to his ruler, what became known as the "blank cheque". "(...) I authorize you to inform your gracious Majesty that Austria can count on full German support even in the case of serious European complications. (...) What were the probable consequences of this statement?

 

The France/Russia allies were situated at opposite ends of Germany. This alliance put Germany in a precarious position. Germany did not want to split its forces to fight France in the West and Russia in the East at the same time. They also knew that it would take about 6 weeks for Russia to mobilize its armed forces. So a German officer came up with the Schleiffen Plan to defeat France quickly:

The bulk of German forces would avoid the heavily fortified France-German border and instead invade Belgium to the north and swing around behind the French defenses in a bid to defeat France within 6 weeks. German forces could then be freed up to defeat Russia. The plan was based on another assumption; that Britain would not join the war to defend neutral Belgium.

Neither Serbia nor Austria-Hungary believed the conflict would spread world-wide. War and conflict had dominated politics in the Balkans for many decades. Territory was lost, gained, and eventually fought over again. The concept of a world-wide conflict was inconceivable. It didn't help that the details of treaties and alliances were kept secret.

Another consideration here is that anger clouds issues. When any person or group faces a fight, defeat or injury is not the main factor in deciding to fight. In other words, neither Serbia nor Austria-Hungary seemed to consider what would happen as a result of extended war. As in many conflicts, the focus was on potential gains (resources, coastal access to ports) but not losses.

NATIONALISM + MILITARISM + ALLIANCES + IMPERIALISM = WWI?

As with most wars, no single cause produced World War One. Historians generally agree that the underlying causes of World War One included nationalism, imperialism, militarism, and alliances. Nationalism created tensions among major powers and produced new nations, which affected the existing balance of power. Imperialism played a major part in setting the stage for war as the countries of Europe vied to acquire overseas possessions. As tensions mounted, the major powers competed for power and prestige by increasing their military strength. Some nations followed a policy of militarism by giving the military a strong hand in government. As well, major powers negotiated alliances to protect their interests.

Trench Warfare

When the front line stabilized, ordinary soldiers began to dig thousands of foxholes (small one or two person holes in the ground offering protection from bullets and artillery shells). These foxholes soon developed into a system of trenches, resulting in the first war in history to be fought almost exclusively from trenches.

Trench warfare was not planned by military generals in 1914 (remember, they expected a quick war). Rather, ordinary soldiers took their own initiative in an attempt to avoid being killed by new state-of-the-art weapons, including quick-firing artillery, machine guns and repeating rifles. The soldiers, by digging the trenches, were adopting the only defensive strategy at their disposal.

As if snipers, artillery barrages or gas were not enough, soldiers also had to deal with trench rats, lice, and trench foot.

Once the trenches had been dug on both sides, assaulting an enemy trench became a very hazardous undertaking. Whenever attacking troops went "over the top" - climbing out of their trenches to attack the enemy's trenches - they were cut down by a hail of bullets, high explosive shells, and shrapnel (little pieces of flying metal from artillery shells designed to explode in the air above attacking troops).

Military planners had predicted a quick and decisive war. Instead, World War I became a long and costly war of attrition. Millions of men would die in trench warfare. One by one, as they waited in their trenches and bunkers, they were routinely killed by snipers' bullets, shrapnel from artillery, poison gas, high explosive shells and disease. Large numbers died as they attempted mass assaults against entrenched enemy positions. Yet military leaders kept sending men "over the top." What does this tell you about national interests?

Weapons of War

World War I was the very first of the industrial, mechanized wars.  Machines and technology were systematically applied to the slaughter of human beings.  Weapons especially designed for efficient killing and mass destruction, such as machine guns and field artillery were mass produced for battle fronts. It was called "total war" because all the resources of society were applied to the war effort. This meant civilian workers were considered legitimate targets. The result was death and destruction on an unprecedented scale.

Armies in World War I possessed devastating firepower but they could only move on the battlefiled as fast as a person can walk. In the past, cavalry (soldiers on horseback) provided rapid mobility to exploit enemy weaknesses on the battlefield. In World War I, cavalry had become obsolete. One machine gun would take a heavy toll on a massed cavalry charge. More accurate and lethal artillery would wipe out such soft targets as soon as they were spotted by forward observers. And the cratered and muddy ground in No Man's Land would slow down cavalry, taking away their advantage of speed.

The weaponry was advanced, more lethal, and more numerous compared to past wars. But the development of modern motorized transport was still in its infancy. Only in later wars, when reliable trucks and tanks were introduced, would armies regain the battlefield mobility they lost in World War I. The trench system, combined with modern weaponry and the lack of battlefield mobility resulted in a 4-year stalemate.

Weapons of World War I

  • Artillery: Responsible for more casualties than any other weapon.
  • Machine-gun: An effective defensive weapon when arranged on a battlefield for maximum effectiveness against soldiers advancing in line across no man's land.
  • Poison gas: The most feared weapon of all! Read Wilfred Owen's famous poem "Dulce et Decorum Est" to get a sense of the effects of poison gas. In 1915 the Germans introduced the use of poison gas against French, Algerian, Canadian and British troops at Ypres. That battle inspired John McCrae's famous poem "In Flander's Fields", which in turn inspired the tradition of wearing a red poppy on Remembrance Day.
  • Tanks: Introduced halfway through the war but they were too unreliable and insufficient in number to have a significant impact on the outcome of the war. Nevertheless, they enjoyed limited success at Cambrai in 1917 and Amiens in 1918 (watch a video narrated by a Canadian soldier who fought at Amiens).
  • Aircraft: Unarmed aircraft were initially used for observing and photographing enemy trenches and troop movements behind enemy lines. They were eventually armed with machineguns and bombs to attack enemy aircraft, airfields, trenches and factories in cities.

War of Attrition

Battles of Attrition

The casualties suffered in the First World War were of a scale never before experienced. Great Britain and her Empire lost over 1,000,000 combatants; France, 1,300,000; Russia, 1,700,000; Germany and its allies, 3,500,000. On average, 5,500 soldiers died every day! Many more were wounded and/or maimed for life.

Although each soldier would have been involved in some form of continual conflict whilst serving on the front-line (e.g. trench raids, snipers, shelling), it is possible to distinguish major battles (or pushes) whose names have gone down in history as some of the bloodiest conflicts ever waged. Keep in mind that there were many more large and small battles than the ones mentioned below.

The Battle of Verdun, 1916

Verdun Cemetary (photo credit: photo credit: Kristoffer Eliassen Grini)

The Battle of Verdun is considered the greatest and lengthiest in world history.  Never before has there been such a long battle, involving so many men and situated on such a tiny piece of land. The battle raged between German and French forces on a battlefield less than 10 square kilometers and lasted from February 21, to December 19, 1916. Estimated casualties (dead, wounded and missing): 540,000 French and 430,000 German casualties in a battle that offered no strategic advantages to either side.
 
Nationalism can be both a strength and liability in wartime. Germany attacked Verdun because the fortresses were a source of French national pride. Although forts no longer served a strategic purpose in modern warfare, Germany knew that French patriots would defend these sites at all costs. The intent was not to win ground but to incite the enemy into battle and win a war of attrition - to cause such heavy casualties that France would be forced out of the war. But the Germans underestimated the determination of the French and the meaning it had to the French. While defending the legendary fortresses, French forces killed an unexpectedly high number of German attackers.

Can you think of any recent attacks that "won" no ground but incited the country attacked into a long drawn-out conflict? 

The Battle of the Somme, 1916

 

A German trench pulverized by artillery at the Batlle of the Somme, 1916.

At 7:30AM on the 1st of July, 1916, after a week-long artillery bombardment; British General Haig launched the now infamous "Big Push" attack across the river Somme. With the French Army being hard-pressed at Verdun, the British intended to break through the German defences in a matter of hours.

The troops were ordered to keep in lines and to march towards the enemy across no-man's land. This, coupled with the failure of the artillery bombardment to destroy much of the German barbed wire and machine-gun posts, led to one of the biggest slaughters in military history.

After the artillery barrage was lifted, the Germans dragged themselves out of their dugouts, manned their posts and destroyed the oncoming waves of British infantry walking across no man's land in orderly lines.

After the first day, with a gain of only 1.5km, the British had suffered 57,470 casualties. Despite this, Haig pressed on with the attack for more than 4 months. For the meagre achievements, total losses on the British side numbered 419,654 with German casualties between 450,000 and 680,000. When the offensive was eventually called off the British were still over 4 kilometres short of Bapaume and Serre, their first-day objectives.

Recruiting in Canada

When Canada went to war in 1914, many people enlisted and went to a war that most believed would be “over in a few weeks”. Enlistments from Canada were not a problem at the beginning of the war; many Canadians were recent immigrants from Britain and were ready to fight when “Mother England” needed help. In the beginning, no one could imagine a war that went on for several years, a war with so much loss of life. A journey overseas promised adventure and excitement, a nice uniform, and a steady wage. 

Canada’s Prime Minister Borden promised the Imperial War Council to maintain a force of 500,000 troops overseas. This was probably too high a target for a country whose entire population was only 10 million, but Borden agreed to the number because he believed that the war was a way for Canada to “prove itself” in the world arena. The Canadian government used propaganda involving nationalism as a basis for enlistment. In the following example, Canada’s government tried to use loyalty to the country of origin as a basis for recruitment. The government believed that French-Canadians would probably not fight for Britain, but they might fight for France.

Written in French, this poster states:

French Canadians

Forward!
For King
For Country,
For France
your Blood.
For Humanity
and Liberty
To Arms!

 

Propaganda also used gender stereotypes to encourage enlistment.  In this poster, the woman is shaming the man into enlisting.  The stereotype?  “Real” men fight and women are home makers. The man is also being asked to represent Ireland, not Canada or the British Empire.  This poster’s appeal is probably directly related to the experiences of most Irish immigrants; the majority had been sent to Canada by wealthy landowners who could not or would not support them during the Potato Famine in Ireland.   The poster does not ask an Irish person to help Britain fight, because Britain currently occupied his country, so Irish nationalism prevented Irish immigrants in Canada from responding quickly to a call for help from Britain.

Propaganda was used to

  • keep morale up at the Home Front (people living at home)
  • encourage people to give their time and money to the war effort
  • portray the enemy as an evil that needs to be fought
  • recruit more soldiers
  • stop information (that might help the enemy) from being published
  • psychologically dishearten the enemy troops
  • give civilians a government-approved version of the war

World War One and Nationalism - Conscription

World War One and Nationalism - Conscription As the war went on, the loss of troops was staggering. Canadian troops had a 1 in 10 chance of being killed. Although prop...

As the war went on, the loss of troops was staggering. Canadian troops had a 1 in 10 chance of being killed.  Although propaganda was used to encourage more enlistment, citizens at home were no longer eager to fight in the “war to end all wars.” Many Canadian citizens saw the war as a problem between European powers and felt Canada had no place in the conflict. 

French Canadians were loyal to Canada, but were not willing to fight for what they believed to be Britain's war. Other immigrants’ past experiences in Europe had not been positive (hence the decision to emigrate) so they felt no desire to defend their nation state of origin in Europe. Farmers and union leaders felt they were performing an essential service - providing food for Canadian citizens and soldiers - therefore they should not have to enlist.

In 1917, Canadian Prime Minister Borden (seen here at a Victory Bond campaign to raise money for the war effort) was being pressured by Britain to maintain the number of troops he had promised at the beginning of the war. Recruits were not coming forward, so, despite election promises made against conscription, Borden’s government brought in a conscription law - the Military Services Act. Up until that point, Canada's armed forces was an all-volunteer army.

The reaction to conscription eventually became known as the conscription crisis. This crisis caused many problems at home.

World War One - Nation Building in Victory and Defeat

World War One - Nation Building in Victory and Defeat World War One became a training ground for young nation-states. Canadians, Australians and New Zealanders entered...

World War One became a training ground for young nation-states. Canadians, Australians and New Zealanders entered the war as dedicated imperialists who were proud and loyal citizens of the British Empire. In the cauldron of war, however, their imperial loyalties were replaced by new national loyalties. Canadian, Australian and New Zealand nationalism were born on the battlefields of World War I.

Gallipoli 1915 - Australian, New Zealand and Turkish nationalism

World War One was the first global conflict. The troops of great empires clashed on several continents, and on several oceans and seas. The stalemate on the major European battlefronts (the Western Front and the Eastern Front) made both sides attempt breakthroughs on other fronts. These included offensives along the Italian-Austrian border, in the Balkans, and in the Middle East. The Gallipoli campaign is an example how nation building can occur in both victory (Turkey) and defeat (Australia and New Zealand).

Anzac Cove after the landing. Note how precarious their position was right from the start, with Turkish troops in the hills firing down on the beach.

 Australian and New Zealand nationalism came into its own on the rocky cliffs and beaches of Gallipoli. The Battle of Gallipoli helped transform the colonies of Australia and New Zealand into nation-states. It also helped transform the Ottoman Empire (a multi-national state) into a nation-state. Turkey had existed as a country on the world's political map for centuries, but it would only become a nation-state through World War I.

Gallipoli and Australian and New Zealand nationalism

The Gallipoli campaign of 1915 was an Allied attempt to knock Ottoman Turkey out of WWI and reopen a supply route to Russia through the Dardenelles (Turkey controlled this narrow water route between the Mediteranean and Black Sea).  Allied casualties (killed, wounded and missing) for the entire nine-month campaign were about 252,000, with Turkey suffering an estimated 251,000.

The Allies hastily assembled a force of 78,000 men and dispatched them from England and Egypt to Gallipoli. As the flotilla gathered near the peninsula, the commanding general, Ian Hamilton, discovered that guns and ammunition had been loaded on separate ships. The transports had to steam to Egypt to be properly loaded for combat. The Turks, now alerted to the Allied plan, used the resulting month's delay to improve their defenses. Some 60,000 Turkish troops, under the German general Otto Liman von Sanders, awaited the Allies.

When the Allies returned they managed to secure the beach at great cost but the planned breakthrough inland never materialised. The end result was 9 months of trench warfare and the eventual Allied evacuation without having achieved their objectives. While the evacuation itself was a masterpiece, the Gallipoli campaign was a major Allied defeat. Yet Australia and New Zealand celebrate April 25th (the anniversary of the landing at Gallipoli) as Anzac day, an important national holiday in both countries.

Gallipoli and Turkish nationalism

Mustafa Kemal (known as "Ataturk" or "Father of the Turks") was the general who saved the Ottoman Empire from defeat at Gallipoli. The Turks won the battle but they were on the losing side in the war. After World War One, the Ottoman Empire, stripped of most of its non-Turkish lands and non-Turkish peoples, became the nation state of Turkey. It also replaced loyalty to a traditional Ottoman imperial leader (the sultan) with loyalty to the Turkish nation. Mustafa Kemal became the first nationalist leader and the first national hero of the modern nation state of Turkey.

Vimy Ridge and Canadian Nationalism

Vimy Ridge, France, was a key objective of the Allies during the First World War. The British and the French armies had tried to capture it from the Germans; both attempts failed. On April 9, 1917, a corps of Canadian soldiers succeeded in capturing Vimy Ridge. The Canadians suffered 20,000 casualties, (including 5,000 dead) and yet April 9, 1917, is considered by some as the day that Canada became a nation.

Many history writers and researchers have referred to WWI as the war where Canada "came of age." This means that when Canada entered the war, she was a colony; at war's end, however, Canada went to the Versailles peace conference and represented herself as an independent country. The rite of passage for Canada and her soldiers was not an easy one. Many lives were lost, many received permanent disabilities. No one who went to war was the same person when they came home. When someone goes through a very difficult act to prove worthiness, it is often called an "Ordeal by Fire". Many people believe that Vimy Ridge was Canada's ordeal by fire.

World War One Ends

World War One Ends Despite Canada's 1917 victory at Vimy Ridge, the war was still a stalemate on both the Western and Eastern Fronts. Two events changed the balance of...

Despite Canada's 1917 victory at Vimy Ridge, the war was still a stalemate on both the Western and Eastern Fronts. Two events changed the balance of power in 1917, one in favour of Germany and one in favour of the Allies.

Germany made the decision to get at Britain by the only way that seemed available. The Germans used submarines (U-boats) to cut off the food and raw materials that Britain imported from overseas. This unrestricted submarine warfare meant sinking American ships bringing supplies to Britain and France. Prior to this, German submarines targeted only Allied shipping. These new attacks on U.S. shipping prompted the Americans to join the Allies and declare war on Germany on April 6, 1917.

At the same time, the Allies faced a potential disaster. There was a revolution in Russia in early 1917 which overthrew the Tsar (monarchy). Russia was effectively out of the war and surrendered to Germany in early 1918. Germany was able to concentrate its war effort on the Western Front - it no longer had the two-front war it had tried to avoid with the Schlieffen Plan in 1914. The surrender of Russia appeared to switch the momentum in Germany’s favour.

The German leadership took advantage of this situation and put together one last big push in 1918 (the Spring Offensive) in the hope of defeating the Allies before fresh American troops arrived in Europe in large numbers. The 1918 German offensive managed to break through Allied lines but once again petered out at the River Marne, the same place where the German advance was halted in 1914.

With American reinforcements arriving in large numbers in 1918, the balance of power in the war of attrition was now firmly in the Allies' favour. Allied troops broke through the exhausted German lines on August 8, 1918, and steadily pushed back the German army. By the fall of 1918 the other Central Power forces were crumbling too. In September, Bulgaria accepted defeat. In October, the Ottoman Empire surrendered. On November 11, 1918, Germany signed an armistice agreement ending World War I (we celebrate this now as Remembrance Day).

We set to work to bury people. We pushed them into the sides of the trenches but bits of them kept getting uncovered and sticking out, like people in a badly made bed. Hands were the worst; they would escape from the sand, pointing, begging - even waving! There was one which we all shook when we passed, saying, "Good morning," in a posh voice. Everybody did it. The bottom of the trench was springy like a mattress because of all  the bodies underneath... 

Leonard Thompson - quoted in Ronald Blythe, Akenfield: Portrait of an English Village (New York: Pantheon Books, 1969)

The war that was to have been over by Christmas 1914 had dragged on for four long years. It had cost 10 million dead and 21 million wounded.  The fighting had virtually destroyed northern France, and devasted Europe's agricultural base. The war (and an even more deadly influenza virus from 1918-1920) had effectively removed a generation of youth from the planet. The Paris Peace Conference: The “Big Four” Demands and National Interests

 
British citizens had experienced loss of family and friends.  They had also experienced the horror of Zeppelin bombing raids and damage to their cities.  They wanted their government to “Squeeze them [the Germans] until the pips squeak.”  Prime Minister Lloyd George, however, was concerned about what Germany would do in the future if it was treated too harshly.  If Germany was humiliated, it could become an enemy once again. Nevertheless, he wanted Germany to pay for war damages and to reduce its future naval capacity. Britain also thought the rich industrial area of Germany adjacent to France, the Rhineland, should be a demilitarized zone.
French citizens had a long-standing anger against the Germans.  They wanted revenge not only for this war but also to recover losses to Germany from previous wars.  They wanted Germany to be so harshly punished that it would never again be able to wage war. Prime Minister Clemenceau was nicknamed "The Tiger". He wanted a harsh settlement that would eliminate any future German threat and to make Germany pay for all the damage that France suffered during the years of fighting.  He wanted, for example, control of the Rhineland (Germany’s main industrial area) and the return of the former French provinces of Alsace and Lorraine.
The USA entered the war in April, 1917.  It had far less casualties and financial burdens than other participants.  In fact, the USA had lent money in the form of arms and equipment to the other Allies.  Wilson wanted to ensure "a just and lasting peace" and to prevent the repetition of practices that had led up to WWI.  He created his 'Fourteen Points' and intended to push these through.  His attitude towards Germany was that it should not be punished too harshly.  He wanted Germany to enter his new ‘League of Nations’ as an equal “instead of a place of mastery.”
Italy had begun the war as an ally of Germany.  As a way of weakening Germany, Britain invited Italy to secret talks in London in 1915.  At the talks, Italy was promised territories from Austria-Hungary if it would side with the Allies.  Italy declared war on Germany in 1915, after agreeing to the promises in the secret Treaty of London.  Italian Prime Minister Vittorio Orlando came to the table expecting the secret treaty to be honored plus a share of German colonies in Africa.

AND GERMANY…?

Germany was not invited to the peace treaty talks. Even though a democratic government had been formed and the Kaiser was no longer in power, it was not allowed to represent its side.  When Germany signed the armistice, they knew about Wilson’s Fourteen Points and expected that these points would be used to determine peace terms.  Remember that Wilson’s Fourteen Points noted that the focus should be on peaceful resolution and justice for all countries involved. Germany’s absence from the talks was interpreted by many of the delegates as an acknowledgement of Germany’s guilt.  Germany was blamed for the entire war and punished accordingly.

WHAT ABOUT CANADA?

Canada was in a unique position during the peace talks.  Canada had entered the war as a colony of Britain.  The flag depicted here, for example, was the Canadian flag at the end of WWI. The Canadian forces, however, had demonstrated in many ways, that pride in Canada gave them the will to fight.  The Battle of Vimy Ridge was the defining moment in many battles waged, because all the Canadian forces, for the first time in history, fought together and won.  Vimy Ridge was a Canadian, not a British colonial victory.  Nationalism directed mainly toward Britain had propelled Canada into WWI but nationalism among Canadians had changed its focus during the war.  Canadian soldiers and other personnel soon considered themselves Canadians first. 

Sir Robert Borden

Robert Borden was the Prime Minister of Canada throughout WWI.  At the peace talks in Paris, Sir Borden insisted that Canada be given a place other than that of a colony of Britain.  Because of Borden’s insistence, Canada would have a larger role in world affairs from that point on.  Unlike many of the country delegations, Canada was not seeking more land or financial restitution.  What was Canada in search of?  Read on to learn how Canada pursued its national interests at the peace conference of 1919.

 

The Treaty of Versailles 1919

This was the treaty between Germany and the victorious Allied nations (including Canada ) which brought the World War to a formal close. It was signed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles on June 28, 1919. The terms of the treaty affected Canada in a direct way only slightly. Canada obtained a small share of the indemnities to be paid by Germany; but otherwise she asked for, and obtained, no direct benefits from the treaty in land or money. She did benefit from it, however, in the enhancement of her national status. In the Peace Conference in Paris, Sir Robert Borden insisted that Canada should have the same representation as Belgium and other small countries at the Conference; and in the end Canada was given, with the other overseas Dominions, representation on the British Empire delegation to the Conference. She was given two seats in the Conference, and these were occupied alternately by Sir Robert Borden, Sir George Foster, the Hon. A. L. Sifton, and the Hon. C. J. Doherty. When the Treaty of Versailles came to be signed, Borden insisted that it should be signed separately on behalf of Canada. Opposition to this proposal arose in the United States delegation, which maintained that if Canada and the other British Dominions signed separately, the British Empire would have six votes in the proposed League of Nations, whereas the United States would have only one. Eventually, the problem was resolved by having the British Empire delegation sign for Great Britain, and the representatives of the British Dominions sign underneath, the names of their respective countries being indented under that of the British Empire. Canada thus, as a result of the treaty, obtained separate representation in the Assembly of the League of Nations, and even obtained the recognition of her right to have her representative elected to the Council of the League, with the result that the representative of Canada was actually elected to the Council of the League in 1927.

The Treaty of Versailles

Keep in mind that the purpose of the peace settlement was to establish the conditions for stable and lasting peace in which the various sides would feel their interests were sufficiently addressed.

The peace talks concluded and the results were published in 1919 as the Treaty of Versailles.  One of the key clauses in this treaty was the “war guilt clause.” The terms of this clause are cited by many historians as one of the key reasons why the ultranationalist Adolf Hitler was able to come to power in the 1930s.  The main points of the Treaty were:

  • Germany must accept full responsibility for the war – this portrayed Germany as the aggressor – this portrayal greatly injured German national pride.
  • This also allowed the Allies to prosecute members of the German military for war crimes and six were convicted – no Allied personnel were tried; again the negative image injured German national pride.
  • Germany had to pay for all the war damages - set at £6.6 million or about $12.5 million.  This was a very heavy penalty to be laid on Germany as it, like other European countries, had suffered devastating human and property losses and damages.  The amount angered most Germans and made them open to any leader who would come forward and find a way out of this debt.

Other terms that affected Germany:

  • Germany’s army was reduced to 100,000 men.
  • Germany could have no air force or submarines, and was limited to six large ships.
  • Germany was split into pieces again.  A very large portion of Germany was now the re-established nation of Poland.
  • Germany lost all her colonies to Allied countries.

The League of Nations

One of the most significant parts of the Treaty of Versailles was the establishment of the League of Nations, the predecessor of the modern-day United Nations (UN).

Woodrow Wilson was President of the United States at the end of WWI. Wilson was an academic, a pacifist, and an idealist. Wilson had a vision of the world that he shared in his Fourteen Points, in which he promoted internationalism over national interests such as nationalism. 

A general association of nations must be formed under specific covenants for the purpose of affording mutual guarantees of political independence and territorial integrity to great and small states alike.

Excerpt from Point 14 of President Wilson's Fourteen Points

In brief, Wilson’s vision advocated:

  • open diplomacy: Diplomacy between countries should be in public, not in secret.
  • free trade: Trade between countries should be fair and equal.
  • international disarmament: The numbers of weapons each country has should decrease.
  • negotiation of colonial claims: A peaceful settlement should be made regarding European colonies.
  • settlement of European borders: Peaceful adjustment of borders should be along lines of nationality.
  • an association of nations: An organization should guarantee equality and collective security among countries.

Albert Einstein, the father of modern physics, was credited with being one of the most brilliant people who ever lived.  He was once asked how he approached problems. He surprised the interviewer by saying, “Imagination is more important than knowledge.” He explained that the ability to dream of possible alternatives (to “think outside the box”) is very important for change to occur.

The problem with visions, however, is that other people have to share the same vision for it to become reality.

Wilson’s desire for an association of nations, or “league of nations”, was adopted into the final Versailles peace treaty because Wilson insisted and he represented a now powerful USA. If the League was to be a success, all nations would need to share the goals of its charter. Unfortunately, the United States government not only refused to approve the Versailles Treaty but also refused to join the League of Nations. The absence from the League of the moderate influence of the United States was a detriment to the League's operations, seriously weakening it.

The Structure of the League of Nations

The League had four main parts:

  • The Council had nine members, five of whom were permanent, while the others changed regularly. The five Permanent Council Members were Great Britain, France, Italy, Japan and Germany (from 1926). Japan and Germany both resigned from the League of Nations in 1933, and Italy resigned in 1937.
  • The Assembly had representatives from all of the members, which varied between 40 and 60. Countries joined and left the League in the 1920s and 1930s, but the USA never joined, and the USSR only joined in 1934 when Stalin became worried about the rise to power of Hitler.
  • The Secretariat was based in Geneva and was headed by the Secretary-General. The first was Sir Eric Drummond, who was British. This meant that many countries around the world thought that the League only represented the interests of the European countries. The Secretariat organized the business of the League and carried out its decisions.
  • The Covenant consisted of the rules which members had to agree to obey when they joined. The rules including accepting the League's decisions and not using force to solve disputes.

Functions of the League of Nations

The League had 3 main functions:

  • to prevent war
  • to carry out the provisions of the peace treaties
  • to assist in the improvement of social and economic conditions

Recognition for Canada's Role in World War I

WWI had been an “ordeal by fire” for Canadians. The Canadian nation had been tested and found worthy. Canada’s experiences in WWI earned Canada recognition for the efforts of both its troops and its citizens. The Imperial War Conference of 1917 officially declared equality among all British nations. During the peace talks, Canada sat at the table as a representative. When the Versailles Treaty was signed, Canada signed as a separate nation. When the League of Nations was formed, Canada was allowed to send its own representatives to the meetings. Of course, ambiguity over what equality among nations meant still existed. The Imperial Conference had also stated that consultation among the British nations would create a common foreign and military policy. What did this mean for Canada?

After WWI, Canada was still tied to Britain in most respects. The problem, then, was that Canadians had to create a nation that allowed expression of Canada’s national interests. The horrors of WWI and the losses to every Canadian community had dampened greatly the enthusiasm of Canadians to be linked to the foreign policy of a European nation. WWI had also helped Canadians develop a sense of who they were as a nation separate from Britain. Most Canadians no longer saw themselves as British subjects. After the war, political leaders in Canada used the increased sense of Canadian nationalism to seek independence from Britain. To begin to understand how leaders such as Adolf Hitler and Benito Mussolini could seize power within democratic systems to create fascist governments, we must understand economic events of the time. Knowledge of economic events also helps you to understand why many leaders chose to appease rather than act against these men.

"The Roaring Twenties" in the USA

The 1920s are sometimes portrayed in the media as a decade of non-stop celebration. Although these years are called the Roaring Twenties, the term is more a descriptor of economic trends and the fast pace of life. These years were anything but calm.

The Roaring Twenties saw an increasingly modern American society with the debate of evolution against creation, the beginning of the civil rights movement for African-Americans, and women enjoying more political and social freedoms. Women got the right to vote in 1920 and young single women were going to clubs - without chaperones! Jazz was the popular and revolutionary new music and people were wearing radical new fashions. In some ways, the 1920s were a period of social revolution similar to the 1960s.

On the other side of the coin, there was a strong conservative movement marked by the arrest and execution of suspected communists, increasing activity of the white-supremacist Ku-Klux-Klan, the prohibition (outlawing) of alchohol (from 1920-1933) and marijuana (on a national level in 1937 but earlier in many states) and the resultant growth in organized crime. There was also a widening gulf among socioeconomic levels with an increased tendency to isolate the USA from international politics.

Isolationism

The American government had rejected the Versailles Settlements and the League of Nations. Some American politicians thought Germany had been treated too harshly; others did not want the United States to get "tangled up in European affairs" and wars. Many Americans believed that membership in the League of Nations would turn the temporary involvement of the United States in Europe into a permanent responsibility. President Coolidge (1923-29) declared, "The business of America is business." What this meant was a policy of isolationism.

The United States tried to retreat into isolationism; that is, it tried not to get involved in international affairs as much as possible. The United States chose to do mainly two things to protect its national interests.

  1. Put a limit on immigration into the United States to protect American jobs.
  2. Put high tariffs or taxes on imported goods to protect its economy. This limited foreign imports and, therefore, protected American industries.

By establishing tariffs and limiting immigration, the United States was able to isolate or limit its involvement with other nations.

The 1920s were a time of optimism in the USA. The United States dominated all international trade during the 1920s. Unlike other nations around the world, the USA actually profited from WWI. The USA had loaned its allies over $7 billion during the war and another $3.3 billion by 1920. Unlike other nations, the USA had not lost many citizens to war and none of its cities had to to rebuild. After WWI, American investors had money to burn.

Investors supported automotive and hydro-electric projects in Canada. They also invested in projects in Germany. The problem with the excess money was that the industrialized nations of the world became dependent on investment from the USA. It was also difficult to export to the USA because high tariffs (taxes) kept imports out and protected American industries from competition.

Think about it ...

  • If the USA was not buying products from other countries, how much would this affect world trade?
  • Thirty percent of the USA’s Gross National Product was dependent on exporting goods to other countries. What would happen to the USA if others could not afford to buy its products?
  • American investors supported projects in Germany and other nations when the markets looked good. What would happen if investors’ confidence decreased or even disappeared?

After WWI, Germany was in desperate financial condition. Inflation spiralled upward as people tried to meet basic needs and rebuild a country devastated by war. German Economy Declines

The industrial boom lasted for nearly seven years and economists kept warning that the system would plunge Germany’s economy into an inflationary spiral that it might not be able to recover from. Thus, Germany’s destiny took its fatal course...

What is Fascism?

Fascism is an ideology that seeks to renew the social, economic, and political aspects of a nation based on an increased sense of national identity and unity. Fascist ideology often includes the following:

  • Patriotism: Good citizens exist to serve the state.
  • Ultranationalism: Extreme nationalism makes frequent use everywhere of nationalistic symbols such as slogans, songs, flags, images of patriots, propaganda.
  • Contempt for human rights: By manipulating the people's fear of enemies and the need for security, leaders convince the people that rights can be overlooked or suspended.
  • Authoritarian Rule : A strong "father figure" leader in whom all decision-making power is concentrated.
  • Totalitarian: A strong centralized government (the state) controls all aspects of the nation; dissent is not tolerated.
  • Militaristic: The military has a prominent role in all aspects of the nation.
  • Anti-communist: Communism is opposed.
  • Populism: The government or state is proclaimed as the voice of the ‘common people’.
  • Anti-liberal: Freedoms and individual rights are rejected.
  • Control of the media: The state controls what the people see and hear through the media; the state is always made to look good and is never criticized.
  • Identification of Enemies of the People: Blame for problems is focused on a specific group(s), whether deserved or not, so that people can channel their emotions and energies against these scapegoats. Fascists manipulate public opinion so that the people believe that only with the help of the state can the nation's enemies be eliminated.

The word fascism actually comes from the Latin word fascis meaning "bundle". Fascists often use the image of a bundle of sticks and axe in their propaganda. The idea is that one stick is easy to snap, but a bundle of sticks is very difficult if not impossible to break—representing strength through unity. An axe is usually tied into the bundle as a symbol of authority.

Note the bundle to the left in this Italian fascist propaganda poster from the 1930s. This poster also illustrates the fascist ideal of patriotism or devotion to the state through the woman bowing to kiss the flag. Click on the poster for a larger view.

How Does Fascism Take Control?

Fascism is a response to crisis. Fascist leaders promise to rescue the people from their problems and restore the nation to its former glory. The “catch” is that it can succeed only if the people are willing to stand together in the face of their foes. Fascism gains the support of the people through propaganda techniques such as rallies, posters, and flooding the media with their promises.

Fascist regimes often promote ideas such as the following:

  • A nation in crisis: The nation is in crisis because the racial and ethnic variety within the country creates friction and weakens the ability to stand as one nation. A conspiracy of certain racial, ethic, or national groups is often identified as part of the nation’s crisis. For example, Adolf Hitler blamed certain groups such as the Jews for Germany's loss of WWI.
  • An idealized past: The fascists build a picture of a legendary past full of glory and success to help build identity and nationalism. It promises to return the nation to that status. For example, Mussolini promised Italians that he would build the ‘New Roman Empire’ with them.
  • A need for social change: The people must submit to major changes, a new social structure, and a new way of life to achieve this revival.

Common Features of Fascist Systems

  1. Survival of the Fittest: Fascists used a unique interpretation of Darwin’s “Theory of Evolution” to justify force being used to resolve issues. In fascism, the strength of a nation is based on its ability to defend itself or to take what it wants.  An inability to defend against force was interpreted as an indication that the state was too weak to survive. The fascists could look at nations and nation-states and use this idea to justify eliminating some weaker group because it would not survive anyway.

This theory was also used to justify the elimination of people who did not meet the ethnic ideal. The poster to the left is one of many examples of horrific propaganda of the Nazi regime. This poster from the 1930s was supposed to promote the monthly magazine from the NAZI racial office.

The text states, "This genetically ill person will cost our people's community 60,000 marks over his lifetime.  Citizens, this is your money. Read Neues Volk, the monthly of the racial policy office of the NSDAP."

The idea the Nazis were promoting was, "Why should the state take care of such people? They are are waste of time and resources." The Nazi Party put thousands, even millions, of such people to death.
  1. Regimented Social Structure: For a fascist system to function effectively, every person must know his or her place. Typically, these regimes are very chauvinistic or male-dominated. Females are typically allocated to one role—mothers to the future generation. 
  2. Authoritarian Rule: Fascist systems are led by a strong leader with absolute authority. The leader is often endowed with a heroic character. The leader is the state.

The poster on the left reads, "One People, One Nation, One Leader!"

As Germany moved closer to WWII, posters such as the one on the right appeared. It reads, "Adolf Hitler is Victory!"

Dictators often are portrayed as larger than life, and the belief is promoted that only with this person can the country be successful.
  1. Violence: Within a fascist state, strict discipline is accepted as a way to deal with the citizens. It is also demanded that the people conduct themselves in a manner acceptable to the regime. Fascist regimes typically have an organized group that maintains order through force and intimidation. Benito Mussolini, leader of Italy, is shown inspecting his “Shock Troops” to the right. Hitler had the SA or Brown Shirts and later the SS or Gestapo to maintain order and eliminate those who challenged the Nazi Party.   2. Statism: Most of the nation is controlled by a strong, central government. Political and economic control of the nation is secured by nationalizing industries related to raw resources and creating their own currencies. Price and wage controls are used to stop inflation and give people the opportunity to meet their basic needs. Private property is allowed, but property rights and private initiative are based upon service to the state. This means that persons who have the favour of the government have more privileges and business licenses are granted based on what the business will do for the country.

  3. Anti-liberalism: Fascism is against gradual reform and does not allow differences of opinion. This carries into all aspects of the citizen’s life.

Evolution of the NAZI Party

Adolf Hitler, like many other WWI soldiers, returned from the horrors of war to a country devastated by the loss of so many young men and destroyed by the battles that had raged. His return was marked by his perception that the war was lost because the people had given up.

As Hitler looked around for the source of this loss of will, he focused on the actions of communists, Jewish people, and church leaders in Germany.

As a corporal in the Austrian army, he learned of Germany’s defeat while recovering from a British gas attack. Hitler later described his reaction,

"There followed terrible days and even worse nights—I knew that all was lost...in these nights hatred grew in me, hatred for those responsible for this deed."

Hitler, like other die-hard reactionaries, believed that “true” Germans had not caused Germany's WWI defeat but rather the communists, Jews, and church leaders in Germany were responsible. These groups were deemed to be traitors and were referred to as the November Criminals who had "stabbed Germany in the back" or betrayed Germany by purposely sabotaging Germany's war effort in WWI.

When the final terms of the Treaty of Versailles were published, the reaction from most Germans, including Hitler, was rage!

Review the terms of the Treaty of Versailles regarding Germany:

  • Germany had to take full responsibility for the war.
  • Germany had to pay for all the war damages—set at £6.6billion in 1921.
  • Germany’s army was reduced to 100 000 men.
  • Germany could have no air force or submarines and was limited to 6 large ships.
  • Germany lost territory in Europe
  • Germany was split in two by the creation of the new nation of Poland.
  • Germany lost all her colonies.
  • Germany was not allowed to unite with the Germans in Austria (known as Anschluss).

Important Note: Remember that Germany had not been allowed to participate in the negotiations at the Paris Peace Conference; the terms of the Versailles Treaty were forced upon Germany by the Allied powers.

Why Did the People of Germany Support Hitler?

Hitler's speeches and Nazi propaganda were very persuasive in convincing Germans that the Nazi Party was the party to accomplish Germany's economic revival and return to international status.

He promised small business owners that they would no longer have to deal with land speculators. He promised all business owners that there would be no more threats of a communist takeover of the economy and that the Nazis would create a strong market for their products.
  2. Germany a Great Nation promise: Hitler promised the German people that Germany would be a strong and powerful nation again. He promised that he would “tear up” the Treaty of Versailles. He vowed that Germany would not honour the terms of the hated agreement. The German identity was being manipulated by the Nazis into ultranationalism under Nazi control.

By creating scapegoats of the communists and, especially, the Jews, Hitler gave people a concrete source for their problems. All the slogans in that image were used to rally German people around Nazi policies that promised to help them improve their lives and restore Germany to its former glory.
  5. Silenced opposition: The Nazi Party often silenced opposition through the use of its paramilitary arm, the SA or Brownshirts. Consisting mostly of thugs, the SA often assaulted opposition members in street fights and attacked Jewish businesses. The SA played a major part in Kristallnacht.

Europe is confronted with a programme of aggression, nicely calculated and timed, unfolding stage by stage, and there is only one choice open, not only to us, but to other countries who are unfortunately concerned — either to submit, like Austria, or else to take effective measures while time remains to ward off the danger and, if it cannot be warded off, to cope with it." 

- Winston Churchill, British House of Commons, March 14, 1938

 

The Munich Accord

With the unification of Germany and Austria accomplished, Hitler's next grab for territory was the Sudenten territory around the edges of the multinational state of Czechoslovakia.

Obviously, Czechoslovakia did not want to give up part of its territory without a fight. To forestall a conflict over the Sudentenland, Britain, France, Italy, and Germany sent representatives to Munich, Germany, to discuss the situation (Chechoslovakia was not invited!). The four countries signed the Munich Accord on September 29, 1938. The accord gave the Sudenten territory to Germany - a war was avoided by once again appeasing Hitler.

The British and French representatives

  • accepted Hitler’s argument that his only reason for wanting the Sudeten was to protect the German citizens living in the region
  • warned Czechoslovakia that it was on its own if it wanted to resist Germany’s attack.

And the world breathed a sigh of relief...

Prime Minister King tonight sent messages of appreciation and gratitude on behalf of the people of Canada…the heart of Canada is rejoicing tonight at the success which has crowned your unremitting efforts for peace…on the very brink of chaos…the voice of reason has found a way out of the conflict which no people in their heart desired…

- Globe and Mail, Toronto, September 30, 1938

Canada supported appeasement for internal reasons. Prime Minister King did not want to involve Canada in another war. The conscription crisis of 1917 had caused such friction between French-speaking and English-speaking Canadians that the union of Canada was threatened. King wanted to avoid any conflicts that would separate Canadians more.

However, not everyone was convinced that appeasement was a good solution to avoiding international conflict. Winston Churchill, soon to be British Prime Minister, accurately predicted what would come to pass.

I think you will find that in a period of time which may be measured by years but may be measured only in months, Czechoslovakia will be engulfed by the Nazi regime.  Perhaps they may join it in despair or in revenge.  At any rate, that story is over and told.  We cannot consider the abandonment of Czechoslovakia in the light only of what happened last month.  It is the most grievous consequence which we have experienced of what we have done and of what we have left undone in the last five years – five years of futile good intention, five years of eager search for the line of least resistance, five years of uninterrupted retreat of British power, five years of neglect of our air defenses.  Those are the features which I stand here to declare and which marked an improved stewardship for which Great Britain and France have dearly to pay. 

- Winston Churchill, British House of Commons, October 1, 1938

 

Hitler Invades Czechoslovakia

Emboldened by his successes, Hitler continued with his expansionist foreign policy. On March 16, 1939, Nazi forces fulfilled Churchill's prophecy by invading the remainder of Czechoslovakia. Hitler announced that Czechoslovakia had "ceased to exist". The sovereign nation state of Czechoslovakia disappeared and the nations of the world stood by and did nothing.

It had become obvious to all that Hitler would not be appeased. Without any opposition, Nazi Germany would continue to take territory. Appeasement had only forestalled the inevitable war while allowing Germany to become stronger.

France and England prepared for war but they knew they could not battle Nazi Germany alone. The United States, however, through its own laws (the Neutrality Acts), was following a policy of isolationism and would not get involved. The Neutrality Acts were designed to keep the United States out of a possible European war.

The British King George VI and his wife Queen Elizabeth I visited Canada for a month in 1939. As Britain’s official heads of state, they sought to gain Canada’s help in the war developing with Nazi Germany. Their visit boosted loyalty for "Mother England". By the time their visit ended, Canadian support was guaranteed. Although the Statute of Westminster declared that Canada could make its own foreign policy decisions, Canada would soon be involved in WWII on Britain's side.

Isolationism

Appeasement was one approach to deal with international disputes. Another approach is to follow a foreign policy of non-intervention.

  • isolationism: a nation concentrates on its own internal affairs and virtually ignores the rest of the world
  • protectionism: a nation chooses to impose duties (taxes) on imports from other countries to protect domestic industries
  • neutrality: a nation refuses to choose sides in any international dispute

Until attacked by Japan in December 1941, over two years after the start of WWII, American foreign policy was one of non-intervention through isolationism and protectionism. This policy (with regard to European affairs) had begun with President George Washington’s administration and was still alive and well until Pearl Harbour was bombed by Japanese forces in 1941.

Why did the USA choose to follow this foreign policy? The United States was created after winning independence from Great Britain. Historically, the USA did not want to involve itself in any matters relating to Europe. George Washington, the first U.S. president, in his Farewell Address to the Nation in 1796 said,

"The great rule of conduct for us, in regard to foreign nations, is in extending our commercial relations, to have with them as little political connection as possible. Europe has a set of primary interests, which to us have none or a very remote relation."

This isolationist point of view toward Europe was also evident in 1823 when President James Monroe gave voice to what would later be termed the Monroe Doctrine: "In the wars of the European powers, in matters relating to themselves, we have never taken part, nor does it comport with our policy, so to do." American Isolationism to Interventionism

The policy of isolationism meant the USA did nothing to stop Hitler's expansionist policies before the outbreak of WWII. Even with the outbreak of WWII in Europe, public opinion continued to reject direct American involvement in the war (as the photo on the left shows). The USA’s policy of isolation did not end until it was attacked by Japan at Pearl Harbour. This attack made the supporters of isolationism take a back seat to the voices for intervention.

However, American isolationism should not be confused with neutrality. Although the Americans did not enter WWII until after their naval base at Pearl Harbour was attacked, they were sympathetic to the Allied cause. Lend-Lease Agreement (March 1941): Under the terms of this agreement, the USA provided arms to Britain, the USSR, China, France, and other Allied nations in exchange for military bases in Newfoundland (not a part of Canada at the time), the Bermudas, and the British West Indies. American Interventionism

American Interventionism Big Ideas: The pursuit of national interests affects foreign policy American Isolationism in Europe In the last activity about American isolat...

 

Big Ideas:

American Isolationism in Europe

In the last activity about American isolationism, you were introduced to the idea of a United States whose foreign policy dictated that it not get entangled in conflicts involving foreign nations. Such an isolationist foreign policy can lead to unintended consequences. For example, in your study of the League of Nations, you learned that the isolationist decision of the United States not to join the League severely hindered the League’s ability to deal with world issues. Although not the only reason for the League's eventual failure, the American absence from the League helped bring about the League's downfall.

The American policy of isolationism, however, applied primarily to Europe. The US followed a policy of interventionism closer to home in order to secure its national interests.

A Tradition of American Interventionism

Although the USA had operated under a policy of non-intervention in Europe, national interests in other areas began to present themselves in other ways. As European countries were building colonies, the US became aware of the profits to be made in international trade. American economic interests combined with the Monroe Doctrine led to an increase in American economic and military expeditions in other nations.

The Monroe Doctrine and the principle of Manifest Destiny

In the painting shown here, the angelic-looking woman is the spirit of Manifest Destiny. She is advancing with settlers across the American West. Her job was to “spread the benefits of American civilization” to other people. Notice that Native Americans are retreating as the settlers and Manifest Destiny advance.

Manifest Destiny led to the acquisition* of territories from the east coast of North America to the west coast while bringing waves of American settlement westward across the continent. This departure from the traditional view of American non-intervention is described best through the words of President James Monroe, the creator of the Monroe Doctrine in 1823:

"…with governments who have declared their independence and maintained it … we could not view any [reason] for oppressing them or controlling in any manner their destiny…in any other light than as the manifestation of an unfriendly disposition toward the United States."

Image Source: Library of Congress

*Acquisition of territory meant buying, annexing, or gaining control of land through warfare.

Essentially, what Montroe meant was that any nation or nation-state that did not allow the US to expand its territory, or refused the invitation to trade with the USA was considered to be “unfriendly’. American foreign policy then allowed the US to act with force to make the offending nation or nation state comply. This 19th century doctrine was to have far-reaching effects on events in the 20th century because it prompted the USA to travel to Japan.

As a result of the Monroe Doctrine, American national interests involved it in several international conflicts before WWI. In the late 1800s and early 1900s, many Latin American nations were unstable because of the failing Spanish Empire. The USA became involved in nations such as Haiti, Cuba, and the Philippines against Spain. This involvement was an example of the USA intervening in the internal affairs of other nations because of American national interests. This policy is called interventionism and is clearly demonstrated in Cuban-US relations after the Spanish American War in 1898.

The Spanish American War started after Spain rejected the American request for Spain to resolve peacefully Cuba's struggle for independence from Spain. The United States gained control of the Spanish colonies of Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Cuba.

American national interests were pursued at the expense of other nations. By the time US military forces left after the Spanish American War, the USA had “negotiated” (without Cuban representation) the following conditions which gave control of Cuba essentially to the United States:

  • No Cuban territory could be transferred to any nation state other than the USA.
  • The USA could intervene in any internal issues in Cuba when the USA thought it was necessary.
  • Cuba was prohibited from negotiating treaties with any other nation without USA approval.
  • Cuba’s economy and foreign policy were under the complete control of the United States.
  • Cuba agreed to sell or lease to the United States "lands necessary for coaling or naval stations at certain specified points to be agreed upon".
  • The United States was allowed to keep a naval base in Guantánamo Bay. (The US keeps a military base there today; it is where the US detains some of its terrorist prisoners.)

Further instances of American interventionism led to the US occupation of Haiti from 1915 to 1934 and also led Commodore Perry to Japan.

Good Neighbour Policy

By the 1930s, American public support for military activities in the name of “economic interests” was lacking. This led to the Good Neighbour Policy developed during President Roosevelt’s administration.  The United States worked quietly at subverting Cuba’s government and economic systems without an overt military presence.

This policy shift occurred because of the rising tensions in Europe and the Depression of the 1930s. The USA did not want an angry and vengeful Latin America on its doorstep.

Swiss Neutrality

Big Idea:

In previous activities you saw how nation states join alliances, attempt to remain isolated or intervene in the affairs of other nations in the name of national interests. Neutrality is another foreign policy option nation states choose to defend their national interests.

Switzerland and Neutrality during WWII

When WWII began, Switzerland was in a position no country wanted to be in.  Switzerland was surrounded on all sides by Germany and its allies or satellites. To prevent Germany from occupying Switzerland as it had Austria and most of France, Switzerland maintained the policy of neutrality it had begun in 1815.

 

 

What is neutrality and how has it worked in wartime?

Neutrality means that Switzerland decided to refuse to “take sides”; it decided to “stay out” of the conflict that raged on all of its borders.  This was done for both political and economic reasons.

Switzerland is a tiny country with over 70 per cent of its population of German heritage.  Hitler’s desire to unite all Germans into one “super” Germany was at the forefront of concerns for what the Nazis had in mind for the Swiss.  Luckily for the Swiss, however, their country and its neutrality were more advantageous to Germany than being forced to join the German Reich.

Switzerland is famous for, and relies almost completely on, foreign exports of its cheeses, watches, chocolate, crystal, and banking.  Hitler chose not to invade Switzerland because a neutral Switzerland gave Germany

  • a means of exchanging German gold for Swiss francs that allowed them to purchase war materials from other “neutral” countries such as Turkey, Sweden, Spain, and Portugal
  • a railway system through otherwise impassable mountain ranges to Italy and Africa
  • a source of aluminum for aircraft
  • spark plugs and timing devices for bombs made in factories that were not bombed every night

These advantages, however, were likely only a temporary consideration for Hitler:

"We'll take Switzerland, the small porcupine, on our way back home!" 

Adolf Hitler, 1939, Final War Papers

Did Switzerland have any other choice?  Probably not. Totally surrounded by the Axis, most of its coal supply came from Germany every week, and all of its exports had to go through Axis-controlled territory. As a landlocked country with no natural resources, the Swiss had to work out some form of accommodation with their neighbours.

Source: http://switzerland.isyours.com/e/swiss-business-guide/wwii.html

 

Switzerland’s Answer to Nazis

The Swiss believed very strongly in “spiritual national defence”.  This meant that only the strong political will of the Swiss population could secure Switzerland’s independence from Germany.  Switzerland was and is a democratic nation.  The notion of “spiritual national defence” united former opponents within Switzerland against a common enemy—Nazi Germany.

Throughout the war, the Swiss press and the BBC were the only major voices of opposition to Nazi policies and practices.  Both Swiss and British broadcasts filled the German airwaves with news of what the Nazis were “really” doing to Jews and other ‘undesirable’ persons in its population.

"Of all the neutrals, Switzerland has the greatest right to distinction. She has been the sole international force linking the hideously-sundered nations and ourselves. What does it matter whether she has been able to give us the commercial advantages we desire or has given too many to the Germans, to keep herself alive? She has been a democratic State, standing for freedom in self-defence among her mountains, and in thought, in spite of race, largely on our side."

Winston Churchill (1874 - 1965), British wartime Prime Minister

Source: http://history-switzerland.geschichte-schweiz.ch/switzerland-neutrality-world-war-ii.html

Switzerland has maintained a strict policy of neutrality, refusing to join the European Union or NATO. However, after a close referendum, Switzerland joined the United Nations in 2002 and signalled a willingness to deploy Swiss troops on UN peacekeeping missions.

Canadian freedoms?

When you think about Canada and your rights as a citizen of this country, you are probably pretty secure that in the “True North strong and free” no one or nothing will interfere with your rights to own property, to an attorney when arrested, a phone call for assistance, the right to know what you are being arrested for and the right to a legal hearing of your case.

Most Canadians believe this, but it is not always the case. At any point in time, the Prime Minister of Canada can invoke The War Measures Act. This act creates martial law, and military rules govern all citizens. Under martial law, Canadians’ individual rights are waived in the interests of national security. In other instances, the federal government can create legislation that allows for the arrest and detainment of individuals who are seen as a threat to national security.

If that sounds like science fiction, it is not.

Police banging on doors at all hours of the day or night, ordering frightened occupants to gather up only what they could carry. Parents and children innocent of any crime ushered from their homes, herded in a central depot, and freighted out by train to remote camps.  A scene from Nazi Germany?  No, it was the internment of the Japanese in British Columbia, 1942.

Source: "Banished and Beyond Tears" http://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.com/index.cfm?PgNm=ArchivedFeatures&Params=A241

"On December 7, 1941, an event took place that had nothing to do with me or my family and yet which had devastating consequences for all of us - Japan bombed Pearl Harbour in a surprise attack. With that event began one of the shoddiest chapters in the tortuous history of democracy in North America."

Dr. David Suzuki, Metamorphosis: Stages in a Life.

A very sad chapter in Canadian history is how Japanese-Canadians were treated during WWII.  The attack on Pearl Harbour created hysteria among Canadians, particularly those living in British Columbia where the majority of Japanese-Canadians lived and worked.  The outcry for the federal government to ‘do something’ about the ‘aliens’ living among them prompted Prime Minister King to impose the programme of arrest and internment of all Japanese males ages 14 to 65.  Soon, the “perceived” threat was expanded to include all persons of Japanese ancestry living in Canada.

In 1942, shortly after the news of the Pearl Harbor attack, a series of actions occurred against all Japanese persons in Canada. Many of the Japanese people arrested were Canadian citizens and had been for more than two generations.

  • February 24, Prime Minister King issued a series of orders-in-council to evacuate all persons of Japanese origin to “protective areas”.
  • All property that could not be carried would be taken “into custody”.
  • Ten days later, the British Columbia Security Commission removed the first 2500 Japanese to Hastings Park (the exhibition grounds in Vancouver—horse barns).
  • Special trains carried the Japanese to camps in remote locations in British Columbia
  • The camps were crowded and had no electricity or running water.
  • On January 19, 1943, an order-in-council liquidated all the Japanese property that had been under "protective custody".
  • They were sent to perform unpaid labour building the Trans-Canada Highway, or to work the sugar beet farms in southern Alberta
  • Others were ‘repatriated’ (forced to return to Japan).

More than 20 000 Japanese-Canadians were affected by the internment policies of the Canadian federal government.

Why?  That is for you to determine.  Historically, politicians would say that the Japanese-Canadians were a “perceived” threat to national security.  The fact that not one person of Japanese descent was ever accused of espionage during WWII did not seem to matter. Many historians believe that the real problem was racism and the attack on Pearl Harbour merely a way of getting rid of an unwanted minority group.

WWII Summary

Hitler's invasion of Poland in September 1939 ended the British and French policy of appeasement. War was declared against Nazi Germany (plus Italy and Japan later) by Britain, France, Australia and New Zealand (at first), joined a few days later by Canada and other countries. Russia joined the Allies in June 1941 when Hitler invaded Russia, and the United States was forced to end its policy of isolationism and join the Allies in December 1941, after Japan bombed Pearl Harbour.

War of Movement

Advances in technology, such as tanks and aircraft, and blitzkrieg tactics made WWII a war of movement. France was quickly defeated with a German invasion plan similar to the Schlieffen Plan that failed in WWI. Watch the video animation of the German invasion of Belgium and France:

Hitler had made a pact with the Soviet Union (Russia) in order to avoid the dreaded two-front war. Once France was defeated, however, Hitler attacked the Soviet Union anyway. Initially, the Soviet Union took a beating and German forces were soon on the outskirts of Moscow. By 1942-43, the Soviet Union began to push German forces back, with the help of the harsh Russian winter (recall Napoleon in 1812), supplies from the USA and other Allied countries, and the determination of Soviet soldiers. Hard pressed by the Soviet armed forces, Nazi Germany's fate was sealed when American, British, and Canadian forces landed in Normandy, France, in 1944, opening a second front, and after bitter fighting, liberated France, Belgium and the Netherlands. Hitler finally committed suicide as Soviet forces entered the German capitol of Berlin. Hitler's deputies surrendered to the Allies in May 1945.

The death toll in the Second World War was the highest of any war in all of human history. As in WWI, soldiers died in the millions. In World War Two, civilians were targetted on an unprecedented scale. Civilians were considered legitimate targets for bombing raids because they aided the war effort. Millions more civilians were targeted by the Nazis in acts of genocide.

Nazi Germany was defeated in 1945 bringing World War II to an end in Europe (Japan surrendered in August 1945 after the USA dropped two atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki). While the Allies celebrated victory, a war crimes trial was about to begin. The ghastly crime of genocide had been committed. How to bring to justice those responsible for the Holocaust?

When Ultranationalism Leads to Genocide: The Holocaust

All that is necessary for the triumph of evil is that good men do nothing.

Edmund Burke

The Holocaust is one of the most prominent examples of ultranationalism leading to genocide. At the end of this lesson you will research other examples. Please note: the Holocaust and genocide in general are horrific episodes in human history that continue to appear even today. This topic can be very emotionally upsetting, especially for people with relatives who lived through these and similar horrors, or had personal experiences.

 

Anti-Semitism

Within the ultranationalist nation state of Germany, the Nazi Party and Adolf Hitler promoted anti-Semitism. The Nazis and Hitler had said many times that a conspiracy of Jews and Communists had kept Germany in misery. Hitler promised that he would deal with this conspiracy, but many of those who supported him initially, other than his closest henchmen, never conceived of the horrors his ‘final solution’ would bring.

The process by which Hitler chose to act out his hatred against the Jewish people was gradual. Initially, most Germans believed he was a raging fool. However, as the Great Depression deepened and people became desperate, more Germans were willing to listen to Hitler’s lies about the Jews.  Hitler used the Jews as scapegoats, and the people of Germany followed him.

Shortly after becoming Chancellor in 1933, Hitler was able to begin his attack against the Jews. As you already learned, all Jewish men and women had to resign from their jobs.  The SA and later the SS prevented customers from entering Jewish businesses, and the Nazis organized boycotts against Jewish-owned stores. Jews were also forced to ride in the back of buses and bullying of Jewish children at schools went unpunished. Stores owned by Jews were made to mark their windows with a yellow Star of David.

In 1938, the Nazi government passed the Nuremberg Laws. These anti-semitic laws stated the following:

  • Only a person of Aryan blood could be a citizen.
  • Jews could not vote, hold public office, or be a citizen.
  • Marriage between Germans and Jews was forbidden; if they did, they risked public ridicule or death.
  • Sexual relations between Jews and Germans was forbidden.
  • Jews were not allowed to display the national flag or any other colours or symbols claiming German citizenship.

Kristallnacht

Later that year, Kristallnacht or the Night of the Broken Glass occurred. In retaliation for the reported murder of a Nazi diplomat by a Jewish person in Paris, Himmler organized his SS troops to conduct a week-long campaign of ‘terror’ against all Jews.

Results of Kristallnacht:

  • 10 000 Jewish-owned businesses were destroyed.
  • All contents of the destroyed businesses were seized.
  • Homes and synagogues were set on fire and the fire brigades showed their loyalty by not putting out the fires.
  • The Jewish community was ordered to pay one million marks to pay for the clean-up.                          
  • Jews were forced to clean the streets after the campaign was over.

Historians are still divided over whether the Germans supported these Nazi actions or whether fear made them turn a blind eye. In the immediate aftermath of Kristallnacht, an anonymous German wrote to the British Consul in Cologne stating, "The German people have nothing whatsoever to do with these riots and burnings." Christopher Isherwood, a British writer living in Germany, witnessed the arrest of a Jew in a cafe by the SA where everybody simply looked away. To create a scene would have provoked a violent response from those doing the arresting. The fear of the concentration camps was such that most felt compelled to remain silent although they did not approve of what was going on.

Why didn't the Jews leave Germany?

Before Kristallnacht, there were many reasons Jews remained in Germany:

  1. When Hitler and the Nazis first started promoting their anti-semitism, few took them seriously. Many Jews and non-Jews alike thought that Hitler would never actually succeed in ruling Germany, much less carry out his genocidal plans.
  2. As time went on, some Jews did leave, but it was difficult to just pack up and travel to another country. It was expensive. Contrary to Nazi dogma that Jews were hoarding wealth stolen from good "true" Germans, most Jews were middleclass and others lived in poverty.
  3. Jews had lived in Germany for centuries. They had ancestral roots in the country. To just leave was not easy. They desired to stay in what they believed was their homeland. They saw themselves as German and Jewish.
  4. Anti-semitism was not new to German Jews. Jews had been persecuted throughout Europe for centuries. What was happening in Germany seemed to be just another period of outward discrimination that would pass as it had before. No one could have forseen the Holocaust that was to come.
  5. Those Jews that did try to emigrate before Kristallnacht faced obstacles not only with exit restrictions from the Nazis but also immigration quotas from other countries. Sadly, anti-semitism existed outside Europe as well. Many countries would not accept Jewish immigrants or they had quotas restricting the number of Jews allowed in, including Canada.

After Kristallnacht, many German Jews began to panic, and a flood of requests for entry to other countries followed. Many of these requests were denied because of quota restrictions and some countries that had been accepting Jewish immigrants even closed their doors completely. Eventually, not just German Jews were caught up in the Holocaust, but Jews living in European territories conquered by the Nazis met the same fate. Tragically, many Jews that had escaped Germany to settle in neighbouring countries were caught when their new homes were overun by the Nazis during the war.

Fascist regimes can function only if the population is under control and fearful, and it believes in the rosy future the regime promises to build. Hitler repeatedly told the German people that, because they were Aryan, they should rule. As you learned, the word untermensch was used to describe all people who were not Aryan. The word means socially and racially inferior and was used as an excuse to murder. In Nazi Germany, the people considered to be untermensch were rounded up like cattle and destroyed.

For example:

  • Black people were sterilized or killed.
  • Jews were persecuted systematically, put into concentration camps, used for medical experiments and slave labour, and eventually became the subject of genocide in the ‘Final Solution’.
  • 85% of Germany's gypsies were killed.
  • 5000 mentally disabled babies were killed from 1939 to 1945.   
  • 72 000 mentally ill patients were killed from 1939 to 1941.
  • 300 000 men and women considered physically disabled and members of families with hereditary illnesses were sterilized from 1934 to 1945.
  • Beggars, homosexuals, prostitutes, alcoholics, pacifists, hooligans, and criminals were also regarded as anti-social, and they were put in concentration camps where many died.

Why didn’t anyone stop Hitler?

Good question and one for which there is no real answer. Some possible explanations include the following:

  • For a time nobody took Hitler seriously. His raving speeches were considered the words of a mad man. After Hitler was in power, he used a powerful core of military troops, the SS, to carry out his commands. Anyone who protested would likely suffer the same fate as the untermensch.
  • The Final Solution was not acted on overnight. The campaign of genocide against the Jews was carried out gradually over several years.

Sadly, anti-Semitism was a common prejudice in many European nations. For centuries the Jews had been subjected to persecution because they were different from the majority of people in the country. Xenophobia or fear of foreigners was rampant. Scapegoats allowed individuals to blame someone else for their misfortunes.

Ultranationalism led to the genocide of Jews in Nazi Germany. It happened because the citizens allowed it to happen. The German people had been humiliated by the Versailles Treaty. Hitler made them feel proud again. Love for Germany and excessive pride in being German led to the Holocaust. There is little doubt that German citizens knew of Hitler's intentions. He made his intentions quite clear:

In the course of my life I have very often been a prophet, and have usually been ridiculed for it. During the time of my struggle for power, it was in the first instance only the Jewish race that received my prophecies with laughter when I said that I would one day take over the leadership of the state and with it that of the whole nation and that I would then among other things settle the Jewish problem...but I think that for some time now they have been laughing on the other side of their face.

Today, I will once more be a prophet: if the international Jewish financiers in and outside Europe should succeed in plunging the nations once more into a world war, then the result will not be the Bolshevising* of the earth and thus the victory of Jewry, but the annihilation of the Jewish race in Europe!

Adolf Hitler, Speech to the Reichstag - 30th January 1939

* The Bolsheviks were the branch of communists in power in Russia. Hitler believed communism was a Jewish conspiracy - even though Russian communists had strong anti-semitic tendencies.

The Final Solution

The Final Solution refers to the Nazi program to exterminate completely all Jews in Europe, which began in earnest in 1941. This is the Holocaust. The map shows the location of the concentration camps throughout Europe. People were sent to these camps for various reasons, but the main reason was because of their race. The “Final Solution” was carried out primarily in the sites marked in red. Why do you think the death camps were built in Nazi occuppied Poland and not in Germany itself?

In January, 1942, the Nazis began the systematic deportation of Jews from all over Europe to six extermination camps established in former Polish territory: Chelmno, Belzec, Sobibor, Treblinka, Auschwitz-Birkenau, and Majdanek. These camps were killing centres. People were taken to the camps to be killed. The elderly, mothers with children, all children under fifteen, and the ill or disabled were killed immediately after they arrived. The method used was poison gas pumped into large rooms that looked like showers. After death, the bodies were disposed of in crematoriums (burned into ash). Healthy adults were worked to death in factories in or near the death camps.

One town where the Jewish population was deported to concentration camps was Sighet, the birthplace of Elie Wiesel, a concentration camp survivor and the author of Night, a novel for which he received the Nobel Peace Prize.

Prosecuting the Criminals

The combined efforts of the Allies defeated Nazi Germany in 1945 and put an end to World War II in Europe, thus stopping the Holocaust (although this was not the Allies' reason for going to war). For six million Jews, the Allied victory came too late. Even so, after the war, the international community attempted to seek justice and put the world on notice that such acts would not go unpunished by the international community.

The Nuremburg Trials, so named for where they were held in Nuremburg, Germany, were set up by the Allied nations after the war to try those Nazis who had been charged with "crimes against humanity". While Hitler had escaped justice by committing suicide in the final days of the war, there were still many Nazis left to prosecute.

The United Nations Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide is the international law established after World War II to address the problem of Genocide. The Convention defined genocide:

“In the present Convention, genocide means any of the following acts committed with intent to
destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such:

(a) Killing members of the group;
(b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group;
(c) Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part;
(d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group;
(e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.”

Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights, “Convention on the Prevention and
Punishment of the Crime of Genocide,” 12 January 1951. (Retrieved 13 July 2012 from http://www2.ohchr.org/english/law/genocide.htm )

Genocide, and other Crimes against Humanity are criminal acts committed by individuals, not nations. For example, all Germans or even all Nazis were not guilty of genocide. Rather, international law goes after the individuals who orchestrated and directly participated in crimes. Typically, the UN International Criminal Court or special tribunals (like Nuremburg above or the International Criminal Tribunal for the Former Yugoslavia; or Rwanda) have been established to prosecute accused criminals. Some countries, like Canada, have amended domestic law to allow for the prosectution of Crimes Against Humanity in Canadian courts, even if the crime was perpertrated on foreign soil.

Ethnic Cleansing

In your upcoming research assignment on genocide, it may be helpful to also use the search term "ethnic cleansing." "Ethnic cleansing" is a term that became popular in the media and official circles to describe the forced expulsion and/or extermination of ethnic groups in the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s. The UN, however, continues to describe events in Bosnia as genocide.

"[E]thnic cleansing is a well-defined policy of a particular group of persons to systematically eliminate another group from a given territory, often based on economic principles, or nationalist claims to the land. Such a policy often involves violence and is very often connected with military operations. ... Ethnic Cleansing is to be achieved by all possible means, from discrimination to extermination, and entails violations of human rights and international humanitarian law."

Drazen Petrovic, "Ethnic Cleansing - An Attempt at Methodology",
European Journal of International Law, Vol. No. 3, page 1.

The United Nations Charter and Self-Determination of Peoples

After World War Two, the question of human rights became more prominent in international relations. This was not surprising given how shocked the world was to learn of the horrors of the holocaust and other crimes against humanity during the war. Among the human rights that the United Nations (UN) wanted to protect and encourage was the right to self-determination of peoples. This was one of many guidelines enshrined in the original United Nations Charter to promote the wider goal of international peace and security.

Members of the United Nations which have or assume responsibilities for the administration of territories whose peoples have not yet attained a full measure of self-government recognize the principle that the interests of the inhabitants of these territories are paramount, and accept as a sacred trust the obligation to promote to the utmost, within the system of international peace and security established by the present Charter, the well-being of the inhabitants of these territories, and, to this end:

a. to ensure, with due respect for the culture of the peoples concerned, their political, economic, social, and educational advancement, their just treatment, and their protection against abuses;

b. to develop self-government, to take due account of the political aspirations of the peoples, and to assist them in the progressive development of their free political institutions, according to the particular circumstances of each territory and its peoples and their varying stages of advancement;

c. to further international peace and security; ...

UN Charter, Chapter XI, Article 73 (1945)

What this meant was decolonization; the process of allowing colonies to become self-governing and independent of the Imperial Powers. It also meant nations within nations now had the backing of international law to seek self-determination.

Decolonization

As you learned previously, European imperialists in the late nineteenth century determined the borders of African countries. They were drawn with little regard for natural or traditional boundaries between African tribes and emerging nations. The imposed boundaries were drawn to satisfy the interests of the European powers and not the colonies they controlled. This simple fact has determined much of the subsequent history of Africa. The borders created multi-national states in a continent of many peoples, languages, and cultures. The small Belgian colony of Rwanda is an example of the consequence of these policies. The territory from which Rwanda was created was home to two traditional enemies: the Hutus and the Tutsis. The Rwandan genocide of the Tutsis in 1994 can in part be blamed on the European imperialists who forced these bitter enemies to live in the same state. Imperialist powers considered their own national interests, not the national interest of the colonies.

Post-War Decolonization

As a result of the policies of the Imperial powers toward their colonies, it is not surprising that the colonies sought independence. After World War Two, the United Nations agreed to promote decolonization. That is, encouraging colonies to seek self-determination. This process continues even today.

The Fall of the Soviet Union and the Rise of Successor States

Once Mikhail Gorbachev came to power in 1985, the communist Soviet Union began to undergo dramatic changes from its totalitarian past. Gorbachev encouraged democracy, (limited) free enterprise and increased media freedom in the Soviet Union. He withdrew from the Soviet war in Afghanistan (1979-89) and attempted to negotiate Soviet-USA disarmament agreements in an effort to lessen tensions between the two superpowers. The USA and the Soviet Union had been in an arms race for the previous 40 years. (Why did he consider this dangerous? Recall what you learned about militarism prior to WWI).

Despite his efforts, Gorbachev's reforms in the Soviet Union seemed to be too slow for many of the liberals and nationalists, and too fast for the conservatives. To stop his policies, communist hardliners attempted a coup d'etat and put Gorbachev under "house arrest" in August 1991.

While tanks rolled into Moscow, his liberal political rival, Boris Yeltsin, made news by climbing atop a tank and defying the hardliners. To protest the attempted coup, and following Yeltsin's lead, thousands of Russians rallied to the scene and faced down government troops. The coup collapsed, and Gorbachev returned but he was soon overshadowed by Yeltsin.

In December 1991, Boris Yeltsin met with the regional leaders of Ukraine and Belarus to establish the Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS), a loose economic and military union but with full independence granted to the republics. Gorbachev resigned and the Soviet Union was dissolved on January 1, 1992, creating the Russian Federation and fourteen new nations where only the Soviet Union (USSR) existed before. The long period of tensions (1946 - 1991) between the USA and the Soviet Union was known as the Cold War. The ideological struggle between the USA and the Soviet Union (USSR) suppressed many nationalist movements during that period. With the sudden and miraculous peaceful end of the Cold War, the world looked forward to a time of peace and prosperity. Instead, there was an upsurge in nationalism that has been unrivalled since the world went to war in 1914.

East European Countries Faced Their Own Challenges for Self-Determination.

The Cold War left Europe divided, with Eastern Europe under Soviet influence and Western Europe allied to the USA. Now that Eastern European countries were free from a foreign master (Moscow) they could get on with the issues facing their own nations. In the cases Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia, that meant very different paths to self-determination.

Czechoslovakia's "Velvet Divorce"

On January 1, 1993, Czechoslovakia became two separate states - the Czech Republic and the Republic of Slovakia. Some have referred to this break-up as the "Velvet Divorce" because of the smooth and peaceful transition that ended the Czechoslovakia federation and created the Czech Republic and the Republic of Slovakia.

For the most part, there existed separate regional political units and peoples in Czechoslovakia. Unlike many multinational states, the boundary between the Czech and Slovak population was clearly defined. The two regional political entities negotiated the decision to end the federation. There is also no strong hatred between the two dominant peoples with very similar ethnic and cultural backgrounds, and poltical leaders did not attempt to incite hatred, so a violent break-up was unlikely.

The situation in Yugoslavia was the complete opposite of Czechoslovakia. Political leaders encouraged ultranationalism and this played a big part in the violent break up of Yugoslavia in the 1990s, resulting in wars and acts of genocide (this is where the term "ethnic cleansing" originated). Another major difference between Yugoslavia and Czechoslovakia is that there was no clear regional divisions between ethnic groups, particularly in Bosnia Herzegovina (in the centre of the former Yugoslavia - see map). Furthermore, the Serbian side opposed the break up of Yugoslavia, and, if it had to happen, Serbia wanted all areas where Serbs lived to be under Serbian control. So did Croatia.

The ethnic nationalist dream of the Black Hand before WWI - a single state for all the ethnically South Slavic people, Yugoslavia - was itself torn apart by nationalism, this time based on even more narrow divisions along religious, linguistic and historical differences. Leaders on both sides emphasized these differences to maintain their power and justify military aggression and crimes against humanity.

Self-Determination Summary

Self-Determination Summary Nations obtain and pursue national self-determination and national interests in various ways. National self-determination movements may also...

Nations obtain and pursue national self-determination and national interests in various ways. National self-determination movements may also have different goals, for example:

  • to govern themselves with complete autonomy in domestic and foreign affairs (African colonies since 1945; Canada after WWI)
  • to govern themselves with limited autonomy, for example self-government in local affairs (Inuit in Nunavut)
  • to unify an artificially divided people (Vietnam)
  • to remove, by whatever means, "unwanted" nationalities (Rwanda; Bosnia; Kosovo)
  • to unify all "nationals" even if this means occupying the territory of other multi-nation states (Serbia; Croatia)
  • to regain former independence (Tibet)
  • to protect a national culture from neighbouring dominant cultures (Tibet; Quebec)

Now, in the specific case of ISIS/ISIL, one might canvas the purposes of the group. Their presumptuous claim to a universal caliphate, which they are said to have claimed, should be interpreted as an idealizing expression of their desperation after having been so severely down and out in the war. The overthrow of the Sunni regime in Iraq, which had existed continuously from the Ottoman Empire through the British-sponsored Hashimite monarchy through the various republican regimes including that of Saddam Husayn, has at length elicited from the Sunnis a magisterial response, refusing to brook any limits to their claims to sovereignty, despite their weakness and hopelessness. Because a somewhat similar situation arose in Syria, the ISIS movement was able to get going and easily claimed all the Sunni-majority areas in Iraq. It remains unproven that they can capture any additional territory. Modern warfare favors native defenders, however ill-armed, and the Kurds and Shi`is of Iraq are indeed very well armed, so there is no likelihood that ISIS can overwhelm either. The hapless Christian and Yazidi minorities, however, are another matter.

In any case, the real intention of ISIS in erasing the political boundary between Syria and Iraq seems only to be to unite the Sunni Arab areas of the two countries in order to give the Sunni Arabs a larger polity and more pooled resources in order to face their numerous enemies. Because the Sunni Arabs have been so abused, especially in Iraq, they have adopted harsh, fierce attitudes. No doubt this movement has wide support among the abused Sunni Arabs, who hope that they might at last get some respect through unity on this point.

That said, it is very difficult to understand why the ISIS leadership would seek to alienate all other Sunni Muslims by claiming the caliphate, including potential supporters in Saudi Arabia and other Gulf oil states. When I looked at videos of alleged ISIS speakers, I was not able to verify that they had actually done this. It is also certainly false that they demand female circumcision; they have specifically denied this, and female circumcision has generally never been practiced in Muslim Asia. So I would not trust a lot of what passes for news. But it would not be surprising that they might try to root out all minorities for the sake of a homogenous population that would produce national unity. Unfortunately, this has been a stock-in-trade of nationalists in modern times, which brands ISIS as a nationalist phenomenon, even while it professes to be antithetical to nationalism. In this, it exactly resembles the Taliban, who were also crypto-nationalists, in their case representing the Pashtun ethnicity, just as the ISIS represent the Sunni Arab one. The litmus test for nationalism is statism. Is the goal of the movement a state or not? For the Taliban as for the ISIS, it certainly is their goal; therefore, they are nationalist statists, or nation-statists. In a way, they represent a movement toward the regularization of political Islam in a world dominated by nation states.

Nationalist movements promote and protect national language, culture and identity through political expression. They aim to control their affairs without outside interference. They are about managing their economic resources by themselves. They may want autonomy within a multinational state in order to structure it to protect their identity, or in certain cases for an independent state of their own.

Taliban meet none of these criteria in Afghanistan or Pakistan, and therefore cannot be considered a Pashtun nationalist movement. They take ideological and political inspiration from Arabs and other non-Pashtuns. They have consciously, as a matter of policy, targeted different cultural traits of Pashtuns, like tribal councils and folk music; they are not concerned about the language and promote mostly Arabic and/or interestingly, Urdu; Economic resources or their control is not their concern; neither is any political or administrative manifestation of Pashtun identity their goal.

They have killed a large number of traditional Pashtun elders in FATA and banned the Jirga as means of dispute settlement in areas under their influence. They have been eliminating the Pashtun way of life.
